Two monitors make sense if you regularly work with two things side by side, like a reference document and the file you are editing, a call and your notes, or code and a preview. One good monitor is better if most of your day happens in a single window, if your desk is shallow or narrow, or if you tend to get neck strain from turning your head. Many remote workers end up best served by a middle option: one main screen plus a laptop screen as the secondary. Here is how to decide, and how to set up whichever you pick so it is comfortable for a full day.

## The short answer by work type

| Your typical work | Better fit | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Writing, email, general office tasks | Single monitor | Most work happens in one window at a time |
| Customer support, chat plus ticketing | Dual | Two tools open at once all day |
| Programming | Dual, or one large wide screen | Code, docs, and output side by side |
| Data work, spreadsheets | One large or ultrawide | Wide sheets fit without scrolling |
| Design, photo, video editing | One high-quality main screen, optional second for tools | Color and detail on the main screen matter most |
| Frequent video calls | Dual or laptop plus monitor | Call on one screen, notes or slides on the other |
| Small desk, shared space | Single monitor | Less clutter, easier to position well |

## Benefits of a single monitor

**Easier to position correctly.** One screen goes straight in front of you, at the right height and distance. There is no compromise about which screen gets the center spot.

**Less neck rotation.** With everything in front of you, you are not turning your head repeatedly throughout the day.

**Fewer distractions.** A second screen often becomes a place for chat, email, and notifications that pull attention away from focused work.

**Less desk space and fewer cables.** Helpful in small rooms, on shallow desks, or if your office doubles as a guest room.

**Lower cost.** You buy one monitor and, if you need it, one arm or stand.

The main downside is window juggling. If you constantly switch between two apps, you spend time and attention flipping back and forth. Keyboard shortcuts for snapping windows side by side help, especially on a larger screen.

## Benefits of two monitors

**Side-by-side work without switching.** Reference material on one screen, active work on the other. For many tasks this is simply faster and less frustrating.

**Better video calls.** Keep the call on one screen and your notes, agenda, or shared documents on the other.

**Flexible layout.** Some people rotate the second screen to portrait orientation for reading long documents, code, or chat threads.

The downsides are real, though:

- Two screens take up more width, and a lot of desks are not wide enough to place both comfortably.
- If both screens are placed symmetrically, with the gap in the middle, you look slightly to one side all day.
- Mismatched screens (different sizes, heights, or brightness) are tiring to look back and forth between.
- More cables, more power, and more ports needed on your laptop or dock.

## What about one large or ultrawide screen?

A single wide or ultrawide monitor sits between the two options. You get room for two windows side by side without a bezel in the middle, and it is easier to keep everything at one height and distance.

Things to consider:

- Very wide screens still require some head turning to see the edges. A curved panel can reduce that by keeping the edges closer to the same distance from your eyes.
- They need a deep desk. A large screen placed too close means you move your head a lot to scan it.
- They are harder to use for screen sharing on calls, since sharing the whole screen can make text tiny for others. Sharing a single window avoids that.

For people who like the idea of two screens but have a shallow desk or neck concerns, one moderately large screen is often the best compromise.

## The laptop plus monitor setup

Many remote workers already have a laptop. Using it as the second screen is a cheap way to get a dual setup. To make it comfortable:

- Put the external monitor directly in front of you. This is your main screen.
- Put the laptop to one side, raised so its screen is closer to the height of the monitor. A stack of books works; a [[laptop-stands|laptop stand]] makes it tidier.
- Use a separate keyboard and mouse so you are not reaching for the laptop's keyboard.
- Keep the laptop for secondary things: chat, calendar, email, the video call.

The laptop screen will usually be smaller and lower than the monitor, so avoid putting your most-used window there.

## How to set up a single monitor

1. Center it directly in front of you, aligned with your keyboard.
2. Set the top of the screen at or slightly below eye level.
3. Place it about an arm's length away. Larger screens can sit a little farther back.
4. Tilt it back slightly so you look at it straight on.
5. Reduce glare by placing it perpendicular to windows where you can.

## How to set up two monitors

The layout depends on how evenly you use them.

### If you use one screen much more than the other

This is the most common situation. Put the **main screen directly in front of you**, centered on your keyboard. Put the **secondary screen to the side**, angled toward you so it forms a gentle curve. Most of the day, you look straight ahead.

### If you use both screens about equally

Place them side by side with the **meeting point of the two screens directly in front of your nose**. Angle both toward you in a shallow V so each is roughly the same distance from your eyes. You will turn your head slightly each way, but evenly.

### Rules for either layout

- Set both screens to the same height, with their tops at or slightly below eye level.
- Keep them at similar distances from your eyes so your focus does not have to shift.
- Match brightness and, if possible, text scaling so switching between them is not jarring.
- If you wear bifocals or progressive lenses, place screens a little lower so you are not tipping your head back to see through the bottom of your lenses.
- Turn your chair or body slightly for long stretches on the side screen, rather than holding your neck twisted.

Positioning two screens is where [[monitor-arms|dual monitor arms]] earn their price. They let you set height, angle, and distance for each screen independently and free up desk space underneath. The free alternative is books or sturdy boxes to match heights.

## Check your desk before you buy

Two screens of moderate size side by side can easily take up most of the width of a smaller desk. Before buying a second monitor or a wide one:

- Measure the usable width of your desk and the width of the screens you are considering, including stands.
- Measure the depth. You need enough room for the screen to sit about an arm's length from your eyes with a keyboard in front of it.
- Check your laptop's ports and whether it can drive two external screens. Some laptops support only one external display without a dock or adapter.

## Common mistakes

**Centering the gap.** Putting two screens symmetrically when you mainly use one leads to hours of looking slightly to the side.

**Mismatched heights.** Pairing a monitor with a low, flat laptop screen means constantly bobbing your head up and down.

**Too big, too close.** A large screen on a shallow desk forces a lot of head and eye movement.

**Adding a second screen for notifications only.** If the second screen mainly shows chat and email, it may cost more focus than it saves.

## Bottom line

Choose a single monitor if your work lives in one window at a time, your desk is small, or neck comfort is your top priority. Choose two screens if you routinely need two things visible at once, and set them up so your most-used screen is straight ahead. If you are unsure, start with your laptop as the second screen. After a couple of weeks you will know whether a real second monitor, a larger single screen, or your current setup fits your work best.

If you have ongoing neck or shoulder pain that does not improve after adjusting your setup, have it looked at by a doctor or physiotherapist.
