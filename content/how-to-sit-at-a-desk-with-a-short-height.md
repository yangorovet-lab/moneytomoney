Most desks and chairs are sized with an average-height adult in mind, so shorter people usually end up with feet that don't reach the floor, a seat that's too deep and a monitor that's too high. The way to sit well is to work from the top down: set your chair so your elbows are level with the desk, then support your feet, fill the gap behind your lower back, and lower the screen until its top edge is at or just below eye level.

## The three problems shorter people run into

It helps to know what you're fixing, because each problem needs a different solution.

1. **Desk height.** A standard desk is around 29 to 30 inches tall. For many shorter people, their ideal typing height is a few inches lower. Raising the chair fixes the arms but leaves the feet hanging.
2. **Seat depth.** Seats are often too long for shorter thighs. To bend your knees, you slide forward, and your back leaves the backrest.
3. **Screen height.** Monitor stands often don't go low enough, so you look up at the screen and tip your head back.

Everything below is about solving those three things, with free options first.

## Step 1: Set the chair for your arms

Your arms decide the chair height, not your legs.

- Sit back in the chair.
- Relax your shoulders and bend your elbows to roughly 90 degrees.
- Raise the seat until your forearms are level with the desk surface or slightly above it.

Your shoulders should stay loose. If you feel them lift as you reach for the keyboard, the chair is still too low.

At this point your feet will probably leave the floor. That's expected. Don't lower the chair to fix it.

## Step 2: Bring the floor up to your feet

Measure the gap between your heels and the floor while you sit in your working position. Then fill it with something stable:

- Hardcover books taped together.
- A sturdy box packed with paper.
- A yoga block on its side.
- A dedicated footrest once you know the height you need.

Your knees should end up roughly level with your hips or slightly below, with your feet flat. A footrest with a slight tilt is often more comfortable over a long day. If you find a pile of books fiddly, an [[footrests|adjustable footrest]] is one of the most useful purchases a shorter person can make.

## Step 3: Fix the seat depth

Sit all the way back and check the space between the front of the seat and the back of your knees. You want a gap of roughly two to four fingers.

If the seat edge presses into the back of your knees:

- **Use the seat depth adjustment** if your chair has one. Slide the seat pan back until you get the gap.
- **Add a back cushion.** A firm cushion or a rolled towel behind your lower back pushes you forward in the seat, so your knees clear the edge while your back stays supported.
- **Try a chair with a shorter seat.** When buying a chair, look specifically for a short seat depth or a sliding seat, plus a low minimum seat height.

Sitting on the front third of a seat with no back support is the most common workaround shorter people use, and it's tiring by mid-afternoon.

## Step 4: Support your lower back

Your lower back has a natural inward curve. Chair lumbar supports are often positioned for a longer torso, so they may press into your mid-back instead of the small of your back.

- If the lumbar support adjusts up and down, lower it until it sits in the curve just above your belt line.
- If it doesn't adjust, a small rolled towel or a lumbar pillow placed at the right height works well.
- You should feel gentle support, not a push forward.

## Step 5: Bring the keyboard and mouse close

Shorter arms mean a shorter comfortable reach.

- Pull the keyboard toward the front of the desk so your elbows stay by your sides.
- Keep the mouse right next to the keyboard. A compact keyboard without a number pad lets the mouse sit closer to your body.
- Fold away the keyboard's rear feet so your wrists stay straight.
- If you still feel your shoulders reaching, consider a keyboard tray that sits below and slightly in front of the desk surface.

## Step 6: Lower the monitor

The top of the screen should be at or slightly below eye level, about an arm's length away. Shorter people often sit lower, so a monitor that's fine for a taller colleague will be too high.

- Take the monitor off any riser or books.
- Lower the stand to its minimum.
- Tilt the screen back slightly so you're not looking at the top edge.
- If it's still too high, a [[monitor-arms|monitor arm with a low mounting range]] lets you bring it down close to the desk surface.
- Bifocal and progressive lens wearers often do better with the monitor lower still, so they don't tilt their head back to read through the bottom of their lenses.

## Summary of settings

| Setting | Target for a shorter person | Common workaround to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Chair height | Forearms level with desk, shoulders relaxed | Lowering the chair so feet reach the floor |
| Feet | Flat on a footrest or books, knees near hip height | Tucking feet under the chair or hooking them on the base |
| Seat depth | Two to four fingers between seat and knees | Perching on the front edge |
| Lumbar support | In the curve of the lower back | Sitting away from the backrest |
| Keyboard and mouse | Close, elbows by your sides | Reaching forward with straight arms |
| Monitor | Top at or just below eye level, arm's length away | Looking up and tipping the head back |

## If you're buying new furniture

You don't need special furniture, but a few features make life easier. Check these specs rather than trusting a size label.

### Chair

- **Minimum seat height.** It needs to be low enough to use without the desk, for example at a kitchen table.
- **Seat depth adjustment or a short seat.** This matters more than almost anything else.
- **Height-adjustable lumbar support.**
- **Armrests that go low or fold away.** High armrests push shoulders up.

### Desk

- **An adjustable desk's lowest height.** Many adjustable desks don't go much lower than a standard desk. Look for one whose minimum height matches your ideal sitting height, so you can sit without a footrest at all.
- **No front apron or drawer** that limits how low you can bring a keyboard tray.

### Standing

If you also stand to work, the same rules apply: elbows around 90 degrees, screen at eye level. Check that the desk or converter goes low enough for your standing elbow height too.

## Common mistakes

- **Letting the chair height follow your legs.** Your arms should set the chair height; your feet get a footrest.
- **Ignoring seat depth.** It's the reason many shorter people can't use a backrest.
- **Keeping the monitor where it came out of the box.** Default stand heights often suit a taller person.
- **Using high, fixed armrests.** If they lift your shoulders, lower them or take them off.
- **Sitting in one position all day.** Change position, stand up and walk around regularly.

If you have ongoing back, neck or hip pain, or numbness or tingling in your legs or hands, get it looked at by a doctor or physiotherapist rather than relying on setup changes alone.

## Quick checklist

- Elbows level with the desk, shoulders down.
- Feet flat on a footrest or books.
- A small gap behind the knees.
- Lower back supported in its curve.
- Keyboard and mouse close to your body.
- Monitor lowered so the top is at or below eye level.

Get those six things right and a standard desk becomes comfortable for a shorter body, often with nothing more than a footrest and a rolled towel.
