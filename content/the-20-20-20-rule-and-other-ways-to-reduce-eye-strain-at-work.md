The 20-20-20 rule is simple: every 20 minutes, look at something about 20 feet (roughly 6 meters) away for at least 20 seconds. It gives the eye muscles that focus on close objects a short break and reminds you to blink. On its own, it helps many people, but eye strain usually has several causes at once. Pairing the rule with a well-placed screen, good lighting, larger text, and regular blinking tends to make a much bigger difference than any single habit.

## What causes eye strain at work

Screen-related eye strain, often called digital eye strain or computer vision syndrome, typically shows up as:

- Tired, sore, or burning eyes
- Dry or watery eyes
- Blurred vision, especially when looking up from the screen
- Headaches, often around the forehead or temples
- Neck and shoulder tension from leaning toward the screen

The usual contributors:

- **Sustained close focus.** Looking at something near for long periods keeps the focusing muscles working without a break.
- **Reduced blinking.** People tend to blink less when concentrating on a screen, and blinks are often incomplete, so the eye surface dries out.
- **Glare and poor lighting.** Reflections on the screen and big differences between screen and room brightness make your eyes work harder.
- **Small text and poor screen position.** Leaning in, squinting, or looking up at a screen all add strain.
- **Uncorrected vision.** An outdated prescription or a need for reading glasses often shows up first at the computer.

## How to make the 20-20-20 rule actually stick

The rule is easy to understand and easy to forget. Some ways to build it in:

- **Use a timer.** A repeating 20-minute reminder on your phone, computer, or a browser extension works. Some people prefer a gentle chime over a pop-up that interrupts.
- **Pick a target in advance.** Identify something far away to look at: a tree outside the window, a building across the street, or the far end of a hallway. If your room is small, look out a window or through a doorway at the farthest point you can see.
- **Attach it to routine actions.** Look away every time you send an email, finish a task, or start a call before the video connects.
- **Combine with blinking.** During those 20 seconds, blink slowly and fully several times.
- **Don't worry about perfection.** Twenty feet is a guideline. The goal is to focus on something much farther away than your screen, for long enough that your eyes relax.

## Set up your screen to reduce strain

Screen position has a big effect on comfort.

| Setting | Recommendation | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Distance | About an arm's length (20 to 30 inches for most monitors) | Reduces focusing effort; larger screens can sit farther away |
| Height | Top of screen at or slightly below eye level | Keeps gaze slightly downward, which exposes less of the eye surface |
| Position | Directly in front of you | Avoids twisting and uneven focus |
| Tilt | Tilted back slightly | Keeps the screen perpendicular to your line of sight |
| Text size | Large enough to read comfortably without leaning in | Less squinting |
| Brightness | Roughly matching the room | Less adjustment between screen and surroundings |
| Contrast | Dark text on light background, or a comfortable dark mode | Clearer text |

### Practical screen tweaks

- **Increase text size or zoom** rather than leaning in. Most operating systems and browsers make this easy.
- **Lower brightness** if the screen looks like a light source in the room. Raise it if you're squinting in a bright room.
- **Clean the screen.** Dust and smudges reduce clarity.
- **Use a warmer screen setting in the evening** if bright blue-white screens feel harsh.

## Fix your lighting

- **Avoid glare.** Place your screen perpendicular to windows, not facing them or with a window directly behind it.
- **Use blinds or curtains** to control bright daylight.
- **Add a task light** for paper documents, placed to the side and angled away from the screen.
- **Keep ambient light on.** A bright screen in a dark room increases the contrast your eyes must deal with.
- **Consider neutral white light** (around 4000K) for daytime desk work.

If your desk lamp reflects off the screen, [[monitor-light-bars|a monitor light bar]] can light the desk surface without creating screen glare, since it shines downward rather than toward the display.

## Help your eyes stay moist

Dry eyes are one of the most common complaints at a screen.

- **Blink on purpose.** Every so often, do a few slow, complete blinks, gently closing your eyes fully.
- **Keep air from blowing on your face.** Point fans, heaters, and air vents away from your eyes.
- **Consider humidity.** Dry indoor air, especially in winter, can make symptoms worse. A humidifier can help some people.
- **Stay hydrated.** It's a small factor, but it doesn't hurt.
- **Lubricating eye drops** can help with dryness. If you use them often, talk to a pharmacist or eye doctor about which type suits you.

## Take proper breaks too

The 20-20-20 rule gives your eyes micro-breaks. Longer breaks help both your eyes and your body:

- **Every hour or so,** get up for a few minutes. Walk, refill water, or look out a window.
- **Take calls away from the screen** when you don't need video.
- **Step outside at lunch.** Natural light and distant views give your eyes a real rest.

## Check your eyes

Eye strain is sometimes a sign that your vision needs correcting.

- **Get regular eye exams**, and mention how many hours you spend at a screen.
- **Ask about computer or occupational glasses** if you wear progressives or bifocals and find yourself tipping your head back to read the screen. Glasses set for intermediate distance can help.
- **Keep your prescription current.** A small change can make a big difference at screen distance.

### What about blue-light glasses?

Blue-light filtering glasses are widely marketed for eye strain. The evidence that they reduce digital eye strain is limited, and the steps above, especially breaks, blinking, screen position, and good lighting, address the main causes more directly. If you find tinted lenses more comfortable, there's little harm, but don't rely on them in place of the basics.

## Common mistakes

- **Working in a dark room with a bright screen.**
- **Sitting too close** because the text is small.
- **Ignoring glare** from windows or overhead lights.
- **Skipping breaks** during focused work, which is exactly when they're most needed.
- **Assuming eye strain is normal** and not getting an eye exam.

## When to see an eye doctor

Occasional tired eyes after a long day are common. Make an appointment with an optometrist or ophthalmologist if you have:

- Eye strain or headaches most days despite changes to your setup.
- Persistent blurred or double vision.
- Eye pain, redness, or sensitivity to light that doesn't go away.
- Sudden changes in vision, which need prompt attention.
