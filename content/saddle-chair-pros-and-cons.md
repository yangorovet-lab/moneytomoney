A saddle chair's main advantage is that it puts you in a half-sitting, half-standing position with your hips open and your pelvis tilted forward, which makes an upright posture easier to hold and lets you work close to a desk or task. Its main drawbacks are no back support on most models, pressure in the groin area for some people, and an adjustment period that can take days or weeks. It works well for forward-focused tasks and as part of a mix of positions, less well as the only chair for a long desk job.

Below are the pros and cons in detail, who each point matters to, and how to set one up if you decide to try it.

## What a saddle chair is

A saddle chair has a seat shaped roughly like a horse saddle, often with a slight dip in the middle and sides that slope down. Some seats are split into two halves to reduce pressure. You sit much higher than on a regular chair, with your thighs angled downward and your feet flat on the floor, closer to a riding position than a typical desk seat.

That open angle between your torso and thighs, well beyond the 90 degrees of a regular chair, is the whole point. It allows the pelvis to roll slightly forward, which helps the lower back keep its natural inward curve without effort.

Saddle chairs are common in dental offices, labs, salons and art studios, where people lean over a patient or workpiece for long periods. They've also found their way into home offices, often next to height-adjustable desks.

## The pros

### Encourages an upright spine

With your hips open, the pelvis tilts forward and the lower back tends to keep its curve. Many people find they slump less than in a standard chair.

### Lets you get close to your work

Because your thighs slope down, your knees are out of the way. You can scoot close to a desk, workbench or drawing surface without your legs bumping into it.

### Works with higher desks

Saddle chairs sit higher than standard chairs, so they suit desks raised a few inches above normal sitting height, such as a standing desk set partway up. That makes them a natural fit for people who alternate between sitting and standing.

### Encourages movement

Many saddle chairs have a seat that tilts or a base with a small amount of give. Your legs are also more active than when slumped in a chair, helping stabilize you.

### Easy to get on and off

It's more like leaning onto a stool than sinking into a chair, so standing up is quick. That can make it easier to take frequent breaks.

### Compact

Most saddle chairs have no backrest or armrests, so they take up little space and slide under a desk.

## The cons

### No back support on most models

You hold yourself upright with your own muscles. That's fine for shorter sessions, but many people get tired over a long day and start to slump or perch. Some saddle chairs add a small backrest, which helps a little but isn't the same as reclining into a full office chair.

### Groin and inner-thigh pressure

The saddle shape spreads your legs apart and puts weight on the area between your seat bones. Some people find that uncomfortable. Split-seat designs and a gentler saddle profile can reduce this, but it doesn't suit everyone.

### An adjustment period

Most people need time to get used to a saddle chair. Starting with short sessions, around 20 to 30 minutes, and building up over a week or two is typical. Feeling some muscle fatigue in the lower back or legs early on is common.

### Not ideal for reclining or relaxing

You can't lean back for a long call or reading session. If you do a lot of that, you'll want a regular chair nearby.

### Needs the right desk height

Because you sit higher, a standard fixed-height desk may be too low. Your elbows should end up at or slightly above desk height. If they're well above it, you'll hunch.

### Clothing matters

Narrow skirts and some tight clothing make straddling the seat awkward.

## Pros and cons at a glance

| Pros | Cons |
|---|---|
| Opens hips and supports an upright spine | Usually no backrest |

| Pairs well with raised or standing desks | Takes time to adapt to |

## Who a saddle chair suits

- **People doing forward-leaning or close work:** drawing, crafts, lab work, dental or salon work, soldering, detailed reading.
- **Standing desk users** who want a higher perch between sitting and standing.
- **People who slump in regular chairs** and want something that encourages a more upright position.
- **People who move a lot and get up often.**

## Who should probably skip it

- **Anyone who needs to recline** or lean back regularly during the workday.
- **People with hip, groin or pelvic floor problems,** unless a clinician has suggested one.
- **People with a fixed-height standard desk** that can't be raised.
- **People who want one chair for a full eight-hour day** with no alternatives.

If you're mostly looking to fix slumping in your current chair, try adjusting it first: seat height so your feet rest flat, a rolled towel at the small of your back, and the backrest slightly reclined. If that doesn't help and your chair can't be adjusted, a new [[office-chairs|office chair with adjustable lumbar support]] may be a more practical all-day solution.

## How to set up a saddle chair

1. **Set the seat height first.** Raise it until your thighs slope down at a clear angle, with your feet flat on the floor and your knees below your hips.
2. **Check your hip width.** Your legs should rest naturally to either side, without strain in the inner thighs.
3. **Adjust the desk.** Your elbows should sit at about 90 degrees with your forearms resting lightly on the desk or keyboard. If you have an adjustable desk, raise it to match the chair. Many people use a height between their normal sitting and standing heights. If you're shopping for a desk to pair with one, [[standing-desks|height-adjustable standing desks]] give you the most flexibility.
4. **Set the monitor.** Top of the screen at or slightly below eye level, about an arm's length away. Because you sit higher, you'll likely need to raise the monitor compared with a regular chair.
5. **Tilt the seat,** if it tilts. A slight forward tilt helps some people, but too much makes you slide.
6. **Start short.** Use it for 20 to 30 minutes, then switch to another chair or stand. Increase gradually as you get used to it.

### Features to look for

- Height range that works with your desk
- Split or contoured seat to reduce pressure
- Seat tilt adjustment
- Firm but padded seat, not overly soft
- Casters suited to your floor
- Optional small backrest if you want occasional support

## Common mistakes

- **Setting it too low.** It then behaves like a strange stool with none of the benefits.
- **Using it all day from the start.** Build up gradually.
- **Leaving the desk at standard height.** You'll hunch over the keyboard.
- **Ignoring discomfort.** Numbness in the groin area or legs is a sign to stop and reconsider.

A health note: this is general information. If you have persistent or severe back, hip or pelvic pain, or numbness or tingling anywhere, see a doctor or physiotherapist before changing your seating.

## Bottom line

A saddle chair is a useful tool, not a universal upgrade. It shines for upright, forward-focused work, pairs well with a height-adjustable desk, and helps many people who slump in regular chairs. Its lack of back support and the pressure it puts on some people make it best as one part of a rotation, alongside a comfortable office chair and time spent standing.
