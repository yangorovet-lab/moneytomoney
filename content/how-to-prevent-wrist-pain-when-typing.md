Most typing-related wrist discomfort comes from three things: wrists bent up, down, or sideways for long periods; pressure on the underside of the wrist from a desk edge or wrist rest; and too many hours without a break. Prevent it by setting your keyboard at or slightly below elbow height, keeping your wrists roughly straight and floating while you type, resting on the heel of your palm only during pauses, and taking short breaks. Most of this costs nothing. Start with your setup, then your habits, and only then consider new gear.

If you already have wrist pain that lasts, keeps coming back, or comes with numbness, tingling, or weakness in your hand, see a doctor or physiotherapist. The advice here is about prevention and comfort, not treatment.

## What "neutral wrist" means

A neutral wrist is roughly in a straight line with your forearm, as if you were holding a glass of water loosely at your side and then lifted it forward. There are three directions to watch:

| Wrist position | What it looks like | Common cause |
|---|---|---|
| Bent up (extension) | Back of the hand tilts toward you | Keyboard feet raised, keyboard too high, heavy wrist rest use |
| Bent down (flexion) | Fingers droop below the wrist | Keyboard too low, typing from a lap |
| Bent sideways (ulnar deviation) | Hands angle outward toward the pinkies | Narrow keyboard, shoulders wider than the keys |

Small amounts of bending are normal. The goal is to avoid holding a strong bend for hours.

## Step 1: Fix the height first

The height of your keyboard relative to your elbows has the biggest effect on your wrists.

1. Sit with your shoulders relaxed and your upper arms hanging by your sides.
2. Bend your elbows to about 90 degrees, or a little more open.
3. Your keyboard's home row should be at about that height, or slightly lower.

If the desk is too high, which is common, raise your chair and support your feet with a box or footrest. If the desk is too low, raise the keyboard on a sturdy board, or raise the desk itself on risers if it is stable.

A keyboard that is too high forces your wrists to bend up and your shoulders to hunch. A keyboard that is too low makes your fingers droop.

## Step 2: Flatten the keyboard

Many keyboards have little flip-out feet at the back. They tilt the keyboard toward you, which bends your wrists upward. For most people, **keep the feet folded in**. Some people even find a slight negative tilt, with the back lower than the front, keeps their wrists straighter, especially at a standing desk or on a keyboard tray.

## Step 3: Get the keyboard and mouse close

Reaching forward for the keyboard or out to the side for the mouse puts extra strain on your wrists, forearms, and shoulders.

- Pull the keyboard close enough that your elbows stay near your sides.
- Center the main typing area (the letter keys, not the whole keyboard) in front of your body.
- Keep the mouse right next to the keyboard at the same height.

This last point is why many people with full-size keyboards shift toward keyboards without a number pad. The number pad pushes the mouse farther out to the right.

## Step 4: Stop typing on your laptop keyboard all day

Laptops put the screen and keyboard together, so you can't get both at the right height at once. If the screen is at eye level, the keyboard is far too high. If the keyboard is right, you are looking down at the screen.

For occasional use, that trade-off is fine. For daily use, raise the laptop to eye level on books or a stand and add a separate keyboard and mouse. An [[external-keyboards|external keyboard]] is one of the cheapest upgrades with the biggest payoff for anyone working mainly on a laptop.

## Step 5: Type with floating wrists

Now for habits.

- **Hover, don't anchor.** While actively typing, let your hands float over the keys and move your whole hand to reach distant keys, rather than planting your wrists and stretching your fingers.
- **Rest during pauses.** When you stop to think, rest the heel of your palm (the fleshy part at the base of your hand) on the desk or a rest, not the soft underside of your wrist.
- **Type lightly.** Many people press keys much harder than needed. Keys only need to register, not bottom out forcefully.
- **Use both hands for key combinations.** Instead of stretching one hand for Ctrl + Shift + a letter, split the keys between hands.
- **Relax your thumbs and pinkies.** Tension often builds in the outer fingers without you noticing.

## About wrist rests

Wrist rests are widely misunderstood. They are meant for **resting during pauses**, not for leaning on while you type. Pressing the underside of your wrist onto a rest while typing can put pressure on the area where tendons and nerves pass into your hand, and it also tends to bend the wrist upward.

If you use one:

- Choose a soft, flat rest at the same height as the front of the keyboard.
- Rest the heel of your palm on it, not your wrist.
- Lift your hands off it while you type.

A folded towel is a perfectly good free wrist rest to try first.

## Step 6: Watch the desk edge

A hard, sharp desk edge pressing into your forearms or wrists can be uncomfortable over time. Pull the keyboard back from the edge a few inches so your forearms rest on the desk, or pad the edge with a folded towel.

## Step 7: Don't forget the mouse

Mouse use often causes more wrist trouble than typing, because the wrist tends to be bent up and twisted palm-down for hours.

- Keep the mouse at keyboard height and close to your body.
- Move from your elbow and shoulder for large movements, not just your wrist.
- Raise pointer speed slightly so you need smaller movements.
- Learn keyboard shortcuts for frequent actions to reduce mouse use.

Some people find that a [[vertical-mice|vertical mouse]] feels more comfortable because it holds the hand in a handshake position rather than flat and twisted. It takes a few days to get used to, and it is not right for everyone, but it is worth considering if mouse use bothers you.

## Step 8: Take micro-breaks

Holding any position for a long time is a problem, even a good one. Build in short breaks:

- Every 20 to 30 minutes, drop your hands into your lap, shake them out gently, and open and close your fingers a few times.
- Every hour or so, get up and move for a couple of minutes.
- Change tasks when you can, alternating typing with calls, reading, or thinking.

Gentle stretches, such as holding your arm out with palm up and lightly pulling your fingers back, or doing the same with palm down, can feel good. Stretches should never hurt. If they cause pain, stop.

## Keyboard types to consider

You don't need a special keyboard to avoid wrist pain, but some features can help:

- **Compact or tenkeyless layout:** keeps the mouse closer.
- **Low-profile keys:** reduce how far your wrists need to bend upward.
- **Split or angled keyboards:** let your hands sit at shoulder width, reducing sideways bending. These take time to learn.
- **Light key action:** reduces how hard you need to press.

Fix your setup and habits before buying anything. A new keyboard used at the wrong height won't help much.

## Common mistakes

- Using the keyboard's back feet to raise it.
- Planting your wrists on a gel rest and typing from that position.
- Typing on a laptop on your lap or sofa for hours.
- Placing the mouse far out to the side.
- Pushing through discomfort to finish a task instead of taking a break.

## Quick checklist

1. Keyboard at or slightly below elbow height.
2. Keyboard feet folded in.
3. Keyboard and mouse close to your body, at the same height.
4. Wrists straight and floating while typing.
5. Palm heels, not wrists, on any rest during pauses.
6. External keyboard and mouse for daily laptop use.
7. Short hand breaks every 20 to 30 minutes.
8. Persistent pain, numbness, or tingling checked by a professional.
