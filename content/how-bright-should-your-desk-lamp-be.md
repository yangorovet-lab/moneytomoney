For most home office desks, a lamp that puts roughly 300 to 500 lux on your work surface is enough for mixed screen and paper work, and 500 to 750 lux or more helps for detailed reading, drawing, or older eyes. In practical terms, that usually means a desk lamp in the range of about 400 to 800 lumens, placed close to the work area and dimmable. The right number depends on how far the lamp sits from the desk, how bright the rest of the room is, and whether you mostly look at a screen or at paper. Dimmability matters more than any single figure, because the right brightness changes through the day.

## Lumens vs. lux: the two numbers that matter

Lamp boxes and listings use a few different terms. Two are worth understanding:

- **Lumens** measure the total light a lamp puts out in all directions. It's the number on the box.
- **Lux** measures how much light actually lands on a surface, like your desk. One lux equals one lumen spread over one square meter.

Lux is what your eyes care about, but it depends on distance and beam spread. A 500-lumen lamp aimed tightly at a small area from 16 inches away can deliver far more lux on your notebook than an 800-lumen lamp bouncing light around the room from a shelf. That's why a lamp's lumen rating is only a starting point.

## How bright for different tasks

These ranges are rough, widely used targets for light on the work surface. Treat them as a guide and adjust by comfort.

| What you're doing | Light on the desk (approx.) | Typical lamp output |
|---|---|---|
| Mostly screen work, room already lit | 300 to 500 lux | 300 to 500 lumens, dimmed as needed |
| Mixed screen and paper | 500 lux | 450 to 800 lumens |
| Reading small print, handwriting, sketching | 750 lux or more | 800 lumens or more, close to the page |
| Fine detail work (crafts, repairs) | 1,000 lux or more | Bright, focused task light |

Two notes:

1. **Screen work needs less desk light than paper work.** Your monitor is its own light source. Too much lamp light on the desk around it can make the screen look washed out and create glare.
2. **Older eyes need more light.** It's common for people in their 50s and 60s to need noticeably more light for the same reading task than they did in their 20s. If you've been squinting at paper lately, brightness may be part of the answer.

## Signs your lamp is too dim

- You lean toward the paper or screen to read.
- You find yourself squinting, especially in the evening.
- Shadows from your hand fall across the page you're writing on.
- The screen looks like a bright rectangle floating in darkness.
- Your eyes feel tired sooner in the evening than in the morning.

## Signs your lamp is too bright

Brighter isn't always better. Too much light causes its own problems:

- You see the lamp's reflection or a bright patch on your monitor.
- White paper on the desk looks glaring, almost like a light source.
- The bare LED or bulb is visible from where you sit.
- Your screen seems dim or low-contrast even at high brightness settings.
- You feel a mild squint or headache even though everything is "well lit."

If any of these apply, lower the brightness, move the lamp, or angle the head down before buying anything.

## Balance the lamp with the room

A quick rule: the screen, the desk, and the room behind the screen should all be in the same general brightness range. Big jumps between them make your eyes keep adapting.

- **Don't use a desk lamp as the only light in a dark room.** A bright pool of light on the desk surrounded by darkness is tiring. Keep a ceiling light, floor lamp, or wall light on too.
- **Match the screen to the desk.** Hold a sheet of white paper next to your monitor. If the screen looks much brighter, turn the screen down or add desk light. If the paper looks much brighter, turn the lamp down or the screen up.
- **Mind the wall behind your monitor.** A soft light behind or above the monitor (not aimed at you) can reduce the contrast between the bright screen and a dark wall in the evening.

## Placement matters as much as brightness

A lamp at the wrong angle can create glare no matter how bright it is. Before changing bulbs:

- **Put the lamp on the opposite side of your writing hand** so your hand doesn't cast a shadow across the page. Right-handers usually put it on the left.
- **Keep the light source below your eye level or shielded**, so you never look straight into it.
- **Aim it at the desk, not the screen.** Angle the head downward and to the side of the monitor.
- **Bring it closer rather than turning it up.** Moving a lamp from 24 inches to 16 inches above the desk increases the light on the surface considerably, often more than stepping up to a stronger bulb.

If your desk is tight and the lamp keeps hitting the screen, a [[monitor-light-bars|monitor light bar]] clips to the top of the monitor and shines down on the desk in front of it, with an asymmetric beam designed to keep light off the screen itself.

## Color temperature and quality

Brightness is only part of comfortable light. Look at these too:

- **Color temperature (in Kelvin).** Around 2,700K to 3,000K is warm and relaxed, good for evenings. Around 4,000K is neutral and works well for most desk work. Above about 5,000K is cool and crisp, which some people like for daytime focus and others find harsh. Adjustable color temperature lets you use cooler light by day and warmer light at night.
- **Color rendering (CRI).** A CRI of 90 or higher makes colors and skin tones look natural and text easier to read. It matters most for art, photos, or reading printed color.
- **Flicker.** Some low-quality LEDs and dimmers flicker faster than you can see. Look for lamps described as flicker-free, especially if you're sensitive to it.
- **Diffusion.** A frosted cover or wide diffuser spreads light and softens shadows, while a bare, bright LED strip can feel glaring.

## What to look for in a desk lamp

If you're shopping, these features do more for comfort than raw lumens:

- **Stepless or multi-level dimming**, so you can dial in the right brightness for daylight versus nighttime.
- **Adjustable head and arm** that lets you aim light where you need it and away from the screen.
- **Enough reach** to put the light over the center of your work area, not just the corner of the desk.
- **Adjustable color temperature** if you work both day and night.
- **A shielded light source** you can't see directly from your seat.

A dimmable [[desk-lamps|adjustable LED desk lamp]] with these features will suit almost any home office better than a very bright lamp with a single setting.

## A simple setup routine

1. **Turn on your normal room lighting.** Don't set up the lamp in the dark.
2. **Position the lamp** on the side opposite your writing hand, head angled down toward the desk, light source out of your line of sight.
3. **Start at a medium setting** and do the white-paper test next to your screen.
4. **Adjust the lamp or the screen** until they look similar in brightness.
5. **Check for reflections** on your monitor from your normal seated position.
6. **Revisit in the evening.** Most people want the lamp a little dimmer and warmer at night and the room light on.

## Free fixes before buying a new lamp

- Swap the bulb in an existing lamp for a brighter or better-quality LED, if the fixture allows it.
- Move the lamp closer to the desk.
- Put a sheet of paper or a light fabric shade over a harsh bare bulb (with enough clearance for heat, and only with LED bulbs).
- Turn off an overhead light that reflects in your screen and use a side lamp instead.
- Clean dusty lamp shades and diffusers; dust cuts output more than you'd expect.

## Eye comfort beyond lighting

Good lighting reduces eye strain but doesn't eliminate it. Take short breaks from close focus, blink regularly, and keep your monitor at a comfortable distance. If you have persistent headaches, blurred vision, or eye discomfort that doesn't improve with better lighting, get your eyes checked by an optometrist or doctor. An outdated prescription is a common cause of strain.

## Bottom line

Aim for roughly 300 to 500 lux on the desk for screen-heavy work and 500 to 750 lux or more for reading and detail work, which typically means a dimmable lamp of about 400 to 800 lumens placed close to the task. Keep the room lit too, point the lamp away from the screen, and choose dimming and good placement over sheer brightness.
