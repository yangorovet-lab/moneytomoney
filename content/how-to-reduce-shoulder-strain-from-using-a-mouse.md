Most shoulder strain from using a mouse comes from one thing: your arm is reaching. When the mouse sits too far to the side, too far forward, or too high, your shoulder has to hold your arm away from your body for hours, and the muscles on top of the shoulder and neck never get to relax. The fix is to bring the mouse close, low and in line with your shoulder, support your forearm, and change your settings so small movements do more of the work. Most of that costs nothing.

## Why a mouse strains your shoulder

The mouse itself is light. The problem is the posture needed to use it.

- **Reaching sideways.** A full-size keyboard with a number pad pushes the mouse far to the right. Your arm swings out from your side to reach it.
- **Reaching forward.** If the mouse is far back on a deep desk, your arm extends forward and the front of your shoulder works constantly.
- **Hiking the shoulder.** A desk that's too high makes you lift your shoulder up to get your forearm onto it.
- **Static holding.** Even small, steady muscle effort is tiring when it lasts for hours without a break.
- **Big arm movements.** Low pointer speed means crossing the screen requires sweeping the whole arm.

Each of these adds a little load. Together they're a common cause of a sore, tight shoulder and neck on the mouse side.

## The target position

Aim for this:

- **Upper arm hanging close to your side,** roughly vertical, not angled forward or out.
- **Elbow bent around 90 degrees,** or slightly more open.
- **Forearm roughly level,** resting lightly on the desk or an armrest.
- **Shoulders relaxed and down,** not shrugged.
- **Mouse within easy reach** right next to the keyboard, so you can grab it without leaning or stretching.

Quick test: sit back, let your arm hang, bend your elbow, and see where your hand lands. That's where your mouse should be, or very close to it.

## Free fixes to try first

### 1. Move the mouse closer

Pull the mouse in toward your body and snug against the keyboard. If there's a gap of more than a few centimeters (an inch or two), close it.

### 2. Deal with the number pad

A full-size keyboard is wide, and the number pad pushes the mouse outward. Options:

- **Shift the keyboard to the left** so the letter keys, not the whole keyboard, are centered on your body. The B key roughly in front of your belly button is a common reference.
- **Use a tenkeyless or compact keyboard** if you rarely use the number pad. That alone can bring the mouse 8 to 10 cm (3 to 4 inches) closer.
- **Try the mouse on the left,** or alternate hands. It feels clumsy for a week or two, but it spreads the load and is free.

### 3. Lower the work surface or raise the chair

If your forearm has to climb up onto the desk, your shoulder hikes. Raise your chair until your elbows are at or slightly above desk height. If your feet no longer rest flat, add a box, a stack of books, or a footrest under them.

### 4. Support the forearm

Resting the forearm on the desk or a padded armrest takes weight off the shoulder. If your chair's armrests are adjustable, set them level with the desk so your forearm can rest across both. A folded towel on the edge of a hard desk makes this more comfortable.

### 5. Speed up the pointer

Increase pointer speed (or mouse DPI in the settings software) so small wrist and forearm movements cover the screen. Start with a modest increase and adjust over a few days. If you turn it up too fast, precision suffers.

### 6. Use keyboard shortcuts

Every shortcut is a mouse trip you don't make. Learning shortcuts for copy, paste, switching windows, closing tabs, and scrolling can remove a surprising amount of mousing.

### 7. Take micro-breaks

Let go of the mouse when you're reading. Drop your hand into your lap. Roll your shoulders back and down every half hour or so. A short pause every so often matters more than a long break once a day.

## Check your whole setup

Shoulder strain rarely comes from the mouse alone. Look at the rest of your workstation too:

| Area | What to check | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Chair height | Elbows level with or slightly above desk | Raise the chair, support feet |
| Armrests | Too high shrugs shoulders, too wide pushes elbows out | Lower, narrow or remove them |
| Monitor | Too high or off to one side | Top at or slightly below eye level, centered, about an arm's length away |
| Laptop use | Using the trackpad on a raised laptop | Separate keyboard and mouse at desk height |
| Desk depth | Mouse set far forward | Bring keyboard and mouse to the front edge |

## When buying something helps

After free fixes, a few purchases can make a real difference for some people.

### A narrower keyboard

A compact keyboard without a number pad lets the mouse sit closer to your body. If you need a number pad, a separate one can sit on the left or be put away. You can [[external-keyboards|look at compact tenkeyless keyboards]] if your current one is full size.

### A different mouse shape

Standard mice turn your forearm palm-down, which some people find tiring for the forearm and shoulder. A vertical mouse holds your hand in a handshake position. It isn't a cure, and some people don't like it, but it's worth considering if you also have forearm or wrist discomfort. If you're curious, you can [[vertical-mice|browse vertical mice in different sizes]] and pick one that matches your hand length.

Other options include trackballs (the device stays still, so the arm doesn't move) and centered pointing devices that sit in front of the keyboard. These all aim at the same goal: less reaching.

### Better chair armrests

If your chair's armrests don't adjust, they may push your shoulders up or your elbows out. Chairs with height- and width-adjustable armrests make forearm support much easier.

## Simple stretches and movements

These are general mobility movements many people find helpful during the day. Move gently, and stop if anything hurts.

- **Shoulder rolls:** roll both shoulders up, back and down slowly, 5 to 10 times.
- **Shoulder blade squeeze:** pull your shoulder blades gently together and down, hold a few seconds, release. Repeat a few times.
- **Upper trap stretch:** sit tall, tilt your ear toward the opposite shoulder, and hold for 15 to 30 seconds. Switch sides.
- **Doorway chest stretch:** place forearms on a door frame and lean gently forward until you feel a stretch across the chest.
- **Arm swing breaks:** stand up and let both arms swing loosely for a few seconds.

## When to see a professional

Some shoulder discomfort after a long day is common. See a doctor or physiotherapist if you have:

- Pain that lasts more than a couple of weeks despite setup changes
- Pain that wakes you at night or limits lifting your arm
- Numbness, tingling, or weakness in the arm or hand
- Pain after an injury or fall

A professional can assess what's going on and suggest exercises that fit your situation.

## Quick checklist

- Mouse right next to the keyboard, within easy reach
- Upper arm hanging close to your side
- Elbow around 90 degrees, forearm supported
- Shoulders relaxed, not shrugged
- Keyboard centered on the letter keys, or compact
- Pointer speed high enough for small movements
- Shortcuts and short breaks built into the day
