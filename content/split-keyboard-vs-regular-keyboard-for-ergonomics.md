A split keyboard is ergonomically better than a regular keyboard for most people who type for hours, because it lets your hands sit at shoulder width with straight wrists instead of bending outward to meet in the middle. The catch is a learning curve and a higher price. If you type a lot and feel wrist, forearm or shoulder tension, a split design is worth considering. If you type lightly and feel fine, a well-placed regular keyboard (ideally one without a number pad) is usually good enough.

## What's actually wrong with a regular keyboard?

A standard keyboard puts all the keys in one narrow block. Your shoulders are wider than that block, so to reach the home row your arms angle inward and your wrists bend outward toward the pinky side. That outward bend is called ulnar deviation.

A regular keyboard can also cause:

- **Pronation.** Keyboards lie flat, so your palms face straight down. That rotates your forearms fully, which some people feel as tension along the top of the forearm.
- **Wrist extension.** If the rear feet are flipped up, or the keyboard sits too high, your wrists bend upward.
- **Shoulder hunching.** Hands close together pull your shoulders slightly forward and in.

None of these are dangerous on their own for a short session. The problem is holding them for thousands of keystrokes a day, every day.

## What a split keyboard changes

A split keyboard divides the keys into two halves. Depending on the design, it can address one or more of the issues above.

| Feature | What it fixes | Found on |
|---|---|---|
| Separated halves | Ulnar deviation; lets hands sit at shoulder width | Fully split keyboards |
| Angled halves in one body | Reduces ulnar deviation, but only partly | Fixed-split "wave" or "ergonomic" boards |
| Tenting (raising the inner edges) | Pronation; puts palms in a more handshake-like angle | Many split boards, sometimes via add-on legs |
| Negative tilt (front higher than back) | Wrist extension | Some ergonomic boards, or add-on risers |
| Column-stagger layout | Finger stretch; keys follow finger lengths | Many enthusiast split boards |
| Thumb clusters | Offloads work from pinkies to stronger thumbs | Many enthusiast split boards |

### Fixed split vs fully split

- **Fixed split (one-piece)** keyboards angle the two halves in a single body, often with a built-in palm rest and a slight hump in the middle. They're easier to adopt, feel familiar and usually keep a standard key layout. The downside is you can't adjust the width or angle.
- **Fully split** keyboards come as two separate pieces connected by a cable or wirelessly. You can set them exactly at shoulder width, rotate each half, and add tenting. They offer the most adjustment but need more desk space and a longer adjustment period.

## Split vs regular: side-by-side

| | Regular keyboard | Fixed split | Fully split |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wrist angle | Bent outward | Improved | Fully adjustable |
| Forearm rotation | Palms flat | Often a slight tent | Adjustable tenting on many |
| Learning curve | None | A few days | One to several weeks, longer with a new layout |
| Price range | Lowest | Moderate | Moderate to high |
| Desk space | Small, especially compact sizes | Wider and deeper | Flexible, but two pieces |
| Portability | Easy | Bulky | Varies |
| Shortcuts and gaming | Familiar | Mostly familiar | Can take relearning |

## Who benefits most from a split keyboard

A split design is a strong option if:

- **You type for several hours a day.** Writers, programmers, support agents and anyone living in email.
- **You have broad shoulders.** The wider your frame, the more a narrow keyboard forces your wrists to bend.
- **You feel tension on the pinky side of your wrist or along your forearm** after typing.
- **You notice your shoulders creeping forward** or up toward your ears as the day goes on.
- **You're willing to practice for a week or two.** Most of the payoff depends on sticking with it.

## Who is fine with a regular keyboard

A regular keyboard can be perfectly comfortable if:

- You type in short bursts and use the mouse more than the keyboard.
- You have a narrow frame and your wrists already look fairly straight.
- You rely heavily on hunt-and-peck typing. Split keyboards assume touch typing, because keys like B, G and T are split between halves. If you reach across with the "wrong" hand, a split board will frustrate you.
- You switch between many computers and don't want to relearn anything.

## Free fixes to try before you buy anything

Many keyboard complaints come from setup, not the keyboard itself. Try these first:

1. **Flip the rear feet down.** A flat or slightly negative tilt reduces upward wrist bend.
2. **Lower the keyboard** so your elbows are around 90 degrees and your forearms are roughly parallel to the floor. If your desk can't go lower, raise your chair instead and put a footrest or a sturdy box under your feet.
3. **Center the letter keys, not the whole keyboard,** on your body. A full-size keyboard with a number pad pushes the letters to the left and your mouse far to the right.
4. **Float your wrists while typing,** and rest the heels of your palms only during pauses.
5. **Type more lightly.** Bottoming out every key with force adds strain no keyboard design can fix.
6. **Take short breaks** every 30 to 60 minutes to let your hands and forearms relax.

If you do these and still feel wrist or forearm tension, the keyboard shape is a more likely culprit.

## A middle-ground option: go compact

If a split keyboard feels like too big a jump, a tenkeyless (no number pad) or smaller keyboard helps more than people expect. It doesn't fix wrist bend, but it lets you keep the mouse closer to your body, which reduces reaching with your mouse arm and shoulder. You can use a separate number pad on the left when you need one.

## How to get used to a split keyboard

The first few days can feel slow. These steps shorten the adjustment:

- **Start with a shoulder-width gap.** For fully split boards, place each half roughly in line with your shoulders, then rotate them slightly so your wrists look straight from above.
- **Add tenting gradually.** Start low. Steep tenting can feel odd and may push your shoulders up if the keyboard is too high.
- **Check your key splits.** Know which hand owns B, T, G and Y. Practice on a typing tutor for 10 to 15 minutes a day.
- **Don't switch back and forth at first.** Using both keyboards in the same week slows the adjustment.
- **Expect a speed dip.** It's normal for your typing speed to drop at first and recover over time.
- **Keep the mouse close.** With a gap in the middle, some people put the mouse or a trackpad between the halves.

## What to look for when shopping

Focus on features, not brand names:

- **Adjustable separation** (or a fixed angle wide enough for your shoulders).
- **Tenting options,** built in or with add-on legs.
- **Flat or negative tilt,** not just rear feet that raise the back.
- **A familiar layout** if you want a quick transition; column stagger and thumb clusters if you're ready for a bigger change.
- **Palm rests** that support the heels of your hands during pauses, not the wrist crease.
- **Key feel.** Lighter switches or keys can help if you tend to type hard.
- **Connectivity** that suits your setup: wired halves, wireless, or both.

## Common mistakes

- **Buying a split keyboard and leaving it too high.** The desk height and chair height matter more than the keyboard shape.
- **Placing the halves too close together,** recreating the regular keyboard angle.
- **Tenting too steeply on day one.**
- **Resting wrists on a hard edge while typing,** which presses on the underside of the wrist.
- **Expecting instant relief.** A better keyboard reduces strain over time; it doesn't reverse irritation overnight.

## A note on pain

Keyboards can reduce awkward postures, but they aren't a treatment. If you have persistent or severe pain, numbness, tingling, or weakness in your hands or fingers, get it checked by a doctor or physiotherapist rather than relying on new equipment to solve it.

## Bottom line

- **Heavy typist with wrist or forearm tension:** a split keyboard, fixed or fully split, is the more ergonomic choice.
- **Want an easy transition:** a fixed-split, one-piece board.
- **Want maximum adjustment and don't mind a learning curve:** a fully split board with tenting.
- **Light typist, no discomfort:** keep a regular keyboard, go compact, lower it, flip the feet down, and center the letter keys on your body.
