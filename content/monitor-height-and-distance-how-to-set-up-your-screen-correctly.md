Set your monitor so the top edge of the screen is at or slightly below eye level, the screen is roughly an arm's length away (about 20 to 30 inches for most people), and the display is tilted back a little so you look at it straight on. That keeps your head balanced over your shoulders instead of tipping forward or back. Below: how to get there in a few minutes, how to adjust for screen size, glasses and desk type, and which fixes cost nothing.

## Why monitor position matters

Your head is heavy, and your neck muscles work harder the further it drifts from a neutral, balanced position. A screen that is too low pulls your chin down and your shoulders forward. A screen that is too high makes you tilt your head back and can dry out your eyes because your eyelids open wider. A screen that is too far away makes you lean in; one that is too close makes your eyes work harder.

None of this matters in five minutes. It matters when you hold the same slightly-off posture for hours a day, every workday.

## Step 1: Set your chair first

Monitor height only makes sense relative to your eyes, and your eye height depends on your chair. So start there:

- Sit all the way back in the chair with your lower back supported. If the chair has no lumbar support, a rolled-up towel or small cushion behind your lower back works fine.
- Adjust seat height so your feet rest flat on the floor and your thighs are roughly parallel to it.
- Your elbows should sit around 90 degrees with your forearms level when your hands are on the keyboard.
- If raising the chair to fit the desk leaves your feet dangling, put a sturdy box, a stack of books or a footrest under them.

## Step 2: Set the monitor height

Sit upright, look straight ahead, and notice where your gaze lands. That is your horizontal eye line.

- **Top edge of the screen:** at or slightly below that eye line.
- **Center of the screen:** somewhere around 15 to 20 degrees below your eye line. In practice, this means your eyes rest naturally on the upper third of the screen and you glance slightly down to read most content.
- **Quick test:** close your eyes, relax your neck, then open them. If you are looking at the top part of the screen without moving your head, you are close.

If your monitor stand does not go high enough, the free fix is a stack of hardcover books, a ream of printer paper or a sturdy box under the base. Make sure whatever you use is wider than the monitor's foot and will not wobble.

### Adjust for bifocals and progressive lenses

If you wear bifocals or progressive lenses, you read through the bottom of the lens, so a screen at standard height makes you tip your head back. Lower the monitor further, often so the top sits a few inches below eye level, and tilt it back more. Computer glasses set for screen distance are another option; ask your optometrist.

## Step 3: Set the viewing distance

A good starting point is roughly an arm's length. Sit back in your chair and stretch your arm forward: your fingertips should land near the screen, or just short of it.

From there, adjust by feel:

- If you lean forward to read text, move the screen closer or increase your display scaling.
- If you have to turn your head to see the edges of the screen, move it further back.

Bigger text is usually a better fix than a closer screen. It keeps the whole display in view and stops the slow drift toward the monitor during the day.

### Rough distance by screen size

Bigger screens generally need to sit further back so you can take in most of the display without swiveling your head. These are starting points, not rules. Adjust to what feels comfortable for your eyes.

| Screen size | Typical starting distance | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 21-24 inch | 20-28 inches (50-70 cm) | Standard arm's-length setup works well |
| 27 inch | 24-32 inches (60-80 cm) | Consider higher scaling at 1440p or 4K |
| 32 inch | 28-36 inches (70-90 cm) | May need a deeper desk or a monitor arm |
| 34 inch ultrawide | 28-36 inches (70-90 cm) | Curved screens help keep edges at a similar distance |
| Laptop (13-16 inch) | 18-24 inches (45-60 cm) | Only practical with a stand and external keyboard |

If your desk is too shallow to get enough distance from a large monitor, pushing the monitor to the very back edge helps. A clamp-on arm frees up more depth because there is no base taking up space; you can [[monitor-arms|browse gas-spring monitor arms]] if that is the bottleneck.

## Step 4: Tilt and center the screen

- **Tilt:** angle the screen back about 10 to 20 degrees, so the surface is roughly perpendicular to your line of sight. The top edge should be slightly further from you than the bottom.
- **Centering:** the screen you use most should be directly in front of you, lined up with your keyboard and your body. A monitor that sits even a little off to one side keeps your neck slightly rotated all day.

## Setting up two monitors

Dual screens are where many setups go wrong. The right arrangement depends on how you use them:

- **One main screen, one secondary:** put the main screen directly in front of you and the second one to the side, angled toward you so it forms a gentle curve. Keep both at the same height.
- **Both used equally:** place them side by side with the seam between them centered in front of your nose, and angle them inward slightly.
- **Stacked vertically:** only do this if the top screen is for occasional glances. Looking up at a screen above eye level for long periods is a common source of neck strain.

## Laptops: the hardest screen to get right

A laptop's screen and keyboard are attached, so you cannot get both in a good position at the same time. If the keyboard is at elbow height, the screen is far too low. If the screen is at eye height, the keyboard is far too high.

The solution is to separate them:

1. Raise the laptop so the top of its screen is at or just below eye level. Books or a box work; a stand is tidier and usually adjustable. If you want one, [[laptop-stands|compare adjustable laptop stands]].
2. Use a separate keyboard and mouse at elbow height.
3. If you work on the laptop all day, consider plugging it into an external monitor and using the laptop screen as a secondary display, or closing it entirely.

## Standing desks

When you switch between sitting and standing, check the setup in both positions:

- Set desk height by your elbows first (roughly 90 degrees, forearms level), then check the screen.
- If the monitor is right when sitting but too low when standing, the issue is usually your standing desk height or your posture, not the monitor. Many people slouch or lock their knees when standing.
- A monitor arm makes it easier to fine-tune height and tilt in each position.

## Lighting and glare

Glare pushes you into awkward postures as you dodge reflections.

- Place the monitor perpendicular to windows where possible, not directly in front of or behind them.
- If overhead lights reflect in the screen, adjust the tilt slightly or dim that light.
- Keep screen brightness close to the brightness of the room. A bright screen in a dark room, or a dim screen in a sunny room, both tire your eyes.

## Common mistakes

- **Setting the monitor before the chair.** Your eye height changes when you adjust your seat, so always do the chair first.
- **Mixing screens at different heights.** If a low laptop sits beside a higher monitor and you use both, your neck keeps moving up and down all day.
- **Leaning in instead of scaling text.** If you catch yourself creeping toward the screen, increase text size.
- **Ignoring glasses.** Progressive and bifocal lens wearers need a lower screen than the standard advice suggests.
- **Setting it once and forgetting it.** Recheck after changing chairs, desks or glasses.

## When it still hurts

Screen position is one factor among several. Take short breaks, get up and move, and rest your eyes. A widely used habit is the 20-20-20 rule: every 20 minutes, look at something about 20 feet away for about 20 seconds.

If you have neck, shoulder or eye pain that persists after fixing your setup, or you notice numbness, tingling or headaches, get it checked by a doctor, physiotherapist or optometrist. This guide is general information, not medical advice.

## Quick checklist

- Chair set first: feet flat, thighs roughly level, elbows around 90 degrees
- Top of the screen at or slightly below eye level
- Eyes land naturally on the upper third of the screen
- Screen roughly an arm's length away (further for larger monitors)
- Screen tilted back 10 to 20 degrees
- Main monitor centered in front of you, lined up with the keyboard
- Two monitors at matching height and distance
- Laptop raised, with a separate keyboard and mouse
- Text large enough that you never need to lean in

## Bottom line

Most people can fix their monitor setup in ten minutes with a stack of books. Set your chair, raise the screen until the top edge meets your eye line, push it back to about an arm's length, and tilt it slightly. Buy equipment only if free fixes cannot get you there.
