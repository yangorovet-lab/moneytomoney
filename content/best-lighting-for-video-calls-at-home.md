The best lighting for video calls is soft, even light coming from in front of you, slightly above eye level, with nothing bright behind you. A window facing you is often the best free option. When daylight isn't reliable, a lamp with a diffuser or a light with adjustable brightness and color temperature, placed just behind your screen, does the same job. Most bad call lighting comes down to two mistakes: a bright window behind you, or a single overhead light casting shadows under your eyes.

## What good video call lighting looks like

Webcams, especially built-in laptop cameras, have small sensors that struggle in dim or uneven light. When there isn't enough light, the image gets grainy, soft and oddly colored. When the light is uneven, the camera tries to average it and ends up underexposing your face or blowing out the background.

Good lighting gives you:

- **Your face as the brightest thing in the frame**, or close to it
- **Soft shadows**, not harsh lines under the nose and eyes
- **Natural skin tones**, without an orange or blue cast
- **Even light on both sides of your face**, or a gentle, intentional falloff

## Start with what you already have

### Use a window, the right way

Daylight is soft, bright and flattering, and it's free.

- **Face the window**, or sit at a slight angle to it. Your screen goes between you and the window, and the light falls on your face.
- **Never sit with the window behind you.** The camera will expose for the bright window and turn you into a silhouette.
- **Window to one side** works too, giving a more dramatic look with one side brighter. Fill the dark side with a lamp or a white surface if the contrast is too strong.
- **Direct sun is too harsh.** Use a sheer curtain, a thin white sheet, or partially closed blinds to soften it.

The downside: daylight changes through the day and with the weather. If you take calls at varying times, add a lamp as backup.

### Move a lamp you already own

A desk or floor lamp can work as a key light (your main light).

- Place it behind your screen, slightly to one side, pointing toward your face.
- Raise it so the light comes from a bit above eye level.
- Soften it: bounce it off a white wall, or drape a thin white cloth or baking paper over the shade, keeping it well clear of the bulb. Check that the bulb doesn't get hot enough to be a fire risk; LED bulbs run much cooler than older types.

### Turn off or balance the overhead light

A ceiling light directly above you creates shadows in your eye sockets and under your nose. Turn it off if your front light is strong enough. If you need it on for room brightness, make sure the light in front of you is brighter.

## Light placement cheat sheet

| Setup | Placement | Look | Good for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single front light | Behind the screen, centered, just above eye level | Even, flat, very clean | Most people, glasses wearers with some adjustment |
| Key light at 45 degrees | To one side of the screen, angled toward you, above eye level | More depth and shape | People who want a more professional look |
| Key plus fill | Main light at 45 degrees, weaker light or white surface on the other side | Balanced with soft shadows | Frequent callers, presenters |
| Window in front | Screen between you and window | Soft, natural | Daytime calls |
| Window beside you plus lamp | Window on one side, lamp on the other | Natural with balanced fill | Home offices with a side window |

## Choose the right light color

Light color is measured in kelvin (K). Lower numbers are warmer and more orange, higher numbers are cooler and bluer.

- **Around 2,700 to 3,000K:** warm, cozy, typical home bulbs. Can look orange on camera.
- **Around 4,000 to 5,000K:** neutral. Usually the most natural-looking skin tones on camera.
- **Around 5,500 to 6,500K:** close to daylight, can look slightly blue indoors.

The most important thing is **matching**. If you mix a warm lamp with cool daylight, one side of your face may look orange and the other blue. Either match your lamp to the daylight, or block the daylight and rely on the lamp.

If your light has adjustable color temperature, start around neutral and tweak until your skin looks natural on camera.

## When to buy a dedicated light

Buy a light if you take frequent calls, work in a dark room, or need to look consistent at any time of day. Useful features:

- **Adjustable brightness**, so you can balance your face with the room.
- **Adjustable color temperature**, to match daylight or room light.
- **A diffuser**, so the light is soft rather than a hard point.
- **A flexible mount or clamp**, so you can place it behind your screen at the right height.
- **USB or mains power**, depending on your desk setup.

A [[desk-lamps|dimmable LED desk lamp with adjustable color temperature]] can double as your work light and your call light. Ring lights and clip-on panels are popular too; smaller ones clip on your monitor, larger ones sit on a stand. Ring lights are easy to use but can leave a circular reflection in glasses and in the eyes.

## Fixing common lighting problems

**You look dark and the background is bright:**
Something bright is behind you. Turn around to face the window, close blinds behind you, or add a light in front of you.

**Your face looks orange:**
Your bulb is too warm, or the camera is struggling to balance mixed light. Use a neutral bulb or turn off the conflicting light source.

**Harsh shadows under your eyes and nose:**
The main light is directly overhead. Add light in front of you, slightly above eye level.

**Glare in your glasses:**
Raise the light higher and move it off to the side, so reflections bounce away from the camera. Tilting your glasses very slightly downward can also help. Larger, softer light sources create less noticeable reflections than small bright ones.

**One side of your face is much darker:**
Add a fill: a second lamp at lower brightness, or a white surface such as a poster board or a white wall on the dark side.

**Grainy image:**
The room is too dark for your camera. Add more light in front of you before you consider a new webcam.

**Shiny forehead:**
The light is too hard and too direct. Diffuse it or move it slightly off-axis.

## Don't forget your eyes

Lighting for the camera shouldn't make your working day harder.

- Don't point bright lights straight into your eyes. Position them above eye level and slightly to the side, or diffuse them.
- Keep brightness comfortable. You need enough light for the camera, not a spotlight.
- Balance screen brightness with the room. A very bright screen in a dark room is tiring.

## The background matters too

A good background supports good lighting:

- A softly lit background adds depth. A small lamp in the back corner can separate you from the wall.
- Avoid bright windows, lamps, or strong reflections behind you.
- Keep the background a little darker than your face so you stand out.

## Quick checklist

- Face the window or put a light behind your screen
- Light slightly above eye level, soft and diffused
- Nothing bright behind you
- Overhead light off or weaker than the front light
- Neutral light color, and don't mix warm and cool sources
- Adjust height and angle to cut glasses glare
- Add a fill light or white surface if one side of your face is dark
