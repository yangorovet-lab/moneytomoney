Choose armrests that adjust in height at the very least, and that can drop low enough to slide under your desk or sit level with it. Beyond height, width adjustment matters most if you have narrow or broad shoulders, and depth and pivot adjustments are nice extras for people who type and mouse for long hours. A good armrest lets your forearms rest lightly with your shoulders relaxed; a bad one pushes your shoulders up or keeps you from pulling close to the desk.

Here's what the different armrest types mean and how to pick the right one for your body and desk.

## What armrests are actually for

Armrests support some of the weight of your arms, which takes load off your shoulders and neck. They also help you get in and out of the chair and give your forearms somewhere to rest while you read, take calls or lean back.

What they shouldn't do:

- **Push your shoulders up.** If the armrests are too high, you shrug all day, which can leave your neck and upper shoulders tense.
- **Keep you away from the desk.** If they hit the desk edge, you end up sitting too far back and reaching forward for the keyboard.
- **Force your arms out wide.** If they're too far apart, your elbows flare out; too close, and they squeeze your hips.

The right armrests support you without changing how you'd naturally sit.

## Armrest types: 1D, 2D, 3D and 4D

Chair listings often describe armrests by the number of directions they adjust. The labels aren't always used consistently, so read the product description to see which adjustments you actually get.

| Type | Usually adjusts | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed | Nothing | Short sessions, or if the fixed height happens to suit you |
| 1D | Height | Most people; the one adjustment that matters most |
| 2D | Height plus width or pivot | People with narrower or broader frames |
| 3D | Height, width or pivot, and depth (forward/back) | Long typing sessions, pulling close to the desk |
| 4D | Height, width, depth and pivot | Shared chairs, hard-to-fit bodies, full-day use |

### Height

The most important adjustment. It lets you match the armrests to your elbow height and your desk height. If a chair only has one armrest adjustment, it should be this one.

### Width

Moves the armrests in or out. Useful if you have narrow shoulders and find standard armrests too far apart, or broad hips or shoulders and find them too close. Some chairs achieve this by sliding the pad sideways, others by moving the entire armrest.

### Depth

Slides the pad forward or back. Sliding back helps you pull close to the desk; sliding forward supports your forearms while you type.

### Pivot

Rotates the pad inward or outward. Angling the front of the pads slightly inward can support your forearms while typing, since your hands naturally come together on the keyboard.

## How to set armrest height correctly

The general rule: armrests should be at or just below your elbow height when your shoulders are relaxed.

1. **Set up your chair first.** Seat height so your feet are flat and your thighs are roughly level; backrest and lumbar support in place.
2. **Relax your shoulders** and let your arms hang by your sides.
3. **Bend your elbows** to about 90 degrees.
4. **Raise or lower the armrests** until they just touch the underside of your forearms, without lifting your shoulders.

If your shoulders rise when you rest on them, lower the armrests. If you have to lean to one side to reach them, raise them.

### How armrests and desk height work together

Ideally your armrests, elbows and desk (or keyboard tray) line up at about the same height. That way your forearms can move from armrest to desk without lifting or dropping.

- **Armrests higher than the desk?** They'll bump the desk edge and push you away. Lower them.
- **Armrests can't go low enough?** Choose a chair where they go lower, or make them removable.
- **Desk too high for your elbows?** Raise your chair and support your feet with a footrest, then adjust the armrests to your new elbow height.

## How to set armrest width

Your elbows should sit close to your sides, not flared out and not pinched in.

- With your arms relaxed at your sides, the armrests should sit just under your elbows.
- If you have to move your elbows outward to reach them, bring them in, if they adjust.
- If they press against your hips or thighs, move them out.

People with narrow shoulders often struggle most with wide armrests, so width adjustment is worth prioritizing if that's you.

## Other features that matter

- **Padding:** a soft or cushioned top is more comfortable for long sessions than hard plastic. Pressure on the forearm near the elbow can be uncomfortable over time.
- **Pad size:** very narrow pads concentrate pressure. Very long pads may hit the desk.
- **Stability:** armrests that wobble or shift when you lean on them are annoying and less supportive.
- **Locking:** some adjustable armrests move too easily. Firm detents or locks keep them where you set them.
- **Flip-up or removable armrests:** useful if you often need to pull right up to the desk or sit cross-legged.

## When to skip armrests

Armrests aren't essential for everyone. You might be better without them if:

- Your desk is low or you use a keyboard tray, and no armrest goes low enough.
- You're small and the armrests are too wide to use.
- You move around a lot, sit cross-legged or perch on the edge.
- You work at a tight desk where armrests constantly hit the edge.

If your current armrests get in the way and don't adjust, check whether they can be removed. Many chairs have armrests held on by a few screws under the seat.

## Free fixes for problem armrests

- **Too low:** wrap them with a folded towel or foam padding, held on with a strap or rubber bands.
- **Too hard:** add a soft cover or cushion.
- **Too high and fixed:** remove them, or raise your seat and use a footrest if your feet no longer reach the floor.
- **Too wide:** rest your forearms on the desk instead, with the desk at elbow height.

If none of these work and you're replacing the chair, [[office-chairs|compare office chairs with adjustable armrests]] and look for at least height adjustment, plus width if you're narrower or broader than average.

## Common mistakes

- **Setting armrests to the highest position** because it feels supportive. It usually means shrugged shoulders.
- **Leaning on armrests while typing.** Rest your forearms lightly or between typing; heavy leaning on the elbow can press on nerves around it.
- **Ignoring the desk.** Armrests and desk height need to work together.
- **Assuming 4D is always better.** More adjustment helps only if you set it up. A well-set 1D armrest beats a badly set 4D one.

A health note: this is general information. If you have persistent or severe shoulder, elbow or neck pain, or numbness or tingling in your arms or hands, see a doctor or physiotherapist.

## Quick checklist

- Height adjustment at minimum
- Armrests at or just below relaxed elbow height
- Low enough to slide under or line up with your desk
- Elbows close to your sides, not flared
- Width adjustment if you're narrow or broad
- Depth and pivot are a bonus for long typing sessions
- Cushioned, stable pads
- Removable if they get in the way
