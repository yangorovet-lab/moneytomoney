The most reliable way to improve posture while working from home is to change your setup so good posture becomes the easy default, then move often enough that you never hold one position for too long. Trying to "sit up straight" by willpower rarely lasts past lunch. If your screen is at eye level, your chair supports your lower back, and your keyboard sits at elbow height, your body settles into a better position on its own. Add regular movement breaks and a few simple exercises, and you've covered most of what matters.

## Why posture often gets worse at home

Home workspaces tend to be improvised. Common culprits:

- **Laptops** that force you to look down or reach up.
- **Kitchen tables** that are too high for comfortable typing.
- **Dining chairs** with no lower back support and no adjustment.
- **Couches and beds** that let you slump for hours.
- **Fewer natural breaks**, since there's no walk to a meeting room or coffee run.

The result is often a forward head, rounded shoulders, and a lower back that sags into the chair. None of these are dangerous in short bursts. The problem is holding them all day, every day.

## What "good posture" really means

There isn't one perfect position. A useful working definition is a **neutral, supported posture you can vary easily**:

- Head balanced over your shoulders, not jutting forward.
- Shoulders relaxed, not hunched toward your ears.
- Upper arms hanging close to your sides, elbows around 90 degrees.
- Wrists straight, not bent up or down.
- Lower back supported with its natural inward curve.
- Hips slightly higher than or level with your knees.
- Feet flat on the floor or a footrest.

The key word is "vary." Even a textbook posture becomes uncomfortable if you hold it for hours. The best posture is often described as your next one.

## Step 1: Fix your chair first

Your chair sets the foundation for everything else.

1. **Set seat height** so your feet rest flat and your thighs are roughly parallel to the floor.
2. **Sit all the way back** so your lower back touches the backrest.
3. **Support your lower back.** If the chair has adjustable lumbar support, position it at the small of your back, around belt height. If it doesn't, a rolled towel or small cushion works surprisingly well.
4. **Check seat depth.** You should be able to fit two to three fingers between the front edge of the seat and the back of your knees.
5. **Lower or remove armrests** if they push your shoulders up.

If your feet don't reach the floor after adjusting for desk height, use a sturdy box, a stack of books, or a footrest.

## Step 2: Raise your screen

A low screen is the main reason heads drift forward. Set it up so:

- The **top of the screen** is at or slightly below eye level.
- The screen is about **an arm's length away**.
- It's **directly in front of you**, not off to one side.

For laptops, raising the screen means you also need a separate keyboard and mouse. Books or a shoebox work as a free laptop riser. If you want something more permanent, [[laptop-stands|a laptop stand]] paired with an external keyboard is the usual fix.

If you wear progressive lenses and find yourself tipping your head back to read the screen, lower the monitor a little so you can read through the bottom part of your lenses without craning.

## Step 3: Bring your keyboard and mouse to you

- Keep the keyboard directly in front of you, close to the desk edge.
- Keep the mouse right next to the keyboard so you don't reach out to the side.
- Type with your elbows near your body and forearms roughly level.
- If the desk is too high, raise your chair and use a footrest rather than typing with shrugged shoulders.

Reaching for a mouse placed far to the right is a common hidden cause of one-sided shoulder and neck tension.

## Step 4: Move more than you think you need to

Setup gets you a good starting position. Movement keeps you from locking into it.

Simple rules that work for many people:

- **Change position at least every 30 minutes.** Stand up, stretch, refill water, or walk while on a phone call.
- **Take a longer break every hour or two**, even just five minutes away from the desk.
- **Alternate sitting and standing** if you have a way to do so, but don't swap all-day sitting for all-day standing.
- **Use natural triggers.** Stand for every call, stretch every time you send a large file, walk during the first minutes after lunch.

Setting a timer can help until breaks become a habit.

## Step 5: Add a few posture-friendly exercises

These are general, gentle movements that many people find helpful. Stop if anything causes pain.

| Exercise | How to do it | Helps with |
|---|---|---|
| Chin tucks | Sitting tall, gently draw your chin straight back, hold 3 to 5 seconds, repeat 10 times | Forward head posture |
| Shoulder blade squeezes | Pull shoulder blades back and down, hold 5 seconds, repeat 10 times | Rounded shoulders |
| Doorway chest stretch | Forearms on a doorframe, step forward gently until you feel a stretch, hold 20 to 30 seconds | Tight chest muscles |
| Seated spinal twist | Sit tall, rotate gently to each side, hold 15 seconds | Stiff mid-back |
| Hip flexor stretch | Half-kneel, shift hips forward gently, hold 20 to 30 seconds per side | Tight hips from sitting |
| Short walk | Two to five minutes | Everything |

Doing a few of these once or twice a day is more realistic than an elaborate routine you'll abandon.

## Step 6: Watch the "posture traps" at home

- **The couch afternoon.** Working from the sofa for a change of scenery is fine occasionally, but set a limit and prop the screen up.
- **Phone neck.** Looking down at your phone between tasks adds to the time your head spends bent forward. Hold it closer to eye level.
- **Leaning on one elbow.** Common during long calls; it twists your spine and loads one shoulder.
- **Crossing your legs for hours.** Switch sides or uncross regularly.
- **Perching on the front edge of the chair.** This usually means the desk is too far away or the chair is too deep. Move closer and use the backrest.

## Should you buy anything?

Many posture problems can be solved with free changes. Consider buying only once you've tried those:

- **A supportive, adjustable chair** if yours has no lumbar support, no height adjustment, or you sit for most of the day.
- **A laptop stand plus external keyboard and mouse** if you work on a laptop.
- **A monitor arm** if your screen can't be raised enough.
- **A footrest** if your feet don't reach the floor.
- **A sit-stand option** if you find it hard to take breaks.

Be wary of posture-correcting braces and gadgets that promise to "fix" posture. Anything that holds you rigidly in place does the opposite of what you want, which is easy, varied movement.

## When to see a professional

Some stiffness after a long day is common. Book an appointment with a doctor or physiotherapist if you have:

- Pain that persists for more than a few weeks or keeps getting worse.
- Numbness, tingling, or weakness in your arms, hands, or legs.
- Pain that wakes you at night.
- Headaches that are frequent or severe.

A physiotherapist can also give you exercises tailored to your body, which beats generic advice.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Feet flat, thighs roughly level, lower back supported
- [ ] Top of the screen at or slightly below eye level, arm's length away
- [ ] Keyboard and mouse close, elbows near 90 degrees
- [ ] Position change at least every 30 minutes
- [ ] A few chin tucks and shoulder squeezes each day
- [ ] Couch and bed work kept to short sessions

Better posture at home isn't about holding yourself stiffly upright. Set up your space so a good position is the natural one, then keep moving through the day.
