An external monitor is the better choice for most full-time home offices, because it gives you a larger screen at a comfortable height and distance, and you can still use the laptop as a second display. A laptop stand is the better choice if you're on a tight budget, short on desk space, move between locations, or only work from home part-time. Either way, you'll need a separate keyboard and mouse, because the whole point is to raise the screen to eye level, and you can't type comfortably on a keyboard that's up there too.

## The problem both options solve

A laptop on a desk has its screen well below eye level. To see it, you tilt your head down and round your shoulders, often for hours. Raising the screen so the top edge sits at or slightly below eye level, about an arm's length away, is the main fix.

There are two ways to get there:

1. **Lift the laptop** on a stand and use it as your screen.
2. **Add an external monitor** at the right height and use the laptop as a keyboard (not ideal), a second screen, or closed.

Both work. The better choice depends on your screen size, space, budget, and how you work.

| Factor | Laptop stand | External monitor |

| Upfront cost | Low | Moderate to high |
| Screen size | Your laptop's screen (often 13 to 16 inches) | Typically 24 to 32 inches |
| Desk space needed | Small | Larger footprint and depth |
| Portability | Excellent, many fold flat | Stays at the desk |

## When a laptop stand is the better choice

A stand makes sense if:

- **You're on a budget.** A stand plus a basic keyboard and mouse costs a fraction of a decent monitor.
- **Your desk is small or shallow.** A laptop on a stand takes up little space, and many desks under 24 inches deep feel cramped with a monitor.
- **You work in different places.** Folding stands travel easily between rooms, coworking spaces, and trips.
- **You mostly work in one window.** Writing, email, and calls work fine on a laptop screen.
- **Your laptop has a large screen** (15 or 16 inches), which narrows the gap with a small monitor.

### Things to know about stands

- **Height:** check that the stand lifts the screen high enough. The top of the screen should be at or slightly below eye level when you sit upright. Some fixed stands only lift a few inches.
- **Stability:** a stand that wobbles when you touch the screen gets annoying. Wider bases and solid materials help.
- **Cooling:** open designs let air flow under the laptop.
- **Distance:** because laptop screens are small, you may want the screen a bit closer than an arm's length. Then check that text is still readable without leaning in.

Free alternative: a stack of sturdy books or a box does the same job. Try it for a week before buying. If you like it, [[laptop-stands|compare adjustable laptop stands]] for something more stable and adjustable.

## When an external monitor is the better choice

A monitor makes sense if:

- **You work from home most days.** Over a full working week, a larger screen at a good height is worth the investment.
- **You use multiple windows.** Spreadsheets, coding, design, research, and video calls alongside documents are all easier on a bigger screen.
- **You have eye strain** from squinting at small text. A larger screen lets you use larger text without losing content.
- **You have the desk space.** Aim for a desk at least 24 inches deep so the monitor can sit at a comfortable distance.

### Things to know about monitors

- **Size:** 24 to 27 inches suits most home offices. Larger screens need more desk depth so you're not turning your head a lot.
- **Resolution:** higher resolution gives sharper text, especially on bigger screens.
- **Height:** many basic monitor stands don't adjust in height. You may need books under it or a monitor arm to get the top at eye level.
- **Connection:** check which ports your laptop has. A single-cable connection that also charges the laptop is the tidiest if your laptop and monitor support it.
- **Adjustability:** tilt, height, and swivel make it much easier to find a comfortable position.

## The best of both: use them together

Many people end up with this setup:

- **External monitor** directly in front of you as the main screen, top at or slightly below eye level.
- **Laptop on a stand** beside it at a similar height, used as a second screen for chat, email, or reference.
- **External keyboard and mouse** directly in front of you.

This gives you the most screen space and keeps both displays raised. If you use two screens, put the one you use most directly in front of you and the other to the side, angled toward you, rather than centering the gap between them.

## Don't forget the keyboard and mouse

Whichever you choose, a separate keyboard and mouse are essential once the screen is raised. Without them, you'd have to reach up to type, which strains your shoulders and wrists.

- Place the keyboard directly in front of you, close to the desk edge.
- Keep elbows around 90 degrees and shoulders relaxed.
- Put the mouse right next to the keyboard.

Budget for these when comparing options. A basic set is inexpensive, and they transform both setups.

## Cost and space at a glance

- **Minimal budget:** books under the laptop plus a basic keyboard and mouse.
- **Small budget:** laptop stand plus keyboard and mouse.
- **Moderate budget:** external monitor plus keyboard and mouse, with books or a riser to set the monitor height.
- **Larger budget:** monitor on an adjustable arm, laptop on a stand as a second screen, and a quality keyboard and mouse.

## Common mistakes

- **Raising the laptop but still typing on it.** This just moves the problem from your neck to your shoulders.
- **Setting a monitor too low.** Many monitor stands leave the screen below eye level; check and adjust.
- **Putting the monitor off to one side**, so you twist your neck all day.
- **Buying a large monitor for a shallow desk**, which puts it too close.
- **Ignoring glare.** Position either screen so windows are to the side, not behind or directly in front of it.

## Quick decision guide

- **Pick a laptop stand** if you work from home part-time, have a small desk, travel with your laptop, or want the cheapest effective fix.
- **Pick an external monitor** if you work from home most days, use several windows, strain to read small text, or do detailed visual work.
- **Pick both** if you want the most flexible, comfortable setup and have the space.

If you notice persistent neck, shoulder, or wrist pain, or numbness or tingling in your arms or hands even after adjusting your setup, see a doctor or physiotherapist.
