A windowless office needs three layers of light: bright, even overhead or ambient light to fill the room, a task lamp aimed at your desk, and a softer light behind or around your monitor so the screen isn't the brightest thing in view. Use cool-to-neutral white bulbs during the working day, a warmer tone in the evening, and position every light so it doesn't reflect off your screen. Done well, a room with no windows can be more comfortable to work in than one with harsh afternoon sun.

## Why windowless rooms feel draining

Without windows, a room usually has one ceiling fixture that was never meant to light a workspace. That leads to a few problems:

- **Too dim overall.** Your eyes work harder, and the room feels gloomy.
- **Strong contrast.** A bright monitor in a dark room makes your eyes adjust every time you look away from the screen.
- **Shadows on the desk.** A single ceiling light behind you casts your own shadow onto your papers and keyboard.
- **No sense of time.** Natural light changes through the day. Without it, some people find the day blurs and energy dips.

The goal is to replace what a window would provide: plenty of light, spread evenly, from more than one direction.

## Layer 1: Ambient light

Ambient light is the general light that fills the room. In a windowless office it needs to do more work than usual.

### What to aim for

- **Several sources instead of one.** A ceiling fixture plus a floor lamp or two spreads light and fills corners.
- **Indirect light.** Uplighter floor lamps bounce light off the ceiling and walls, which creates a soft, even glow similar to daylight from a window.
- **Bright enough to read comfortably anywhere in the room**, not just at the desk.

### Free and cheap fixes first

- **Swap the bulbs.** Many rooms are lit with low-output bulbs. Replacing them with brighter LED bulbs, within the fixture's rated limits, is the cheapest upgrade. Check the brightness in lumens on the packaging rather than watts.
- **Use light walls.** Pale, matte walls and ceilings reflect light around the room. Dark walls absorb it. If you can repaint, a light, matte finish makes every lamp work harder.
- **Add a mirror** on the wall facing a lamp to bounce light back into the room, keeping it out of your line of sight to the screen.
- **Move an existing floor lamp** from another room to test before buying anything.

## Layer 2: Task lighting

Task light illuminates the work surface: your keyboard, notes and anything you read on paper.

- Place the lamp on the side opposite your writing hand so your hand doesn't cast a shadow.
- Choose a lamp with an adjustable arm and head so you can aim it at the desk, not at the screen or your eyes.
- Look for a dimmer and adjustable color temperature. You'll want different settings for daytime work and evenings.
- A diffused or wide beam avoids a harsh hot spot on the desk.

If you mainly work on a screen and rarely read paper, a [[desk-lamps|dimmable LED desk lamp with an adjustable arm]] can double as both task and supplementary ambient light.

## Layer 3: Screen and bias lighting

The biggest eye-comfort problem in a windowless room is a bright monitor surrounded by darkness. Two things help.

### Monitor light bars

A light bar sits on top of the monitor and shines down onto the desk in front of the screen, with little light hitting the display itself. It lights your keyboard without adding glare and takes up no desk space, so on a cramped desk a [[monitor-light-bars|monitor light bar]] can replace a desk lamp entirely.

### Bias lighting

A soft light behind the monitor, shining onto the wall, reduces the contrast between the screen and the room. LED strips on the back of the monitor or a small lamp behind it both work. Keep it dimmer than the screen and use a neutral white.

### Match the screen to the room

- Lower the monitor brightness until it roughly matches the brightness of a white sheet of paper on your desk.
- Enable your operating system's automatic night mode or a warmer color setting in the evening.

## Color temperature: what to choose

Color temperature is measured in kelvin (K). Lower numbers are warmer and more yellow; higher numbers are cooler and bluer.

| Color temperature | Look | Best use in a windowless office |
|---|---|---|
| Around 2700-3000K | Warm white, cozy | Evening, winding down, bias lighting after hours |
| Around 3500-4000K | Neutral white | All-day general work, most comfortable default |
| Around 5000-6500K | Cool white, daylight-like | Focused daytime work, detailed tasks |

Neutral white is a safe default. Cooler light can feel more alert and closer to daylight during the day, while warm light in the evening feels more relaxed. Bulbs and lamps with adjustable color temperature let you shift through the day, which helps replace the natural change you'd get from a window.

### Color rendering

Also check the color rendering index (CRI) on the packaging. Higher numbers show colors more naturally, and a CRI of 90 or above is a commonly recommended level for workspaces. It matters most for design, photography and video calls, where you want skin tones to look natural.

## Positioning: avoid glare and shadows

- **Don't place a lamp behind you** aimed toward the screen; it reflects off the display.
- **Don't place bright light directly behind your monitor** facing you. Your eyes will squint against it.
- **Position overhead lights to the side** of your desk rather than directly behind your chair, if you can move the fixture or the desk.
- **Tilt the screen** slightly to remove any remaining reflections.
- **Check from your seat.** Sit in your working position and look for bright spots or reflections on the screen and on glossy surfaces.

## Lighting for video calls

Windowless rooms can make you look shadowy on camera. For calls:

- Put a soft light in front of you, slightly above eye level, such as a desk lamp bounced off the wall behind the monitor or a small light next to the screen.
- Avoid a single overhead light, which creates shadows under the eyes.
- Keep the background reasonably lit so you don't look like you're sitting in a cave.

## Don't forget real daylight

No lamp fully replaces time outdoors. If you work in a windowless room:

- Take breaks outside, even briefly, especially in the morning.
- Take calls on a walk when you can.
- Look at something far away every so often to rest your focusing muscles.

If you have headaches, persistent eye strain, or low mood that seems tied to the season or your workspace, talk to a doctor or eye care professional. Lighting can make a room more comfortable, but it isn't a treatment.

## Sample setup

1. Brighter LED bulbs in the ceiling fixture, neutral white.
2. One uplighter floor lamp in a corner to bounce light off the ceiling.
3. An adjustable desk lamp on the side opposite your writing hand, or a light bar on the monitor.
4. A dim, neutral bias light behind the monitor.
5. Monitor brightness matched to the room.
6. A warm setting on the lamps for evening work.

## Quick checklist

- Use at least three light sources, not one ceiling fixture.
- Prefer indirect light that bounces off walls and ceiling.
- Add a task light aimed at the desk, not at the screen.
- Reduce contrast with bias lighting and a dimmer monitor.
- Use neutral or cool white by day, warm in the evening.
- Check for glare and shadows from your seat.
- Get outside for real daylight during breaks.
