To set up a monitor above a laptop, put the laptop on a stand or a stack of books so it's closer to eye level, then mount the monitor on an arm or a tall riser so its bottom edge sits just above the laptop's top edge. Place the screen you use most at the most comfortable height, tilt the lower screen back a little, and tell your computer that the monitor is above the laptop in display settings so the mouse moves between them naturally. A vertical stack saves desk width, but only works well if you avoid craning your neck up to the top screen.

## Is stacking the right layout for you?

Stacked screens make sense when:

- Your desk is narrow and there isn't room for side-by-side screens.
- You use the second screen for reference: chat, email, documentation, a video call.
- You want to keep one screen directly in front of you instead of turning your head sideways.

It's a weaker fit when:

- You need both screens equally all day. Looking up for hours is harder on the neck than turning a little to the side.
- You wear progressive or bifocal lenses. These make you tilt your head back to see higher screens through the lower part of the lens.
- The monitor is very large. A tall screen stacked above a laptop can put its top well above your eye line.

## Decide which screen is your main one

This matters more than anything else. Your neck is happiest looking straight ahead or slightly down.

- **If the monitor is your main screen**, put the monitor at the comfortable height (top at or slightly below eye level) and let the laptop sit lower, used for secondary windows. This is the more ergonomic option.
- **If the laptop is your main screen**, raise it so its top is near eye level, and accept that the monitor above will be used only for glances.

Most people who stack end up doing the first: the monitor becomes the main screen at a good height, and the laptop screen below becomes a secondary display.

## Step-by-step setup

### 1. Position the laptop

- Put the laptop directly in front of you, centered on your body.
- Raise it a few inches with a stand or books so the screen tilt and the keyboard aren't fighting each other. If you raise it, use an external keyboard and mouse.
- Tilt the screen back slightly, so you look at it straight on rather than at an angle.

A [[laptop-stands|sturdy laptop stand]] makes this easier than books, especially if it lets you adjust height and slides the laptop closer to you.

### 2. Mount the monitor above

You have three main options:

| Option | Pros | Cons | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monitor arm (clamp or grommet) | Very adjustable, frees desk space, easy to reposition | Needs a sturdy desk edge; check weight and size limits | Most stacked setups |
| Tall monitor riser or shelf | No clamping, stable | Fixed height; can put the screen too high or too low | Desks that can't take a clamp |
| Monitor's own stand at maximum height | Free | Rarely tall enough to clear a laptop | Small laptops and tall stands |

For stacking, a monitor arm is usually the best choice. Look for one with enough vertical travel to raise the monitor's bottom edge above your laptop screen, a tilt adjustment, and a weight rating above your monitor's weight. Gas-spring arms make fine height adjustments easy. You can [[monitor-arms|browse gas-spring monitor arms]] and compare their height range with the gap you need to clear.

### 3. Set the heights

- **Main screen:** top at or slightly below eye level.
- **Gap:** keep the two screens as close together as you can. The smaller the gap, the less you move your head.
- **Distance:** both screens around an arm's length away. The monitor can sit a little farther back if it's larger.
- **Tilt:** tilt the top monitor down slightly toward you, and the lower laptop screen back, so both face your eyes.

A quick check: sit back in your normal posture and glance between screens. You should be moving your eyes and tilting your head only slightly. If you tip your chin up to read the top screen, it's too high.

### 4. Configure display settings

Your operating system needs to know where the screens are, or the mouse cursor will jump off the wrong edge.

- **Windows:** Settings, System, Display. Drag the monitor icon so it sits above the laptop icon. Line up their centers.
- **macOS:** System Settings, Displays, Arrange. Drag the monitor above the built-in display.

Also check:

- **Scaling:** laptops and monitors often have different pixel densities. Adjust scaling so text looks roughly the same size on both.
- **Main display:** set which screen shows the taskbar or menu bar and opens new windows.
- **Brightness:** match brightness roughly between the two, so your eyes aren't adjusting each time you look up or down.

## Free and cheap ways to try it first

Before buying an arm, you can test the layout:

- Stack sturdy books or a box behind the laptop and set the monitor on top, with the laptop directly in front.
- Lower the laptop fully flat on the desk and raise the monitor on its stand as much as possible.
- Use the laptop closed and only the monitor for a day, to see how much you actually need two screens.

Spend a few days with a temporary setup. If you find you rarely look up at the top screen, or your neck complains, a side-by-side layout might suit you better.

## Common mistakes

- **Main screen on top.** If you mostly work on the monitor, don't let it drift high above eye level.
- **Big gap between screens.** Leaving several inches of space means bigger head movements every time you switch.
- **Laptop keyboard as your only keyboard.** Once the laptop is raised, typing on it pushes your shoulders up. Use a separate keyboard and mouse.
- **Wrong arrangement in settings.** If the cursor exits the side of the screen instead of the top, fix the arrangement.
- **Glare.** Two screens mean two chances for reflections from a window or lamp. Angle them away from bright light.
- **Ignoring lens type.** Progressive lens wearers often do better with the main screen lower than usual, or with a side-by-side layout.

## Neck comfort tips for stacked screens

- Keep the screen you use most in the most comfortable position.
- Put reference material on the top screen only if you check it occasionally.
- Move windows between screens rather than your head, when a task takes a while.
- Take regular short breaks and look away at something across the room every so often.

If you develop neck pain, headaches, or tingling in your arms or hands that doesn't settle with setup changes, check in with a doctor or physiotherapist.

## Quick checklist

- Decide which screen is your main one
- Main screen top at or slightly below eye level
- Screens stacked closely with a minimal gap
- Both screens roughly an arm's length away, tilted toward your eyes
- Laptop raised and paired with an external keyboard and mouse
- Monitor positioned above the laptop in display settings
- Scaling and brightness matched between screens
