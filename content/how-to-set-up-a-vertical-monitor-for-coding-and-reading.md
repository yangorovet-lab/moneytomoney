To set up a vertical monitor, rotate it 90 degrees on a stand or arm that supports portrait mode, tell your operating system to rotate the display, and then position it so your eyes land about a third of the way down the screen rather than at the very top. Place it at roughly an arm's length, angle it slightly toward you if it sits beside a main screen, and bump up text scaling so long lines of code or documents stay readable. Portrait mode shines for anything tall and narrow: source files, logs, documentation, PDFs and long articles.

## Why portrait works so well for code and text

Most code and prose is far taller than it is wide. On a landscape screen, you see a wide band of mostly empty space and a short slice of the file. Turn the same monitor on its side and you get many more lines at once.

That changes how you work:

- **More context without scrolling.** You can see a whole function, or most of a class, and keep your place.
- **Better reading line length.** A portrait screen's narrower width keeps text lines closer to a comfortable length, especially for PDFs and web pages.
- **Natural fit for secondary content.** Docs, chat, terminal output and logs stack vertically and rarely need width.

The trade-off: wide content like spreadsheets, timelines, video editing and side-by-side diffs gets cramped. That's why many people pair one vertical monitor with a landscape main screen.

## Which monitors work best vertically

Not every monitor is a good portrait candidate. Check these before you rotate:

| Factor | What to look for | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Size | Around 24 to 27 inches | Larger panels get very tall in portrait, forcing you to tilt your head |
| Panel type | Wide viewing angles (IPS-type panels are usually good) | Some panels shift color or contrast when viewed from the side, which is how portrait orientation presents them |
| Resolution | Enough pixel density for sharp text | Code at small sizes needs crisp rendering |
| Stand | Built-in pivot, or a VESA mount | Without pivot or VESA, you can't rotate safely |
| Bezels | Thin, if placing beside another screen | Reduces the visual gap between screens |

Ultrawide monitors are a poor fit for portrait. The result is extremely tall and narrow, and the top will sit far above a comfortable eye line.

## Step 1: Rotate the hardware

### If your stand has a pivot

Many monitor stands pivot. Before rotating:

1. **Raise the stand to its highest position** so the corner won't hit the desk as it turns.
2. **Tilt the screen back slightly**, if the manual recommends it.
3. **Rotate in the direction the manual shows.** Some stands only turn one way.
4. **Check the cables.** Make sure power and video cables have slack and aren't pulled tight or pinched at the port.

### If your stand doesn't pivot

Check the back of the monitor for a VESA pattern (four screw holes in a square). If it's there, a monitor arm or a separate pivoting stand can hold it in portrait. A good arm also lets you fine-tune height and angle, which matters more for a tall portrait screen. If you're shopping, [[monitor-arms|browse monitor arms that rotate to portrait]] and confirm the weight and VESA size match your screen.

Never prop a monitor on its side without a proper mount. It's unstable, and the ports and panel edges aren't designed to bear weight.

## Step 2: Rotate the display in software

- **Windows:** open display settings, select the rotated monitor, and set display orientation to Portrait (or Portrait flipped, depending on which way you turned it).
- **macOS:** open Displays in System Settings, select the monitor, and set Rotation to 90 or 270 degrees.
- **Linux:** most desktop environments have a rotation option in display settings. You can also use your display server's command-line tools.

While you're there, check scaling. A portrait monitor often benefits from slightly larger text scaling so you can read comfortably without leaning in. Also check the arrangement diagram so your mouse moves naturally between screens. Drag the portrait display's icon so its vertical position matches its physical position relative to your main screen.

## Step 3: Get the height and distance right

A portrait screen is much taller than a landscape one, so standard monitor-height advice needs a small adjustment.

- **Aim your eye line at the upper third of the screen.** The usual guidance of putting the top of the screen at or slightly below eye level doesn't quite work when the screen is very tall, because the bottom would end up too low. Instead, place it so your natural gaze falls a bit below the top edge and you only need to glance down for the lower part.
- **Avoid tilting your head back.** If you catch yourself looking up at the top lines, lower the screen.
- **Use a slight backward tilt** so the whole surface is closer to perpendicular to your line of sight.
- **Distance: about an arm's length.** Increase text size instead of pulling the screen closer.

If you wear progressive lenses, you may find the top of a tall screen blurry. Lower the monitor more than usual, or ask your eye doctor about computer-specific glasses.

## Step 4: Position it relative to your main screen

Most people use a vertical monitor as a second screen. Placement depends on how you split your time.

### Main screen plus a side portrait screen

- Put your main (landscape) screen directly in front of you.
- Put the portrait screen to the side of your dominant eye or the side you look at more.
- Angle the portrait screen inward so it faces you instead of sitting flat in line.
- Align the upper parts of both screens roughly, so your gaze doesn't jump up and down.

### Portrait as the main screen

If you mostly code or write, a portrait screen can be your primary display. Center it in front of you and put any secondary screen off to the side.

### Two portrait screens

Two portrait monitors side by side act like a wide, tall workspace with a bezel in the middle. This works for code on one and docs on the other. Center the pair so the bezel lines up with your nose.

## Step 5: Set up your apps for portrait

The hardware only helps if your software uses the space.

**For coding:**

- Keep the editor full-width on the portrait screen with a moderate font size.
- Move the file tree and terminal elsewhere, or collapse them, so code gets the width.
- Consider a line-length guide in your editor so lines don't run off the edge.
- Put logs, test output, or a terminal on the portrait screen while the main editor stays landscape, if that suits you better.

**For reading:**

- Use single-page view with "fit to width" for PDFs.
- Use a reader mode in the browser for long articles.
- Keep chat, email or task lists stacked vertically in one column.

**Window management:** Tiling tools or built-in snap features make it easy to split a portrait screen into top and bottom halves, for example docs on top and a terminal below.

## Lighting and eye comfort

A tall screen reflects more of the room. Face it away from windows or put windows to the side. Match screen brightness to the room so it doesn't glow or look dim. A small task light or a [[monitor-light-bars|monitor light bar]] can light your desk without adding glare to the screen.

Take regular breaks from close focus. Looking at something far away for a short while every so often helps many people with tired eyes. If you have persistent eye strain, headaches, or neck pain, see an eye doctor or physiotherapist.

## Common mistakes

- **Using a screen that's too big.** A 32-inch monitor in portrait puts the top far above eye level.
- **Setting the top edge at eye level.** On a tall screen that sends the bottom too low. Aim for the upper third instead.
- **Forgetting to angle it.** A side screen set flat forces you to turn your head.
- **Tight cables.** Rotation can pull on ports. Leave slack.
- **Relying on a flimsy stand.** Wobble is more noticeable on a tall screen.
- **Skipping scaling.** Tiny text leads to leaning forward, which undoes the ergonomic benefit.

## Quick checklist

- Monitor is 24 to 27 inches with wide viewing angles
- Stand pivots or the monitor is on a VESA arm
- OS rotation and screen arrangement are correct
- Eyes land on the upper third of the screen
- Roughly an arm's length away, angled toward you
- Text scaled up enough to read without leaning
- Editor, docs and logs arranged to use the tall layout
