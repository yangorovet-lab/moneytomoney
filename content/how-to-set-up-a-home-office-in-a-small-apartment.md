You don't need a spare room to work comfortably from a small apartment. You need a spot about 4 feet wide and 5 feet deep (desk plus room for your chair), a screen at eye level, a chair that supports you, and a way to put work out of sight when the day is done. Start by choosing the right corner, pick a desk sized to the space rather than the other way around, and build upward instead of outward.

This guide walks through each decision in the order that saves the most space and money.

## Step 1: Measure what you actually need

Before looking at furniture, figure out your minimum footprint:

- **Desk width:** about 36 to 48 inches fits a laptop plus one monitor. A 30-inch desk can work for a laptop alone with an external keyboard.
- **Desk depth:** 20 to 24 inches is enough for a laptop, or for a monitor on an arm. Shallower desks force the screen too close unless you mount it.
- **Chair space:** leave about 3 feet behind the desk edge so you can sit down and push back without hitting a wall or bed.
- **Legroom:** make sure there's nothing under the desk blocking your knees and feet.

Mark the footprint with painter's tape on the floor. Walk around it for a day. If it blocks a doorway, a closet or the path to the kitchen, you'll resent it within a week.

## Step 2: Choose the spot

In a small apartment, location matters more than furniture. Compare the usual candidates:

| Spot | Pros | Cons | Works best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Living room corner | Usually the most space and light | Work is always visible; TV and household noise | Full-time remote workers |
| Bedroom | Quiet, door closes | Harder to switch off; can hurt sleep routines | People who need privacy for calls |
| Beside a window | Great daylight | Glare if the window is in front of or behind the screen | Anyone who can angle the desk sideways |
| Hallway or nook | Uses dead space | Often narrow and dim; little depth | Part-time or laptop-only work |
| Closet ("cloffice") | Doors hide everything | Tight depth, poor airflow, needs lighting | Small studios with a spare closet |
| Kitchen or dining table | No new furniture | Wrong heights, daily setup and pack-down | Short-term or occasional work |

A few rules that apply almost everywhere:

- **Put windows to the side of your screen,** not behind it or behind you, to avoid glare.
- **Face into the room or a wall that looks presentable** if you take video calls.
- **Stay near an outlet.** Long extension cords across walkways are a tripping hazard.
- **Avoid facing your bed** if you can. A visual boundary between work and rest helps.

## Step 3: Pick a desk that fits the space

Some formats work especially well in small apartments:

- **Narrow writing desk.** Simple and light. Look for a shallow depth and no bulky drawers under the work surface.
- **Wall-mounted or fold-down desk.** Disappears when closed. Check that the height works for typing, since many are mounted at a standard height that may not suit you.
- **Corner desk.** Uses a spot that's usually wasted. A small L-shape can hold a monitor on one side and paperwork on the other.
- **Standing desk frame with a short top.** Height-adjustable desks come in compact sizes, and changing height lets the desk suit different people or double as a counter. Shop by minimum top size and frame width. You can [[standing-desks|compare compact standing desks]] if you want the option to stand.
- **Existing furniture.** A console table, dresser top or dining table can work if the height is right. Most people type comfortably at a surface around elbow height when seated, which is often lower than a dining table.

Skip a big desk "for later." A cramped room with a huge desk feels worse than a small room with a small desk.

## Step 4: Get the ergonomics right in a small footprint

Small spaces often push people toward the couch or bed. It's fine occasionally, but for daily work, set up the basics:

- **Screen:** top at or slightly below eye level, about an arm's length away.
- **Elbows:** around 90 degrees, shoulders relaxed, wrists straight.
- **Feet:** flat on the floor or a footrest.
- **Back:** supported, especially in the lower back.

### Laptop-only setups

Working on a laptop alone forces you to choose between a good screen height and a good keyboard height. The fix: raise the laptop on a stack of books or a box, and use a separate keyboard and mouse. A folding [[laptop-stands|laptop stand]] is a compact, easy-to-store version of the same idea.

### Chairs for small spaces

A good task chair takes up about as much floor as a dining chair once it's pushed in. Choose one with:

- Adjustable seat height.
- Lumbar support (or add a rolled towel or small pillow).
- No arms, or arms that flip up or lower, so it slides under the desk.
- A compact base if floor space is tight.

If you share the chair with dining or other uses, a firm cushion and a lumbar pillow can make a regular chair workable for a while.

## Step 5: Build up, not out

Floor space is the scarcest resource. Use the vertical space above and around the desk:

- **Wall shelves** above the desk for books, supplies and a small printer.
- **Pegboards or rail systems** for headphones, cables and notebooks.
- **A monitor arm** to lift the screen off the desk, freeing the entire surface underneath. It also lets you push the screen back on a shallow desk.
- **Under-desk drawers or a rolling cart** that tucks away when not needed.
- **Hooks on the side of the desk** for a bag or headset.

## Step 6: Tame cables

Cables make small spaces look chaotic. Keep it simple:

- Use one power strip mounted under or behind the desk.
- Bundle cables with reusable ties or velcro straps.
- Run cables along a desk leg into the strip.
- An under-desk [[cable-trays|cable tray]] holds the strip and adapters off the floor, which is especially helpful if you need to vacuum around the desk often.

## Step 7: Light it well

Small apartments often have one overhead light and a window. That's rarely enough.

- Put your desk sideways to the window to use daylight without glare.
- Add a desk lamp on the side opposite your writing hand.
- Use warmer, lower light in the evening if the desk is in your bedroom, so the space feels less like an office at night.

## Step 8: Create a work/home boundary

This is the part people overlook, and it matters more in a studio or one-bedroom.

- **Close it down at the end of the day.** Fold the desk, close the closet doors, or throw a cloth over the monitor.
- **Use a folding screen, curtain or bookshelf** as a room divider to separate the desk from the living area.
- **Keep work items in one bin** that goes in a closet after hours.
- **Set a start and end routine,** like a short walk before work and after, to replace the commute.

## Common mistakes

- **Buying furniture before measuring.** Measure the spot, the doorways and the path from the front door.
- **Working from the couch every day.** It's comfortable short term and hard on your neck and back long term.
- **Putting the desk facing a window.** Pretty view, lots of glare.
- **Too little light.** A dim corner leads to squinting and fatigue.
- **Letting work sprawl.** Without a defined zone, papers and devices spread across the apartment.

## Budget priorities

If you're furnishing from scratch, spend in this order:

1. **A chair that fits you.** You sit in it all day.
2. **A way to raise your screen,** such as books, a laptop stand or a monitor arm.
3. **An external keyboard and mouse** if you use a laptop.
4. **A desk the right size and height.**
5. **Lighting and storage.**

Everything else can wait.

## When to get help

If you have back, neck or wrist pain that doesn't improve after adjusting your setup, or you notice numbness or tingling, get it checked by a doctor or physiotherapist.

## Quick checklist

- Tape out a footprint of roughly 4 by 5 feet before buying anything.
- Choose a spot with the window to the side and an outlet nearby.
- Pick a desk sized for your space, not for "someday."
- Screen at eye level, elbows around 90 degrees, feet flat, back supported.
- Build storage upward; lift the monitor off the desk if space is tight.
- Tame cables with one strip and a tray.
- Close the office at the end of the day.
