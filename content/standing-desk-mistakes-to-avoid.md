The biggest standing desk mistakes are setting the desk to the wrong height, standing too long too soon, forgetting to move the monitor when you switch positions, and standing still on a hard floor. On the buying side, people most often pick a desk with too narrow a height range, underestimate how much weight it will carry, and forget about cables. Every one of these is easy to avoid once you know it's coming.

## Mistakes when using a standing desk

### 1. Setting the desk to screen height instead of elbow height

It's tempting to raise the desk until the monitor looks right. That usually puts the keyboard at chest level, so your shoulders rise and your wrists bend back.

**Do this instead:** set the desk so the keyboard sits at or just below elbow height, with elbows at about 90 degrees and shoulders relaxed. Then adjust the monitor separately so its top is at or slightly below eye level.

### 2. Not moving the monitor when you switch

A monitor on its own stand at a comfortable sitting height is usually fine when standing too, since it moves with the desk. The problem comes when you change your chair height, add a converter, or use a laptop.

**Do this instead:** check screen height each time you switch. If the numbers change often, a height-adjustable monitor arm makes fine tuning quick.

### 3. Standing all day from day one

A new desk invites enthusiasm. Several hours of standing on the first day often leads to sore feet, tight calves and an aching lower back.

**Do this instead:** start with 15 to 30 minute standing sessions a few times a day and build up gradually over a few weeks. Sit down before discomfort starts.

### 4. Treating standing as the goal

Standing still for hours isn't the aim. The benefit of a sit-stand desk is variety: changing position and moving more through the day.

**Do this instead:** switch between sitting and standing regularly, roughly every 30 to 60 minutes, and add short walks. Take calls on your feet.

### 5. Standing on a hard floor in socks or bare feet

Hardwood, tile and concrete wear out feet quickly.

**Do this instead:** wear supportive shoes, or stand on a cushioned surface. A folded yoga mat is a decent free start. For regular use, an [[anti-fatigue-mats|anti-fatigue mat with beveled edges]] is one of the most useful accessories.

### 6. Locking knees and leaning on one hip

Standing rigidly, knees locked, weight sunk into one hip, is tiring and loads your lower back unevenly.

**Do this instead:** keep a slight bend in your knees, shift your weight often, and put one foot up on a low box or footrest, switching sides every few minutes.

### 7. Leaning on the desk

Leaning your forearms or elbows on the desk to take weight off your feet usually means you're tired, the desk is too low, or both.

**Do this instead:** check that the desk is at elbow height, and sit down when you're tired.

### 8. Forgetting to lower the desk all the way when you sit

People sometimes sit down without lowering the desk fully, leaving the keyboard a couple of inches too high.

**Do this instead:** use memory presets if your desk has them. Save your sitting and standing heights once, then use the same button every time.

## Mistakes when buying a standing desk

### 9. Ignoring the height range

A desk that doesn't go low enough for sitting or high enough for standing won't fit you. Shorter people need a low minimum; taller people need a high maximum.

**Do this instead:** measure your seated and standing elbow heights (in the shoes you'll wear) and add the thickness of your keyboard. Make sure both numbers fall within the desk's range, with some headroom.

### 10. Underestimating weight

Monitors, arms, a desktop computer, speakers and books add up quickly. A frame near its limit may move slowly, strain or wobble.

**Do this instead:** add up your equipment's weight and choose a desk with a load capacity well above it.

### 11. Choosing a frame that wobbles at standing height

Almost every desk is steadier when low. At standing height, lighter frames can sway when you type. Single-motor desks and thinner legs are often more prone to this, though design matters as much as motor count.

**Do this instead:** look at leg design, crossbars and frame stability specs. Check the return policy in case the wobble bothers you.

### 12. Picking the wrong desktop size

Too shallow and your monitor ends up too close. Too wide for your room and the desk blocks a walkway or window.

**Do this instead:** measure your space. A deeper desktop gives you room to keep the screen about an arm's length away.

### 13. Forgetting about cables

Cables that are fine at sitting height can get yanked, snagged or unplugged when the desk rises.

**Do this instead:** leave enough slack for full travel, route cables along one leg, and mount a power strip under the desktop so it travels with the desk. An under-desk [[cable-trays|cable management tray]] keeps everything tidy and moving together.

### 14. Buying a desk when a converter would do (or vice versa)

Converters sit on top of an existing desk. They're cheaper and don't require replacing furniture, but they raise the keyboard height even when "sitting" and can be heavy to lift. Full standing desks adjust the whole surface.

**Do this instead:** consider a converter if you're renting or on a tight budget and your current desk is at a good sitting height. Choose a full standing desk if you have space and want a single surface for everything.

## Mistakes with the whole workstation

### 15. Keeping the laptop as your only screen

Standing makes laptop use worse: you look down even farther.

**Do this instead:** raise the laptop so its screen is near eye level and use an external keyboard and mouse.

### 16. Keeping an old chair that doesn't fit the new routine

A standing desk doesn't replace a good chair. You'll still sit for most of the day.

**Do this instead:** make sure your chair supports you properly at your sitting height, with feet flat and back supported.

### 17. Ignoring the space around you

Standing tends to involve more movement. A chair behind you, a bin under your feet, or a mat that slides around can get in the way.

**Do this instead:** clear the standing area and make sure the chair can roll out of the way easily.

## Quick reference table

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Desk set to screen height | Keyboard at elbow height, monitor separate |
| Standing for hours immediately | Build up from short sessions |
| Standing still | Shift weight, alternate positions |
| Hard floor, no shoes | Supportive shoes or a mat |
| Locked knees, leaning | Soft knees, foot up on a low box |
| Wrong height range | Measure seated and standing elbow heights first |
| Wobble at standing height | Check frame design and return policy |
| Cables snagging | Slack, routing, under-desk tray |

## When to get help

Some foot or leg soreness in the first week or two is normal. Pain that persists, gets worse, or comes with swelling, numbness or tingling should be checked by a doctor or physiotherapist. The same goes if you have existing foot, knee, hip, back or circulation problems before you start standing more.

## Bottom line

Set height by your elbows, not your screen. Build up standing time slowly. Keep moving and protect your feet. When buying, check height range, weight capacity, stability and cable routing before anything else.
