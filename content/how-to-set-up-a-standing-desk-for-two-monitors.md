Start by deciding which screen you look at most. If you use both equally, center the pair so the seam between them sits directly in front of your nose. If one is your main screen, put it straight ahead and angle the second one off to the side. Then set both screens to the same height, with the tops at or slightly below eye level and about an arm's length away, angle them inward into a gentle curve, and mount them on a dual monitor arm or stable stands. Finally, leave enough cable slack for the desk's full height range and check the desk's weight capacity. Do that and your setup works whether you're sitting or standing.

## Before you start: three things to check

### 1. Weight capacity

Add up everything the desk will carry: both monitors, the arm or stands, a laptop, speakers, lamp and anything else. Compare that with the desk's rated lifting capacity. Staying well under the limit is kinder to the motors and reduces strain on the frame.

### 2. Desk width and depth

Two 24-inch monitors side by side need roughly 110 cm (about 43 inches) of width, and two 27-inch monitors need more. Depth matters too: you want the screens about an arm's length from your eyes, which is hard on a shallow desk. Monitor arms let you push screens back or pull them forward beyond the desk edge.

### 3. Stability

Standing desks tend to wobble more at standing height, and two monitors on arms concentrate weight and leverage at the back edge. Desks with a crossbar, sturdy legs, and a solid frame usually handle this better. A wide desktop also spreads the load.

## Choose your layout

| How you use the screens | Best layout |
|---|---|
| One main screen, one for reference (chat, email, docs) | Main screen centered, second angled to the side of your dominant eye or dominant work |
| Both used about equally | Both screens symmetrical, seam centered in front of you |
| Coding or reading long documents | Main landscape centered, second in portrait to the side |
| Laptop plus one monitor | Monitor centered, laptop on a stand beside it at a similar height |

A common mistake is centering two screens when you use one far more than the other. You end up turning your head to the same side all day. Put your main screen in front of you instead.

## Step-by-step setup

### Step 1: Set your standing and sitting keyboard heights

Monitor height depends on your eye level, which depends on your working posture. Set the desk first.

- **Standing:** raise the desk until your elbows are bent about 90 degrees with your forearms roughly level and shoulders relaxed.
- **Sitting:** adjust your chair so your feet are flat and thighs roughly level, then lower the desk to the same elbow height.
- **Save both heights** in the desk's memory presets if it has them.

### Step 2: Mount the monitors

You have three main options.

**Dual monitor arm (usually best for standing desks)**

A dual arm clamps to the desk and holds both screens. Advantages:

- Frees desk space
- Lets you fine-tune height, angle, depth and rotation for each screen
- Moves with the desk, so screens stay in position as the desk rises

Check that the arm's weight range matches each monitor, that your monitors have VESA mounting holes, and that the clamp suits your desktop thickness. On a thin or particleboard desktop, a reinforcement plate spreads the clamp load. Gas-spring arms are easier to adjust; if you're shopping, you can [[monitor-arms|browse dual monitor arms]] and filter by weight capacity.

**Two separate arms**

Two single arms give more placement flexibility (for example, one screen higher or in portrait), but take two clamp positions.

**Built-in stands**

Monitor stands work fine if they adjust in height and tilt. If they don't, stack sturdy books or a riser under one to match heights. Stands take up more desk space and can rock more on a moving desk.

### Step 3: Set the height

- **Top of each screen at or slightly below eye level** when you sit or stand straight.
- **Match the two tops** so your eyes move across in a straight line. If your monitors are different sizes, match the upper edge of the viewable area rather than the frame.
- **Tilt slightly back** so the screens face your eyes.

If you wear progressive lenses, set the screens a bit lower so you don't tilt your head back to read through the bottom of your lenses.

### Step 4: Set the distance

About an arm's length from your eyes is a good starting point. Larger screens may need to be a bit farther. If you lean in to read, increase text size rather than pulling the screens closer.

### Step 5: Angle them inward

Angle the screens so they form a gentle curve around you. For a centered pair, each screen turns inward slightly. For a main-plus-side layout, keep the main screen square to you and angle the side one inward. The goal is that you turn your eyes and a little bit of your head, not your shoulders.

### Step 6: Configure the operating system

- Arrange the displays in settings to match their physical positions, including height differences, so the mouse moves naturally.
- Set your main display as primary so the taskbar or menu bar sits there.
- Match scaling so text looks similar on both screens.

## Cables: leave slack for the full height range

A standing desk moves up and down a long way. Every cable from the monitors must be able to move with it.

1. **Put a power strip on or under the desk** so monitor power cords plug into something that moves with the desk.
2. **Route video cables along the monitor arm** using the arm's clips or channels.
3. **Bundle cables under the desk in a tray,** then run one cable bundle down to the wall.
4. **Raise the desk to maximum height** and check that nothing is pulled tight.
5. **Lower it fully** and check that nothing gets pinched.

A [[cable-trays|cable tray under the desk]] makes this much tidier. Free alternative: a sturdy basket screwed to the underside, or binder clips along the back edge.

If you use a laptop with a dock, put the dock on the desk too, not on the floor.

## Reduce wobble

Two monitors exaggerate desk wobble, and shaking screens are tiring to look at.

- **Tighten all frame bolts.** They loosen over time.
- **Level the feet** using the adjustable glides.
- **Move the arm clamp** toward a leg rather than the middle of the desk's back edge.
- **Push screens closer to the arm pole** to reduce leverage.
- **Keep the desk away from the wall's baseboard** so it doesn't bump.
- **Type gently.** Heavy typing on a tall desk transmits movement to the screens.

If wobble is still bad at standing height, a desk with a stronger frame or crossbar may be the real fix.

## Sitting vs standing: does the monitor setup need to change?

If your monitors are mounted to the desk, they move with it, so the screen-to-keyboard relationship stays the same. The only thing that may differ is your posture. Many people slouch a bit more when sitting and stand taller when standing, so you may want to fine-tune the tilt. Monitor arms make that a few-second adjustment.

If your monitors sit on a separate surface (a wall mount or a shelf behind the desk), they won't move when the desk does. That setup only works for one position.

## Common mistakes

- **Exceeding the desk's lifting capacity.**
- **Centering both screens when you use one more.**
- **Screens at different heights.**
- **Cables too short** for maximum desk height.
- **Mounting a heavy arm on a thin desktop** without reinforcement.
- **Screens too close,** which leads to turning your head more and more eye strain.

## Quick checklist

- Total weight under the desk's lifting capacity
- Keyboard height set for sitting and standing, saved as presets
- Main screen centered, or seam centered for equal use
- Tops of both screens at or slightly below eye level and matched
- About an arm's length away, angled into a gentle curve
- Cables have slack at max height and don't pinch at the lowest
- Frame bolts tight, arm clamped near a leg
