Get a monitor light bar if your desk is mostly keyboard, mouse, and screen, space is tight, and glare on your monitor bothers you. Get a desk lamp if you read paper, write by hand, sketch, or need light spread over a wider area, or if your monitor is curved, very thin at the top, or mounted in a way that won't hold a bar. Many people who do mostly screen work are happier with a light bar; people who split time between screen and paper usually want a lamp. Either one works best as a supplement to decent room lighting, not a replacement for it.

## How each one works

**A monitor light bar** is a slim, horizontal light that clamps or rests on the top edge of your monitor. It shines down and slightly forward onto the area directly in front of the screen, usually your keyboard and a strip of desk. Most are designed with an asymmetric beam, meaning the light is aimed away from the screen so it doesn't reflect back at you.

**A desk lamp** stands on the desk or clamps to its edge, with an arm you can position. It can light a larger area, and you can aim it wherever you need, including onto papers to the side of your keyboard.

## Side-by-side comparison

| Factor | Monitor light bar | Desk lamp |
|---|---|---|
| Desk space used | None | A base or clamp, plus arm clearance |
| Area lit | Narrow strip in front of the screen | Wider, adjustable area |
| Screen glare | Low when designed with an asymmetric beam | Depends on placement; easy to cause glare |
| Paper and handwriting | Adequate for small notes directly in front | Better for reading, writing, drawing |
| Flexibility | Fixed position | Can be moved and re-aimed |
| Compatibility | Needs a suitable monitor top edge | Works with any setup |
| Power | Often powered by a USB port | Wall outlet or USB |
| Look | Minimal, almost invisible | More visible, can be decorative |

## Why glare is the deciding factor for many people

When light hits your screen, it washes out contrast and makes you work harder to read. A desk lamp placed behind or beside your monitor and aimed toward it will create reflections, especially on glossy screens. People often try to fix this by turning the lamp off and working in dim light, which creates a different problem: a bright screen in a dark room.

Light bars largely avoid this by design. Because they sit on top of the monitor and aim downward and away from the screen, the light reaches your desk but not the display. This is their main advantage, and it is why they have become popular for screen-heavy work.

You can get a similar result with a desk lamp if you position it carefully (see below). It just takes more attention.

## Who should get a monitor light bar

A light bar is a good fit if most of these apply:

- You work almost entirely on a computer.
- Your desk is small or cluttered and you don't want another object on it.
- You notice glare or reflections on your screen from your current lighting.
- You work in the evening in a room with dim overhead light.
- Your monitor has a flat, reasonably thick top edge, or you've confirmed the bar you're considering fits your monitor's shape.

If this sounds like you, [[monitor-light-bars|browse monitor light bars]] and pay attention to features like an asymmetric beam, adjustable brightness, adjustable color temperature, and a clamp that fits your monitor.

### When a light bar may not work

- **Curved monitors.** Some bars are designed for curved screens; many are not. Check compatibility.
- **Very thin monitors or ones with a webcam on top.** The clamp may not sit securely, or it may block the camera.
- **Laptop-only setups.** Laptop lids are usually too thin and light to support a bar well.
- **Multiple screens.** A single bar lights the area in front of one screen. If you use two screens side by side, you may need one on each, or a lamp.

## Who should get a desk lamp

A lamp is the better choice if:

- You regularly read printed documents, write by hand, or draw.
- You want to light a wider area, such as both a keyboard and a notebook to the side.
- Your monitor setup doesn't support a light bar.
- You like being able to move the light for different tasks.
- You want the lamp to double as general room lighting in a small space.

When shopping for [[desk-lamps|desk lamps for a home office]], look for an adjustable arm or head, a diffused light source that doesn't create harsh hot spots, adjustable brightness, and a warm-to-cool color range. A base or clamp that fits your desk matters too.

## How to position a desk lamp without glare

If you go with a lamp, placement makes a big difference.

1. **Put it to the side, not behind the monitor.** Aim it at your work surface, not at the screen.
2. **Place it on the opposite side of your writing hand** if you write on paper, so your hand doesn't cast a shadow. Right-handed people usually put it on the left.
3. **Keep the light source below eye level or shielded** so you're not looking into a bare bulb.
4. **Angle the head down** so light falls onto the desk, then check your screen for reflections.
5. **Adjust brightness** so the desk is comfortably lit but not much brighter than the screen.

## Don't forget the rest of the room

Neither a light bar nor a lamp should be your only source of light. Working in a dark room with only a small pool of light at your desk creates a strong contrast between the bright screen and dark surroundings, which many people find tiring.

Free and low-cost fixes to try first:

- Turn on overhead or ambient lighting when you work in the evening.
- Place your desk so windows are to your side rather than directly in front of or behind your screen. A window behind you reflects in your screen; a window behind the screen makes you squint.
- Use curtains or blinds to control harsh daylight.
- Lower your screen brightness at night so it's closer to the room brightness.
- Place a floor lamp or small lamp behind your monitor, aimed at the wall, to soften the contrast. This is sometimes called bias lighting.

## Color temperature and brightness

Both light bars and many lamps let you adjust color temperature, from warm (more yellow) to cool (more blue-white).

- **Cooler light** tends to feel brighter and more alert, and many people like it during the day.
- **Warmer light** tends to feel softer and more relaxing, and many people prefer it in the evening.

There is no one right setting. Pick what feels comfortable and makes text easy to read. A good rule of thumb is to match your desk light roughly to the rest of the room, so you don't have a patch of cold white light in a warm-lit room or vice versa.

## Can you use both?

Yes, and some people do. A light bar handles the keyboard area without glare, and a lamp to the side covers paperwork when needed. If you only buy one, start with the one that fits your main task, and see whether you actually miss the other.

## Common mistakes

- **Aiming a lamp at the screen** or placing it directly behind the monitor.
- **Buying a light bar without checking your monitor's top edge** or webcam position.
- **Relying on one small light in a dark room** in the evening.
- **Choosing maximum brightness** instead of adjusting to the task and time of day.
- **Ignoring the window.** Natural light placement often matters more than any lamp.

## Bottom line

For screen-only work in a small space, a monitor light bar is usually the cleaner, glare-free choice. For mixed work with paper, or if your screen can't hold a bar, choose a positionable desk lamp and place it to the side. In both cases, keep some ambient light on in the room.

If you regularly get headaches, eye strain, or blurry vision after screen work even with good lighting, have your eyes checked by an optometrist or doctor. Sometimes the fix is an updated prescription or computer-specific glasses rather than a new light.
