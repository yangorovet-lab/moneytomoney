If you wear progressive lenses, set your monitor lower than standard advice suggests, tilt it back, and bring it close enough that you can read it through the middle of your lenses without lifting your chin. For most progressive wearers, that means the top of the screen sits noticeably below eye level, often a few inches lower than for someone in single-vision glasses. If you still find yourself tipping your head back or hunting for the sharp spot, a pair of dedicated computer glasses is usually the real fix.

Here's why progressives make screens awkward, and how to set things up step by step.

## Why progressive lenses and monitors clash

A progressive lens has three zones blended together:

- **Top:** distance vision, for driving or looking across a room.
- **Middle:** intermediate vision, roughly arm's length, which is where a monitor sits.
- **Bottom:** near vision, for reading a phone or book.

The trouble is that the intermediate zone is a narrow band in the middle, and it's also narrow from side to side. The edges of the lens are blurry by design. So to read a screen at arm's length, you have to look through a small window in the lower-middle part of the lens.

With a monitor at the usual height, most wearers do one of two things: tilt the head back to drop the screen into the middle zone, or lean forward to use the reading zone. Both strain the neck over a long day. Tilting the head back in particular loads the muscles at the base of the skull and top of the shoulders, which is where many progressive wearers report tension.

The goal of the setup is simple: let your head stay level and comfortable while your eyes naturally look down into the intermediate part of the lens.

## Step 1: Start with your chair and posture

Everything else depends on your eye height, so set this first:

1. Adjust your chair so your feet rest flat on the floor and your thighs are roughly level.
2. Sit back against the backrest, with your head balanced over your shoulders rather than pushed forward.
3. Look straight ahead with your head level. That's your eye-level line.

## Step 2: Lower the monitor

Standard advice puts the top of the screen at or slightly below eye level. For progressive wearers, go lower.

**How to find your height:**

1. Sit in your normal position with your head level.
2. Without moving your head, lower your gaze until text on the screen looks sharp through your lenses.
3. Adjust the monitor so the area you look at most, usually the upper-middle part of the screen, falls where your eyes naturally land.

In many cases this puts the top of the screen a few inches below eye level, and the center of the screen well below. If your monitor stand won't go that low, remove the stand and use the base of a monitor arm, or set the screen directly on the desk if the stand is the only thing holding it too high.

**Free fixes if your screen is too high:**

- Remove any books or risers under the monitor.
- Lower your desk if it's adjustable, then raise your chair to keep your arms comfortable, adding a footrest or box if your feet no longer reach the floor.
- If you use a laptop with an external monitor, put the external screen on the desk surface and keep the laptop as a secondary display.

## Step 3: Tilt the screen back

Tilting the top of the monitor away from you by 10 to 20 degrees helps a lot. It keeps the whole screen at a more even distance from your eyes as you look down, so the top and bottom edges stay in the same focus zone.

Most monitor stands allow some tilt. If yours doesn't tilt enough, or won't go low enough, a monitor arm usually solves both problems at once. ## Step 4: Set the right distance

The intermediate zone of most progressive lenses is designed for something around arm's length, but the sharpest distance varies by prescription.

- Start with the screen roughly at arm's length, about 20 to 30 inches (50 to 75 cm).
- Move it closer or farther in small steps until text looks sharp without you leaning in.
- If you catch yourself leaning forward, the screen is too far or the text is too small.

A tape measure is useful here. Write down the distance that works; you'll want it later if you order computer glasses.

## Step 5: Increase text size before straining

Making text bigger widens the range of distances where it looks clear. Try:

- Operating system display scaling at 125 or 150 percent.
- Browser zoom (Ctrl or Cmd plus the plus key).
- Larger default font sizes in your email and document apps.

This costs nothing and often reduces the urge to lean in.

## Step 6: Deal with side-to-side blur

Because the intermediate zone is narrow horizontally, looking at the edges of a wide screen with your eyes alone can blur text. Progressive wearers tend to do better when they turn their head slightly to follow what they're reading, keeping the eyes centered in the lens.

That has practical consequences:

- **One monitor is easier than two.** If you use two screens, put the one you use most directly in front of you, and angle the second so you turn your head, not just your eyes.
- **Ultra-wide screens can be tiring** for progressive wearers. If you already own one, push it back a little and keep important windows near the center.
- **Center your main work window** instead of pinning it to one side.

## Setup summary

| Setting | Typical advice | For progressive wearers |
|---|---|---|
| Monitor top | At or slightly below eye level | A few inches below eye level, sometimes more |
| Tilt | Slight | Tilt back roughly 10 to 20 degrees |
| Distance | About arm's length | Arm's length, fine-tuned for sharpness |
| Text size | Default | Scaled up 125 to 150 percent if needed |
| Dual monitors | Side by side | Main screen centered; turn head to the second |

## When computer glasses make more sense

Setup changes help many people, but they have limits. If you spend most of your day at a screen, ask your eye care professional about glasses made for computer work. Common options include:

- **Intermediate single-vision lenses:** set for your screen distance. The whole lens is sharp at that range, with no hunting for a sweet spot. The trade-off is that things across the room will look blurry.
- **Office or occupational progressives:** these devote most of the lens to intermediate and near vision, with a wider intermediate zone than regular progressives. They let you see the screen, your keyboard and papers.
- **Adjusted everyday progressives:** sometimes a different lens design or fitting height gives a wider intermediate zone.

When you book the appointment, bring the distance from your eyes to your screen and mention how many hours you spend at a computer. That information helps them recommend the right design. With dedicated computer glasses, you can often raise the monitor back toward the standard height, since you no longer need to look down through the bottom of the lens.

## Laptops and progressive lenses

Laptop screens sit low, which actually suits progressive wearers better than a tall monitor does. The problem is that you end up looking down steeply and hunching. A good compromise:

- Raise the laptop a little on a stand or a stack of books, but not as high as you would with single-vision glasses.
- Use an external keyboard and mouse so you're not reaching.
- Tilt the screen back until the text looks sharp.

## Lighting and glare

Reflections on the screen make it harder to find the sharp zone. Place the monitor so windows are to the side rather than behind you or directly behind the screen. An anti-reflective coating on your lenses also helps; ask about it at your next eye exam.

## Signs your setup still isn't right

- You tilt your head back to read.
- You move your head up and down to find sharp text.
- Your neck or upper shoulders ache by the end of the day.
- You get frequent headaches or tired eyes at the screen.

If adjustments don't fix these, see your optometrist or ophthalmologist. Persistent headaches, eye strain or neck pain, and any numbness or tingling in your arms or hands, are worth a check-up with a doctor or physiotherapist too.

## Quick checklist

- Chair set first: feet flat, sitting back, head level
- Monitor lowered until you can read without lifting your chin
- Screen tilted back about 10 to 20 degrees
- Distance adjusted until text is sharp without leaning
- Text scaled up if needed
- Main screen centered; turn your head for a second screen
- Ask about computer glasses if you work at a screen most of the day
