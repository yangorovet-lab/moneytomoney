If your desk is too high, raise your chair until your elbows are level with the desk surface, then put a footrest or a stack of books under your feet so they stay flat. That single change fixes the problem for most people and costs nothing. If you'd need an unreasonably tall footrest to make it work, lower the work surface itself instead, with a keyboard tray, shorter legs or a height-adjustable desk.

## How to tell your desk is too high

Sit in your normal working position with your hands on the keyboard and check for these signs:

- Your shoulders creep up toward your ears while you type.
- Your elbows sit below the desk surface, so your wrists bend upward to reach the keys.
- Your forearms rest on the hard front edge of the desk.
- You get tension across the top of your shoulders or the side of your neck by the end of the day.
- The monitor feels too high even on its lowest setting, so you tip your head back.

A quick test: relax your arms at your sides, bend your elbows to about 90 degrees and look at where your forearms land. They should be level with the desk surface or slightly above it. If the desk edge hits your forearm halfway up, it's too high for your current chair setting.

Most standard desks are around 29 to 30 inches tall. That height suits people on the taller side. If you're shorter than average, or you have a short torso, a standard desk can easily sit a couple of inches higher than your elbows.

## Fix 1: Raise your chair (free)

This is the first thing to try, and it usually works.

1. Raise the seat until your forearms are level with the desk surface when your elbows are bent about 90 degrees.
2. Keep your shoulders relaxed and down.
3. Sit all the way back so your lower back touches the backrest.

If your chair doesn't go high enough, a firm cushion on the seat can add an inch or two. Pick something that doesn't squash flat after an hour.

## Fix 2: Support your feet (free or cheap)

Raising the chair usually lifts your feet off the floor. Dangling feet put pressure on the back of your thighs and pull you forward off the backrest, so fill the gap:

- A stack of hardcover books, taped together so it doesn't slide.
- A sturdy box filled with paper.
- A couple of large ring binders laid flat for a gentle slope.

Once you know the height that works, you can replace the books with an [[footrests|adjustable footrest with a non-slip top]]. Aim for knees at roughly hip height and feet flat.

## Fix 3: Add a keyboard tray

If raising your chair would put your feet on a tower of books, lower the keyboard instead of raising yourself. An under-desk keyboard tray drops the keyboard and mouse a few inches below the desk surface, which is often exactly the gap you need.

Things to check before choosing one:

- **Clearance.** A tray takes up knee room. Make sure your thighs still fit underneath it.
- **Space for the mouse.** A tray that only fits a keyboard just moves the problem to your mouse arm.
- **Mounting.** Some trays need a desk with no front drawer or support bar in the way.
- **Adjustable height and tilt.** A flat or slightly negative tilt (front edge higher than the back) keeps your wrists straighter than a keyboard tilted up on its feet.

Also fold away the little feet on the back of your keyboard. They raise the keys and bend your wrists further upward.

## Fix 4: Shorten the desk legs

This works for simple wooden or metal desks with straight legs, and it's permanent, so measure carefully.

1. Find your ideal work height: sit with your feet flat and elbows at 90 degrees, then measure from the floor to the underside of your forearm.
2. Subtract the desk top's thickness to get the leg length you need.
3. Check that your thighs will still fit under the desk, including any drawer or apron.

Some desks have screw-in leg levelers or legs that come in replaceable lengths, which avoids cutting. Don't cut the legs of a desk you rent, share or might sell.

## Fix 5: Lower the monitor too

A high desk often means a high screen. The top of the monitor should be at or slightly below eye level. If the stand won't go lower:

- Remove the stand and use a VESA mount if the monitor supports it.
- Use a [[monitor-arms|monitor arm that can drop the screen lower than its stand]].
- For a laptop, use an external monitor or raise yourself with the chair rather than lowering the screen.

## Fix 6: Switch to an adjustable desk

If you're furnishing a room from scratch or sharing a desk with someone of a different height, an adjustable desk solves the problem outright. Check the lowest height before buying. Many adjustable desks bottom out around the height of a standard desk, which doesn't help a shorter person at all. Look for a low minimum height and a range that covers both sitting and standing for you.

## Fix 7: Use a lower table or desk

Sometimes the simplest answer is a different piece of furniture. Tables sold for writing or kids' study, or desks built to a lower spec, can be a couple of inches shorter than standard. Measure your ideal height before shopping and bring a tape measure.

## Which fix should you choose?

| Your situation | Best fix | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Desk is 1-2 inches too high | Raise chair and add books under feet | Free |
| Desk is 2-4 inches too high | Raise chair plus footrest, or a keyboard tray | Low |
| Desk is more than 4 inches too high | Keyboard tray, shorter legs or a lower desk | Low to medium |
| You share the desk with a taller person | Adjustable footrest and chair, or an adjustable desk | Low to high |
| Monitor is also too high | Monitor arm or VESA mount | Low to medium |
| You're buying a new desk anyway | Adjustable desk with a low minimum height | Higher |

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Keeping the chair low and reaching up.** Shrugged shoulders all day lead to neck and shoulder tension.
- **Resting your wrists on the desk edge.** A hard edge pressing into your forearm or wrist is uncomfortable and puts pressure on the underside of the wrist.
- **Propping the keyboard on its feet.** This makes the wrist angle worse on a high desk.
- **Forgetting your feet after raising the chair.** Solving your arm position by creating dangling feet swaps one problem for another.
- **Buying a keyboard tray without checking knee room.** Measure the space between your thighs and the underside of the desk first.

## A quick setup order

When you rebuild your workspace, do it in this order so each change doesn't undo the last:

1. Chair height to match the desk (elbows level with the surface).
2. Feet supported with a footrest or books.
3. Seat depth and lower back support checked.
4. Keyboard and mouse close, directly in front of you.
5. Monitor about an arm's length away, top edge at or slightly below eye level.

If you still have shoulder, neck or wrist pain after adjusting everything, or you notice numbness or tingling in your hands, see a doctor or physiotherapist. Furniture changes help with comfort, but they don't replace a proper assessment.

## Bottom line

A desk that's too high is one of the most common setup problems and one of the easiest to fix. Raise your chair, support your feet, and see how it feels for a few days. If you need more than a few inches of foot support, lower the work surface instead with a keyboard tray, shorter legs or a desk that goes lower.
