Most people don't need a headrest on an office chair for typing and focused computer work, because when you sit upright to type, your head shouldn't be touching it anyway. A headrest becomes useful if you lean back often, such as during calls, reading, thinking breaks, or while watching presentations, or if you're tall and want support for your upper back and neck when reclining. If you mostly work upright at a keyboard, spend your money on good lumbar support and adjustability instead.

## What a headrest actually does

A headrest supports the weight of your head when you **recline**. That's the key point. When you sit upright to type, your head is balanced over your shoulders and your spine carries it. A headrest positioned correctly sits a little behind your head in that position, not pressing on it.

When you lean back, your head moves behind your center of gravity and your neck muscles have to work to hold it up. A headrest takes over that job, letting the neck muscles relax.

So the real question isn't "Do I need a headrest?" but "How much time do I spend reclined at my desk?"

## Who benefits from a headrest

A headrest is worth having if you:

- **Recline for calls or meetings.** Many people lean back during long video or phone calls. Without a headrest, the neck holds the head for the whole call.
- **Read long documents on screen or paper** while sitting back.
- **Take short rest breaks in the chair**, leaning back to reset without getting up.
- **Watch video content for work**, like training, demos, or reviews.
- **Are tall.** On many chairs the backrest ends below the shoulder blades of taller users. A headrest (or a taller back) provides support where the backrest stops.
- **Have neck discomfort** that eases when your head is supported. Discuss persistent neck pain with a doctor or physiotherapist, but a supportive recline can make breaks more restful.

## Who probably doesn't need one

You can skip a headrest if you:

- Sit mostly upright and type or use a mouse for most of the day.
- Rarely use the chair's recline function.
- Use a standing desk for a good part of the day.
- Are shorter than average and the headrest would sit too high to reach your head anyway.
- Find that a headrest pushes your head forward. This happens on some chairs and is worse than no headrest at all.

## Headrest vs no headrest at a glance

| Your situation | Headrest? | What to prioritize instead |
|---|---|---|
| Typing upright most of the day | Optional | Lumbar support, seat depth, armrests |
| Frequent calls leaning back | Yes | Adjustable headrest, good recline lock |
| Tall (backrest ends below shoulders) | Often yes | High-back chair or adjustable headrest |
| Neck discomfort when reclining | Often yes | Adjustable height and angle |
| Short user, headrest can't lower enough | No | Mid-back chair that fits your frame |
| Shared chair, different users | Only if adjustable | Wide adjustment range |

## The problem with fixed headrests

A headrest that doesn't adjust can be worse than none. Everybody's torso length and head position are different. If a fixed headrest sits too low, it presses into the base of your skull or upper neck. If it's too far forward, it nudges your head into a forward position, which is exactly the posture you're trying to avoid.

If you're choosing a chair with a headrest, look for:

- **Height adjustment** so it lines up with the back of your head or the upper neck curve.
- **Angle or depth adjustment** so it can move forward or back to meet your head when reclined, without pushing it forward when upright.
- **Removability**, so you can take it off if it's in the way.
- **A comfortable, supportive shape**, curved to cradle the back of the head or neck rather than a flat pad.

## How to set up a headrest correctly

1. **Adjust the rest of the chair first.** Set seat height, seat depth, lumbar support, and armrests before touching the headrest.
2. **Sit upright in your normal typing position.** The headrest should be just behind your head, with a small gap, or lightly touching without pushing.
3. **Set the height** so the support lines up with the back of your head, around the level of your ears or slightly below. Some people prefer it cradling the curve at the top of the neck. Either is fine as long as it doesn't push your chin down.
4. **Recline and test.** Lean back to your usual reclined angle. Your head should rest into the headrest without your chin tipping toward your chest.
5. **Fine-tune the angle.** If your head feels pushed forward when reclined, move the headrest back or tilt it.

## What matters more than a headrest

If your chair budget is limited, these features usually matter more for daily comfort:

- **Adjustable lumbar support** that fits the small of your back.
- **Seat height** that lets your feet sit flat.
- **Seat depth adjustment** so there are two to three fingers of space behind your knees.
- **Adjustable armrests** that let your shoulders relax.
- **A recline with tension control and a lock**, so you can lean back and still feel supported.

## Free fixes if your chair lacks a headrest

- **Recline less, stand more.** Instead of leaning back in your chair during calls, stand or walk if you can.
- **Rest against a wall.** For reading breaks, a comfortable chair against a wall or a sofa with a cushion behind the head does the job.
- **Small cushion or rolled towel.** Placed behind the upper neck when you recline, it can give temporary support. Remove it when you sit up to type so it doesn't push your head forward.
- **Adjust the recline.** Many chairs let you lock the backrest more upright, which reduces the need for head support.

## Aftermarket headrests: worth it?

Clip-on and add-on headrests exist for chairs that don't have one. They can work, but check:

- **Compatibility** with your chair's backrest shape and thickness.
- **Stability.** Some attachments wobble or shift when you lean on them.
- **Adjustability.** A fixed add-on has the same problem as a fixed built-in headrest.

If you want a headrest mainly because the chair is too short for your back, an add-on may not fix it. A chair with a taller backrest often solves the root problem better.

## Common mistakes

- **Leaning your head on the headrest while typing.** This tilts the head back and pulls you away from the keyboard. Keep the headrest for reclining.
- **Setting it too low**, so it presses into your neck and pushes your head forward.
- **Buying a chair for its headrest** while ignoring lumbar support and seat fit.
- **Keeping a headrest that bothers you.** If it's not adjustable and it's in the way, remove it if you can.

## Bottom line

A headrest is helpful if you spend meaningful time leaning back, are tall, or want support for your neck during breaks. It's not essential for upright typing. If you do get one, make sure it adjusts in height and angle, and set it so it supports your head when you recline without pushing it forward when you work. If you have neck pain that sticks around, numbness, or tingling in your arms, get it checked by a doctor or physiotherapist.
