You can make a kitchen chair far more comfortable for a workday by fixing four things: seat height relative to the table, support for your lower back, padding under your sit bones, and somewhere solid for your feet. Most of that costs nothing. A folded towel, a firm cushion, and a stack of books can turn a dining chair from "painful by lunch" into "fine for a few hours." It will never match a good adjustable office chair, but it can get surprisingly close for part-time remote work.

## Why kitchen chairs feel so bad after an hour

Dining chairs are built for a meal, not an eight-hour stretch of typing and staring at one spot. The usual problems are:

- **Flat, hard seat.** All your weight lands on two small bony points, so you shift constantly.
- **No lumbar curve.** The backrest is flat or slatted, so your lower back rounds and you slide into a slump.
- **Fixed height.** Kitchen tables are usually around 29 to 30 inches tall, and the chair is set for eating, not typing. Your elbows often end up below the table surface, so your shoulders hike up.
- **Deep or shallow seat.** A seat that's too deep pushes you away from the backrest; one that's too shallow leaves your thighs unsupported.

Each fix below targets one of these.

## Step 1: Get the height right first

Height is the fix that matters most, and it drives everything else.

Sit with your hands on the keyboard. Your elbows should be at roughly 90 degrees or slightly more open, with forearms close to level and shoulders relaxed. If the keyboard sits higher than your elbows, you need to raise yourself.

**How to raise your seat for free:**

- A firm, folded blanket or a couple of folded bath towels adds 1 to 3 inches.
- A firm sofa cushion or a dense throw pillow can add 2 to 4 inches, though soft ones compress fast.
- Avoid stacking loose items you can slide off. Anything you add should stay put when you shift your weight.

Once you're higher, check your feet. If they no longer rest flat on the floor, go to Step 3.

### A quick height check

| What you notice | What it usually means | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Shoulders creep up while typing | Table is too high for you | Raise the seat |
| Feet dangle or only toes touch | Seat is now too high for the floor | Add a footrest |
| Thighs pressed against underside of table | Seat too high for the table's apron | Lower the cushion or sit farther back |
| Wrists bent upward to reach keys | Keyboard above elbow height | Raise the seat or use a lower surface |

## Step 2: Add lower back support

Your lower spine has a natural inward curve. A flat kitchen chair back doesn't support it, so after a while you slump and the curve reverses. That is the most common source of the dull, end-of-day lower back ache.

**Free option: the rolled towel.** Roll a bath towel tightly into a cylinder about 3 to 5 inches thick. Place it horizontally in the small of your back, roughly at belt height or just above. Secure it with a couple of rubber bands or hair ties so it keeps its shape. Adjust the thickness until it feels supportive but doesn't push you forward.

**Other free options:**

A small folded throw pillow or a rolled-up sweater also works. If the chair has slats or a gap above the seat, tie the roll to the backrest with a scarf so it doesn't slip through.

**If you want to buy something:** a lumbar pillow with a strap is a small, inexpensive upgrade. Look for firm memory foam or a fairly dense filling, an adjustable strap, and a removable, washable cover. Very soft pillows flatten and stop doing their job.

## Step 3: Support your feet

Once you've raised your seat, your feet may no longer rest flat. Dangling feet put pressure on the backs of your thighs and make you perch on the front edge of the seat, which undoes the back support you just added.

Your goal: feet flat, knees at roughly hip height or slightly lower, thighs close to level.

**Free footrest ideas:**

- A sturdy box filled with paper so it doesn't crush.
- A stack of hardcover books, taped together so they don't slide.
- A low kitchen step stool or a yoga block.

If you're going to keep working from this chair for months, a purpose-made footrest is worth considering. An angled or rocking top lets you shift your ankles, which helps circulation. You can [[footrests|compare adjustable under-desk footrests]] and look for one with a height range that covers the gap between your feet and the floor.

## Step 4: Pad the seat properly

A hard seat forces you to fidget. The goal of padding is to spread your weight over a larger area, not to sink in.

**What works:**

- **Firm foam or memory foam seat cushions** that keep their shape. You want something that compresses a little but doesn't bottom out.
- **Coccyx cutout cushions** (a U-shaped gap at the back) can help if you feel pressure on your tailbone.
- **Wedge cushions** that are slightly thicker at the back tilt your pelvis forward a little, which makes it easier to keep your lower back's natural curve.

**What doesn't work well:**

- Very soft pillows, which flatten within an hour.
- Thick cushions that raise you so much you need a footrest and lose your back support at the same time.
- Anything slippery that makes you slide forward.

A good rule: after adding a cushion, redo the height check from Step 1.

## Step 5: Fix seat depth

Sit all the way back against the backrest (or your lumbar roll). You should be able to fit two to four fingers between the front edge of the seat and the back of your knees.

- **Seat too deep:** add a firm pillow or folded blanket behind your back to bring you forward.
- **Seat too shallow:** harder to fix. A slightly deeper cushion helps a little, and a footrest takes weight off your thighs.

## Step 6: Set up the screen and keyboard around the chair

A comfortable chair still feels bad if you're craning at a laptop. Kitchen chairs make this worse because you already can't recline.

- **Screen height:** the top of the screen should sit at or slightly below eye level, about an arm's length away. Stack books under a laptop until it's there.
- **Separate keyboard and mouse:** once the laptop is raised, you need an external keyboard and mouse at elbow height. Even an inexpensive wired set works.
- **Distance:** pull the chair close enough that you're not reaching forward. Your upper arms should hang roughly by your sides.

If you'd rather not balance a laptop on a book stack, a folding [[laptop-stands|laptop stand with adjustable height]] is one of the most useful cheap upgrades for a kitchen table setup.

## Step 7: Move more than you would in a proper chair

Even with every fix above, a kitchen chair offers less support than an adjustable office chair. You can make up for part of that by moving more often.

- Stand up every 30 to 45 minutes, even for a minute.
- Take calls standing or walking when you can.
- Sit back against the lumbar roll for reading, and sit tall and closer to the table for typing.

## Common mistakes

- **Stacking cushions until the seat is soft and tall.** You end up unstable and need to re-fix everything else.
- **Putting lumbar support too low.** It should sit in the curve of the lower back, not at the tailbone.
- **Ignoring the feet.** Dangling feet are one of the fastest ways to end up perched on the seat's edge.
- **Buying accessories before trying the free fixes.** Try towels, books and pillows for a few days so you know what you actually need.

## When to stop patching and buy a real chair

Kitchen chair fixes make sense if you work from home a couple of days a week or you're still figuring out your setup. Consider an office chair if:

- You sit for most of a full workday, five days a week.
- You keep adjusting cushions and rolls every hour.
- You still have discomfort after a week or two of a properly set up kitchen chair.
- More than one person shares the chair and needs different heights.

When you do shop, prioritize seat height adjustment, adjustable lumbar support, seat depth adjustment and adjustable armrests. You can [[office-chairs|browse office chairs with adjustable lumbar support]] and filter by those features rather than looks.

If you have back pain that persists, gets worse, or comes with numbness or tingling in your legs, have it checked by a doctor or physiotherapist rather than relying on setup changes alone.

## Quick checklist

- Elbows at roughly 90 degrees with forearms close to level
- Firm cushion that raises you without sinking
- Rolled towel or lumbar pillow at belt height
- Feet flat on the floor or a footrest
- Two to four fingers of space behind your knees
- Screen top at or slightly below eye level, about an arm's length away
- External keyboard and mouse at elbow height
- A short standing break every 30 to 45 minutes
