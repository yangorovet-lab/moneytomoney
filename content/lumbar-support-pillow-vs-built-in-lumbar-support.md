If your current chair fits you reasonably well and only lacks support in the lower back, a lumbar support pillow is the cheaper and perfectly good fix. If your chair is also the wrong size, doesn't adjust, or you're buying a new one anyway, built-in adjustable lumbar support is the better long-term choice because it stays in place and can be tuned to your spine. The key question isn't pillow versus chair. It's whether the support sits in the right spot, at the right depth, every time you sit down.

Here's how to tell which one your situation calls for.

## What lumbar support is supposed to do

Your lower back has a natural inward curve. When you sit for a long time without support, the pelvis tends to roll backward, that curve flattens, and you slump. Many people then feel tension or aching in the lower back by the afternoon.

Good lumbar support fills the gap between your lower back and the backrest, so keeping that gentle curve takes no effort. It should feel like a light, even contact, not a hard push. Too much support can be as uncomfortable as none, because it forces the back into an exaggerated arch.

Note that this is general information. If you have back pain that is severe, getting worse, or comes with numbness, tingling or weakness in the legs, see a doctor or physiotherapist before changing your setup.

## Try the free version first

Before spending anything, roll up a bath towel or a small hand towel into a cylinder about 3 to 4 inches (8 to 10 cm) across. Sit all the way back in your chair and place it at the small of your back, roughly at belt level or just above.

Use it for a few days and adjust:

- **Too firm or thick?** Unroll it a bit.
- **Slipping down?** Tuck it behind a strap, or use a rubber band or tape to hold the roll's shape.
- **Feels right?** You now know roughly how much support you like and where. That tells you what to buy.

Plenty of people find the towel does the job and never buy anything.

## Lumbar support pillows: pros and cons

A lumbar pillow is a shaped cushion, usually memory foam or firm foam, that straps onto the backrest or just sits against it.

**Pros**

- **Low cost** compared with a new chair.
- **Portable.** You can move it between your desk chair, your car and a dining chair.
- **Easy to test.** If it doesn't work, you're not stuck with a whole chair.
- **Works on any chair,** including ones with no lumbar adjustment at all.

**Cons**

- **Slips and shifts.** Even with a strap, many pillows slide down or sideways as you move.
- **Adds depth.** A thick pillow pushes you forward on the seat, which effectively shortens the seat depth and can leave your thighs with less support.
- **Fixed shape.** You can adjust height by moving the strap, but thickness and firmness are whatever the foam is.
- **Heat.** Dense memory foam can feel warm over long sessions.

## Built-in lumbar support: pros and cons

Built-in support is part of the chair's backrest. It ranges from a fixed curve molded into the back to a separately adjustable pad.

**Pros**

- **Stays put.** No slipping, no re-adjusting every time you stand up.
- **Integrated with the whole chair,** so seat depth and backrest angle aren't thrown off by an extra cushion.
- **Adjustable versions can be fine-tuned** for height and sometimes depth.

**Cons**

- **Higher cost,** since it usually means buying a new chair.
- **Fixed lumbar curves don't fit everyone.** If the bulge sits too high or too low for your torso, it can feel worse than nothing.
- **Hard to test online.** You won't know if the curve lands in the right place until you sit in it.

## The types of built-in lumbar support

Not all built-in support is equal. When comparing chairs, look at the product specs for these terms:

| Type | What adjusts | Who it suits |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed contour | Nothing; the curve is molded in | People whose back happens to match the chair's shape |
| Height-adjustable | Pad or backrest slides up and down | Most people; handles different torso lengths |
| Depth-adjustable | A knob or lever changes how far it pushes in | People who want more or less pressure |
| Height and depth | Both | Anyone sharing a chair, or who is hard to fit |
| Self-adjusting/flexible back | Backrest flexes with your movement | People who shift position a lot |

Height adjustment matters most, because torso lengths vary a lot. Depth adjustment is a bonus that lets you soften or firm up the support without adding anything.

## Side-by-side comparison

| Factor | Lumbar pillow | Built-in (adjustable) |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | Low | Part of a new chair |
| Stays in place | Often slips | Yes |
| Adjust height | By moving the strap | Built in on most good chairs |
| Adjust firmness/depth | No, unless you swap pillows | On some chairs |
| Effect on seat depth | Pushes you forward | None |
| Portability | Excellent | None |
| Best when | Your chair otherwise fits | You need a new chair anyway |

## How to choose: a simple decision guide

**A pillow is the right call if:**

- Your chair's seat height, seat depth and armrests already fit you.
- The towel test helped, and you want something tidier.
- You want support in more than one seat, such as the car.
- Your budget is tight.

**A chair with built-in adjustable lumbar support is the better call if:**

- Your chair is also wrong in other ways: the seat is too deep, the backrest doesn't recline or lock, or the height won't go low enough.
- You've tried pillows and they keep slipping or pushing you forward.
- More than one person uses the chair.
- You sit for most of the day and want something you set once and forget.

If you're in the second group, [[office-chairs|compare office chairs with adjustable lumbar support]] and make height adjustment a must-have.

### A combination that sometimes works

Some people with a fixed-contour chair find the built-in curve sits slightly too low. A thin cushion or folded towel placed above it can fill the gap. This is a reasonable fix, but if you're stacking multiple things to get comfortable, it's a sign the chair doesn't fit.

## How to position either type correctly

Whether it's a pillow or a pad in the chair, the setup steps are the same:

1. **Sit all the way back** so your buttocks touch the backrest. Lumbar support can't work if you perch on the front edge.
2. **Find the small of your back.** It's the deepest part of the curve, usually around belt level or slightly above.
3. **Place the support there,** centered in the curve rather than on the tailbone or up at the shoulder blades.
4. **Check the pressure.** You should feel gentle, even contact. If you're being pushed into an arch or forward off the seat, reduce the depth or use a thinner pillow.
5. **Check the seat depth afterward.** With a pillow in place, you should still have about two to four fingers of space between the seat edge and the back of your knees, with most of your thighs supported.
6. **Recline slightly.** A backrest angle just past upright, around 100 to 110 degrees, often feels better than bolt upright and lets the backrest share the load.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Placing support too low.** Pushing on the pelvis or tailbone tips you forward.
- **Choosing the thickest pillow.** More isn't better. Start thin.
- **Ignoring the rest of the chair.** Lumbar support won't fix a seat that's too deep or a desk that's too high.
- **Sitting still all day.** Even perfect support doesn't replace movement. Stand up, walk and stretch at least every 30 to 60 minutes.
- **Expecting it to treat pain.** Support can make sitting more comfortable, but ongoing pain needs a professional assessment.

## Bottom line

Start with a rolled towel to learn where and how much support you need. If that works and your chair otherwise fits, a lumbar pillow is a sensible, low-cost upgrade. If your chair has other problems, or pillows keep sliding around, put the money toward a chair with height-adjustable, and ideally depth-adjustable, built-in lumbar support. Either way, sit all the way back and keep the support in the small of your back, not above or below it.
