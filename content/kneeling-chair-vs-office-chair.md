For most people who sit at a desk all day, a well-adjusted office chair is the better main seat, and a kneeling chair works best as a part-time alternative. A kneeling chair tilts you forward and opens the angle at your hips, which some people find makes upright posture easier. But most kneeling chairs have no backrest, put pressure on your shins and knees, and make it harder to shift positions, so few people are comfortable in one for a full workday.

This guide explains what each chair does to your body, where each one wins, and how to decide.

## How the two chairs differ

### A standard office chair

You sit with your thighs roughly level, feet flat on the floor, hips and knees around 90 degrees. A good office chair supports your lower back with a backrest and lumbar support, lets you recline, and adjusts in several ways: seat height, seat depth, armrests and backrest tension.

### A kneeling chair

The seat slopes forward, and a second padded surface below it supports your shins. Your thighs angle downward, which opens the angle between your torso and thighs well beyond 90 degrees. Part of your weight shifts from your seat bones to your shins.

The idea is that an open hip angle lets your pelvis tilt forward naturally, which makes it easier to keep the gentle inward curve in your lower back without effort. Many kneeling chairs have no backrest, so your own muscles hold you upright. Some models add a backrest, rocking runners or casters.

## Kneeling chair: pros and cons

**Pros**

- **Encourages an upright position** by tilting the pelvis forward.
- **Opens the hip angle,** which some people with tight hips find more comfortable than a 90-degree seat.
- **Compact.** Many models tuck neatly under a desk and take up little floor space.
- **Encourages movement** on rocking models, where you can gently shift forward and back.
- **Useful for forward-leaning tasks** like drawing, writing by hand or working close to the desk surface.

**Cons**

- **Shin and knee pressure.** Kneeling pads carry part of your weight, which can become uncomfortable and may not suit people with knee problems.
- **No back support on most models.** You can't lean back and rest, so muscles tire over long sessions.
- **Harder to change position.** Your legs are locked into one posture. Getting in and out takes practice.
- **Limited adjustability** compared with a good office chair.
- **Doesn't fit every desk.** You typically sit a bit lower or higher than in a regular chair, so check that your desk height still puts your elbows at about 90 degrees.
- **Circulation.** Some people notice discomfort or tingling in their lower legs after a long session.

## Office chair: pros and cons

**Pros**

- **Back support.** You can lean back and let the chair take some of the load.
- **Easy to shift positions,** recline, and move around the desk.
- **Highly adjustable,** so it can be tuned to your body and desk.
- **Comfortable for long sessions** when set up correctly.

**Cons**

- **Easy to slump.** Without good lumbar support and a correct setup, many people end up with a rounded lower back.
- **Takes more space.**
- **Quality varies a lot.** A poorly designed or badly adjusted chair can cause discomfort just as easily as it prevents it.

## Side-by-side comparison

| Factor | Kneeling chair | Office chair |
|---|---|---|
| Hip angle | Open, well past 90 degrees | Around 90 degrees, more when reclined |
| Back support | Usually none | Backrest and lumbar support |
| Weight on knees/shins | Yes | No |
| Ease of changing position | Limited | Easy |
| Adjustability | Height, sometimes angle | Height, depth, armrests, tilt and more |
| Comfortable for a full day | For few people | For most people when well fitted |
| Space | Compact | Larger |
| Best for | Short sessions, variety, forward tasks | Main all-day seat |

## Who a kneeling chair suits

A kneeling chair may be worth a try if:

- You tend to slump in a regular chair and want something that nudges you upright.
- You like sitting forward, close to the desk, for writing or detail work.
- You already use a standing desk and want another position in your rotation.
- Your knees are healthy and you're comfortable kneeling on a padded surface.
- You have a good office chair already and want variety rather than a replacement.

## Who should stick with an office chair

An office chair is the better choice if:

- You have knee pain, knee injuries or conditions affecting your knees or shins.
- You sit for most of the working day and need to rest against a backrest.
- You like to recline during calls or reading.
- You need armrests to support your arms.
- You share the chair with others of different sizes.

If your main problem is slumping or lower back discomfort, try fixing your current chair first. A rolled towel at the small of your back, a correctly set seat height and a slight recline help many people before any purchase. ## The best of both: rotate positions

No single posture is good for eight hours. Your body does best when you change position regularly. A kneeling chair can be a useful part of that rotation:

- **Morning:** office chair for focused work.
- **Midday:** kneeling chair for 30 to 60 minutes of writing or email.
- **Afternoon:** stand for part of the time if you have a standing desk, then back to the office chair.

Start with short sessions on a kneeling chair, around 15 to 30 minutes, and build up gradually. Stop if you notice pain in your knees, shins or lower back.

## How to set up a kneeling chair

If you decide to try one, a correct setup makes a big difference:

1. **Adjust the seat height** so your elbows are at about desk height with your shoulders relaxed, just like in an office chair.
2. **Set the seat angle** if it adjusts. A gentle forward slope is usually more comfortable than a steep one.
3. **Place your shins on the pads** so most of your weight stays on your seat bones. The shin pads should support, not carry most of your weight.
4. **Keep the monitor at the right height:** top of the screen at or slightly below eye level, about an arm's length away.
5. **Get up regularly.** Stand and walk for a few minutes every half hour or so, which is easy since getting off a kneeling chair already means moving.

### Features to look for

- Thick, firm padding on both seat and shin rests
- Adjustable height, ideally with some angle adjustment
- A sturdy frame that doesn't wobble when you shift
- Rocking base if you like gentle movement
- A backrest if you want to lean back occasionally

## Common mistakes

- **Replacing your office chair entirely** instead of adding the kneeling chair as an option.
- **Putting all your weight on your knees.** Most of it should be on your seat.
- **Using it for hours on day one.** Build up slowly.
- **Ignoring desk height.** If your elbows end up far above or below the desk, you'll strain your shoulders and wrists.
- **Pushing through pain.** Kneeling chairs aren't for everyone, and that's fine.

A note on health: this is general information. If you have persistent or severe back, hip or knee pain, or numbness or tingling in your legs, see a doctor or physiotherapist before changing your seating.

## Bottom line

A kneeling chair is a good tool for variety and for people who like sitting upright and forward, but it's hard to beat a well-fitted office chair as your main seat. If you're curious, keep your office chair, add a kneeling chair for short sessions, and let your body tell you how much time it wants in each.
