Start small and build up. Stand for 15 to 30 minutes at a time, two or three times a day, for the first week, then add time only when your feet, legs and back feel fine the next morning. Set the desk so your elbows sit at about 90 degrees, wear supportive shoes or stand on a cushioned surface, and keep switching between sitting and standing rather than trying to stand all day. Most standing desk pain comes from doing too much too soon on a hard floor at the wrong height.

## Why a new standing desk can hurt

Standing is not harder than sitting in any dramatic way, but it uses your body differently. After years of sitting at work, your feet, calves and lower back simply aren't used to holding you up for long stretches. Typical early complaints include:

- Sore feet, especially the heels and arches
- Tight or aching calves
- Lower back ache from locking the knees or leaning on one hip
- Shoulder and neck tension when the desk is set too high or too low

The fix is almost always the same: shorter standing sessions, a better surface underfoot, and a correct desk height.

## Step 1: Set the desk height before you stand for real

A standing desk set at the wrong height causes more problems than standing itself.

1. Stand straight in the shoes you'll actually wear (or in socks if you'll stand on a mat barefoot).
2. Let your arms hang, then bend your elbows to about 90 degrees.
3. Raise or lower the desk until the keyboard surface is at, or a hair below, elbow height.
4. Your wrists should be straight, not bent up or down, and your shoulders relaxed.

Then set your screen. The top of the monitor should be at or slightly below eye level, about an arm's length away. If you work on a laptop, raise it with a stand or books and use an external keyboard and mouse. A laptop screen at keyboard height will have you bending your neck within minutes.

Write down or save your sitting and standing heights. Many electric desks have memory presets; use them. Getting the same position every time is half the battle.

## Step 2: Follow a gradual schedule

There is no single correct timetable, but a slow ramp works for most people. Here's a sensible starting plan:

| Week | Standing per session | Sessions per day | Rough daily total |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 15 to 20 minutes | 2 to 3 | 30 to 60 minutes |
| 2 | 20 to 30 minutes | 3 to 4 | 1 to 1.5 hours |
| 3 | 30 to 45 minutes | 3 to 4 | 1.5 to 2 hours |
| 4 and beyond | 30 to 60 minutes | as feels right | 2 to 4 hours |

Two rules make this work:

- **Only move to the next week if you feel fine.** If your feet ache the next morning, stay where you are for a few more days.
- **Sit down before it hurts.** The goal is to leave each standing session feeling fine, not to push through discomfort.

Many people find that a good long-term pattern is changing position every 30 to 60 minutes. The movement matters more than the exact ratio of sitting to standing.

### Make it automatic

- Tie standing to specific tasks: calls, email triage, the first half hour after lunch.
- Use a simple timer or a calendar reminder for the first couple of weeks.
- Stand for meetings where you're mostly listening.

## Step 3: Take care of your feet

Feet are the first thing to complain, and they're the easiest to help.

**Free fixes first:**

- **Wear supportive shoes.** Everyday sneakers with some cushioning beat bare feet or thin slippers on hardwood or tile.
- **Use a folded yoga mat or a thick rug.** Hard floors make standing tiring much faster.
- **Shift your weight.** Move from foot to foot, rock gently heel to toe, or step one foot slightly forward.
- **Raise one foot.** A low box or a thick book under one foot, switched every few minutes, takes pressure off your lower back.

**Worth buying if you stand regularly:** an anti-fatigue mat is the most useful accessory for a new standing desk user. Look for one that's at least half an inch thick, has beveled edges so you don't trip, and is firm enough that you don't sink in. Some have raised areas or a contoured surface that encourages you to shift position. You can [[anti-fatigue-mats|compare anti-fatigue mats for standing desks]] and pick a size that lets you move around a bit.

## Step 4: Stand well, not stiffly

How you stand matters as much as how long.

- **Soft knees.** Don't lock them. Keep a very slight bend.
- **Weight spread evenly.** Avoid sinking into one hip for minutes at a time.
- **Screen and keyboard close enough.** Leaning forward to reach the keyboard strains your lower back.
- **Shoulders down.** If you catch them creeping up, your desk is probably too high.
- **Move.** Small movements, weight shifts, and stretches are good. Standing perfectly still is the most tiring way to stand.

## Step 5: Keep sitting well, too

A standing desk works best as a sit-stand desk. When you sit back down:

- Lower the desk all the way back to your sitting height. Don't sit at standing height with your arms raised.
- Check that your feet rest flat on the floor or a footrest.
- Readjust your chair, since you'll likely have shifted it while standing.

## Common beginner mistakes

- **Standing for hours on day one.** Enthusiasm is the number one cause of sore feet in week one.
- **Setting the desk too high.** People often raise it until the screen looks right, leaving the keyboard at chest height.
- **Standing barefoot on hard floors.** Wear shoes or use a mat.
- **Locking the knees and leaning.** It shifts load onto the lower back.
- **Forgetting the monitor.** A monitor that is fine for sitting may be too low when you stand if it sits directly on the desktop. Adjust it every time you switch, or use a [[monitor-arms|height-adjustable monitor arm]] so moving it takes seconds.
- **Treating standing as exercise.** It's a way to add variety and movement, not a replacement for walking or other activity.

## If you're still in pain after a few weeks

Some soreness during the first week or two is normal. Pain that doesn't settle after a couple of weeks of gradual use usually points to one of a few causes:

- **Desk or screen height** is still off. Recheck elbow and eye height.
- **Footwear or flooring** is too hard. Add a mat or change shoes.
- **Session length** is too long. Drop back to 15 to 20 minutes and rebuild.
- **Posture habits**, such as locking the knees or leaning on one leg.

If you have persistent or worsening pain, sharp pain, swelling, or numbness or tingling in your feet or legs, stop and see a doctor or physiotherapist. People with existing foot, knee, back, or circulation issues should ask a professional how much standing is sensible for them before ramping up.

## Bottom line

- Start with 15 to 20 minute sessions, two or three times a day.
- Set the keyboard at elbow height and the top of the screen at or slightly below eye level.
- Protect your feet with supportive shoes or a cushioned mat.
- Keep moving: shift weight, change positions, alternate between sitting and standing.
- Increase standing time only when your body feels fine the next day.
