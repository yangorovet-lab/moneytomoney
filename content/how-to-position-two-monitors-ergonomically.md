The right dual-monitor layout depends on how you split your time between the screens. If you use one screen most of the day, put it directly in front of you and set the second one off to the side, angled toward you. If you use both about equally, push them together so the seam between them sits right in front of your nose, and angle both inward. In either case, keep the top edges at or slightly below eye level and both screens about an arm's length away.

## Step one: figure out your usage split

Before you move anything, spend a day paying attention to where your eyes actually go. Most people fall into one of three patterns:

- **Primary plus reference (roughly 80/20 or more lopsided).** You write, code or design on one screen and glance at email, chat or documentation on the other.
- **Equal use (roughly 50/50).** You compare documents side by side, trade across two charts, or drag work back and forth constantly.
- **Stacked needs.** You want a lot of screen area but have a narrow desk, or you need a laptop plus one external monitor.

The pattern matters because your neck is happiest when your most-used screen is straight ahead. Turning your head 30 or 40 degrees to one side for a few seconds is fine. Holding that twist for hours is what leads to a stiff, one-sided neck.

## Layout A: one primary screen, one secondary

This is the most common and usually the most comfortable setup.

1. **Center the primary monitor** on your body. The middle of the screen should line up with your nose and your keyboard's "B" key (or the center of the letter keys, not the center of the whole keyboard with the number pad).
2. **Place the secondary monitor** directly next to it on your dominant eye side, or on whichever side your work naturally flows. If you're not sure, the side your mouse is on often works well.
3. **Angle the secondary screen inward** by about 15 to 30 degrees so its face points toward your eyes rather than straight ahead.
4. **Keep the edges touching or nearly touching** so you aren't turning your head across a gap.

Because you look at the secondary screen only briefly, it's acceptable for it to sit a little farther away or even slightly lower than the primary.

## Layout B: two screens used equally

1. **Put the two screens edge to edge** and center the seam on your body.
2. **Angle each screen inward** so they form a shallow "V" or gentle arc around you. A good test: from your seated position, both screen faces should look roughly square to your eyes when you turn your head toward them.
3. **Match their heights** so the top edges line up. A mismatch forces your eyes and neck to adjust every time you switch.
4. **Move your chair, not just your head.** With a 50/50 split, you'll turn a bit more. A chair that swivels easily lets your whole body rotate instead of loading your neck.

If you find yourself favoring one screen after a few weeks, switch to Layout A.

## Layout C: stacked or laptop plus monitor

**Stacking vertically** saves desk width, but the upper screen tends to end up too high. It only works well if the lower screen sits low (close to the desk) and the upper screen is tilted down toward you. Reserve the top screen for things you check occasionally, like a chat window or dashboard. Looking up for long stretches is harder on the neck than looking down.

**Laptop plus external monitor:** Make the external monitor your primary screen and center it. Put the laptop to one side on a stand so its screen is as close as possible to the height of the monitor. Use an external keyboard and mouse so your hands aren't pulled toward the laptop. A [[laptop-stands|laptop stand]] makes this layout much easier to get right.

## Height, distance and tilt for both screens

These are the same rules as for a single monitor, applied to each screen.

| Setting | Guideline | How to check |
|---|---|---|
| Height | Top of the screen at or slightly below eye level | Sit upright, look straight ahead; your gaze should hit the top edge or just under it |
| Distance | Roughly an arm's length (about 20 to 30 inches for most setups) | Extend your arm from your seat; fingertips should reach close to the screen |
| Tilt | Tip the top back 10 to 20 degrees | The screen face should look perpendicular to your line of sight |
| Horizontal angle | Primary straight on; secondary angled inward | Both screens should appear square to your eyes when you face them |
| Brightness | Similar on both screens | Adjust so neither looks like a light source next to the other |

**Wear bifocals or progressives?** Lower both screens a bit more than usual, sometimes to the point where the top edge is a few inches below eye level, so you don't tip your head back to read through the lower part of your lenses.

**Larger screens need more distance.** If you have two big monitors, you may need to sit a little farther back so you can see most of each screen without swinging your head. If the setup still feels too wide, that's a sign one screen should become secondary.

## Fixing mismatched monitors

Two different screens are very common, and they create three problems.

### Different heights

Raise the shorter one with a sturdy stack of books, a ream of paper or a solid box until the top edges line up. This is free and works well if the stack is stable. If you want finer control, [[monitor-arms|a dual monitor arm]] lets you set height and angle for each screen independently and frees up desk space.

### Different sizes

Line up the **top edges** or the **centers**, depending on which feels better. Most people find aligning the centers vertically makes switching feel smoother. The smaller screen usually makes the better secondary display.

### Different resolutions or scaling

If text looks much larger on one screen than the other, your eyes refocus and your brain has to readjust with every glance. In your operating system's display settings, adjust the scaling so text appears roughly the same physical size on both. Also match the arrangement in the settings to the physical layout, so the mouse moves across the correct edge.

## Common mistakes

- **Centering the gap in a primary/secondary setup.** If you mostly use one screen, a centered seam means your neck is turned all day.
- **Leaving both screens flat in a straight line.** The outer edges end up farther away and at an angle, which causes extra eye strain.
- **Stacking the secondary screen too high.** Looking up to read is uncomfortable within minutes and a pain after hours.
- **Keyboard not centered on the main screen.** Your body tends to face the keyboard. If the keyboard points one way and the screen the other, you twist.
- **Ignoring glare.** Two screens mean twice as many surfaces to catch window light. Position them so windows are to the side, not behind or directly in front of you.
- **Forgetting breaks.** Every 20 minutes or so, look at something far away for a few seconds, and stand up at least once an hour.

## Do you need to buy anything?

Often, no. Most dual-monitor problems can be fixed with a few free moves:

- Swap which screen is primary.
- Raise the lower screen on books or a box.
- Angle the side screen inward.
- Slide your keyboard so it's centered on your main screen.
- Sit a few inches farther back.

Consider buying gear if:

- **Your monitor stands don't adjust in height or angle.** A dual arm solves both at once and clears desk space.
- **Your desk is shallow** and you can't get the screens far enough away. An arm mounted at the back edge can gain you several inches.
- **You work from a laptop** and the second screen can't get to a matching height without a stand.

When choosing a dual arm, check the weight and size limits for each screen, that your monitors have VESA mounting holes, and that your desk edge can take a clamp (thickness and whether there's a lip or crossbar in the way).

## When discomfort persists

If your neck or shoulders still ache after you've adjusted your layout, look at the rest of your setup too: chair height, keyboard position and how often you move. Persistent or severe pain, or any numbness or tingling in your arms or hands, should be checked by a doctor or physiotherapist.

## Quick checklist

- Decide whether you use one screen more (primary + secondary) or both equally.
- Primary screen: centered on your nose and keyboard.
- Equal use: seam centered, both screens angled inward.
- Top edges at or slightly below eye level; lower still if you wear progressives.
- About an arm's length away, tilted back slightly.
- Matching heights, brightness and text size.
- Windows to the side to cut glare.
- Swivel your chair instead of twisting your neck, and take regular breaks.
