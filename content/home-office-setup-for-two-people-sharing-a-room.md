Sharing a home office works best when each person gets their own desk, their own chair adjusted to their body, and a clear plan for noise. The layout matters most: back-to-back or side-by-side with a gap usually beats facing each other, because it cuts visual distraction and keeps one person out of the other's video calls. Add headsets, a few soft surfaces to absorb sound, and a simple agreement about who takes calls where, and two people can work comfortably in a room that feels made for one.

## Start with a conversation, not furniture

Before moving anything, figure out how each of you actually works. Mismatched schedules cause more friction than small desks.

Talk through:

- **How many calls each person has per day,** and whether they're camera-on.
- **Whether either of you needs deep quiet** for focused work, or prefers background noise.
- **Work hours.** An early starter and a late finisher can share happily if the late one doesn't need the overhead light at 7 a.m.
- **Storage and equipment.** One person may need two monitors and a printer; the other just a laptop.
- **Temperature and light preferences.** One person near a window and one near a radiator is a common source of quiet resentment.

The answers decide the layout. Two people with frequent calls need more separation than one caller and one quiet typist.

## Layout options compared

| Layout | Best for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Back to back, each facing a wall | Frequent calls for both people; long rooms | Needs enough depth for two chairs to roll back |
| Side by side along one wall | Narrow rooms; shared cable runs | Voices carry directly; elbows can bump without a gap |
| L-shaped, each on one wall of a corner | Square rooms; moderate calls | Chairs may collide in the corner; plan clearance |
| Facing each other across a double desk | Collaborative work, few calls | Each person appears in the other's camera view |
| Opposite ends of the room | Two heavy callers in a larger room | Longer cable runs; harder to share a printer |

### Back to back

This is often the best choice for two people who both take calls. Each person faces a wall, which keeps the other out of their camera frame and gives a sense of their own space. You need enough room for both chairs to push back: plan for at least about 3 feet of clearance behind each chair, so roughly 5 to 6 feet between the two desk edges if chairs back toward each other.

### Side by side

Works well in a narrow room or along a long wall. Leave a gap between desks, even a small one, or put a low bookcase, plant, or storage unit between them. A physical divider does a lot psychologically, even if it doesn't block much sound.

### Facing each other

Tempting because it feels sociable, but it has real drawbacks: you see the other person moving every time you look up, and each of you is likely to show up in the background of the other's video. It suits couples or colleagues who collaborate constantly and rarely take calls.

## Give each person their own ergonomics

Two people rarely share the same body size, and a shared desk or chair setup almost always fits one of them badly. Each person should have:

1. **Their own chair**, adjusted so their feet rest flat and their thighs are roughly parallel to the floor.
2. **Desk or keyboard height at their own elbow level.** If you're both at the same fixed-height desk and one person is much shorter, the shorter person usually needs to raise their chair and add a footrest.
3. **The top of their monitor at or slightly below their eye level**, about an arm's length away.
4. **Their own keyboard and mouse**, if they use a laptop, so the screen can be raised.

If you share one long desk, don't assume one height works for both. Height-adjustable desks solve this cleanly, and two separate [[standing-desks|standing desks]] let each person set their own height and switch between sitting and standing without affecting the other. If budget is tight, a fixed desk plus adjustable chairs and a footrest for the shorter person is a solid compromise.

## Managing noise

Noise is usually the biggest problem in a shared office. Tackle it in layers.

### Behavior first (free)

- **Agree on a call signal.** A small flag, a lamp, or a shared calendar makes it obvious when someone's on a call.
- **Move the calls, not the worker.** If one person has a long meeting, can they take it from another room, the kitchen, or a bedroom? Even moving the most important calls out helps.
- **Stagger calls** where possible. Two people talking at once in one room is hard on both.
- **Use chat for quick questions** during focus time rather than talking across the room.

### Sound-absorbing surfaces

Hard rooms echo, which makes every voice louder and more distracting. Soft surfaces help:

- A rug on hard floors
- Curtains, especially heavy ones
- Bookshelves full of books on shared walls
- Upholstered furniture, cushions, or fabric panels

None of these will soundproof a room, but they take the harsh edge off voices and improve call quality for both people.

### Headsets

When both people take calls, a headset with a microphone that picks up your voice and not the room is close to essential. It stops your colleague's meeting from leaking into yours and means you can speak at a normal volume. Look for [[headsets|a headset with a noise-canceling microphone]] if call audio matters; for focus time, closed-back or noise-canceling headphones also cut the other person's typing and voice.

## Video calls in a shared room

- **Check your background** from your seat with the camera on. Is the other person visible? Rotate your desk or add a divider if so.
- **Light your face from the front or side.** A window behind you turns you into a silhouette.
- **Blur or replace the background** if you can't avoid the other person being in frame.
- **Mute when not speaking.** It helps the other person as much as your call.

## Lighting that works for both

Lighting disagreements are common. One person may want bright overhead light, the other a dimmer room.

- Give each person **their own desk lamp** so they can control their own task light.
- Avoid putting either desk where a window is directly behind the screen or directly behind the person.
- If one person works later, a lamp aimed at their desk (not across the room) keeps the space comfortable for the other.

## Power, cables, and shared gear

Two people double the cables, and a messy floor becomes a tripping hazard fast.

- **Plan outlets early.** Put each desk near its own outlet or run a single surge protector per desk rather than chaining extensions.
- **Route cables along walls or under desks.** Cable trays, clips, or even zip ties keep things off the floor.
- **Share where it makes sense.** A printer, router, and paper supplies can sit on a shared shelf between desks.
- **Keep chargers separate** to avoid "borrowing" arguments.

## Storage and boundaries

Clear boundaries help both people feel settled:

- Each person gets their own drawer, shelf, or cart. Mixed stacks of paper create friction.
- Keep shared items in one clearly shared place.
- A small rolling cart under each desk keeps personal gear close and frees up the desktop.
- Clear your desk at the end of the day if you're sharing it or the room doubles as something else.

## Common mistakes

- **One desk for two people** without enough width. Each person needs at least about 30 inches of desk width to work comfortably, and more for dual monitors.
- **Chairs that collide.** Measure how far each chair rolls back before committing to a layout.
- **Assuming one height fits both.** It rarely does.
- **Ignoring calls until they happen.** Agree on call etiquette in advance.
- **All hard surfaces.** Bare floors and walls make everything louder.

## Quick checklist

- Talk through schedules, calls, and preferences first
- Pick a layout that keeps each person out of the other's camera view
- Each person has their own adjustable chair and correctly set desk height
- Monitor top at or slightly below eye level for each user
- Headsets for calls; a signal for "on a call"
- Rugs, curtains, or shelves to soften sound
- Separate task lamps
- Cables routed off the floor, one surge protector per desk

## Bottom line

A shared home office works when each person has their own properly adjusted workstation and you've agreed on how to handle noise before it becomes a problem. Back-to-back layouts suit two regular callers best, side-by-side works for narrow rooms, and a couple of headsets plus some soft furnishings will do more for your peace than any piece of furniture.
