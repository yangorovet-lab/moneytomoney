Noise gets into a home office in two ways: through gaps and thin surfaces (traffic, neighbors, the TV next door) and by bouncing around inside the room (echo, your own keyboard, a humming computer). Sealing gaps around the door and windows cuts the first kind; adding soft materials like rugs, curtains and upholstered furniture cuts the second. Then quiet your own equipment and, for the noise you can't stop, use a headset with good noise cancellation. Most of the improvement comes from the cheapest steps.

## Start by working out what kind of noise you have

Different noise needs different fixes, so spend a day noting what bothers you.

| Noise type | Examples | What helps most |
|---|---|---|
| Airborne noise from outside the room | Voices, TV, traffic, dogs barking | Sealing gaps, heavier curtains, a solid door |
| Echo inside the room | Your voice sounds hollow on calls, sounds ring | Rugs, curtains, soft furniture, bookshelves |
| Impact noise | Footsteps upstairs, doors slamming | Hard to block; masking and headsets help most |
| Your own equipment | Clicky keyboard, loud fans, desk vibration | Quieter gear, maintenance, pads |
| Low hums | HVAC, fridges, traffic rumble | Masking sound and noise-canceling headphones |

Soundproofing a room completely takes construction work: extra layers of drywall, decoupled walls, special doors. This guide focuses on what you can do without building work, which is enough for most home offices.

## Step 1: Seal the gaps (cheap, high impact)

Sound travels through air, so any gap is a leak. Doors are usually the worst offender.

- **Door bottom.** The gap under an interior door lets in a lot of sound. A door sweep or a draft stopper closes it. A rolled towel is a free test.
- **Door edges.** Self-adhesive weatherstripping around the frame helps the door seal when closed.
- **Windows.** Weatherstripping around window frames reduces traffic noise and drafts at the same time.
- **Outlets and vents on shared walls.** Gaps around outlet boxes can let sound through. Foam outlet gaskets are an inexpensive fix.

A hollow-core interior door blocks little sound compared with a solid-core one. If you own your home and the door is a big problem, swapping it can make a noticeable difference.

## Step 2: Soften the room (mostly free)

Hard surfaces like bare floors, walls and glass reflect sound, so the room rings. That echo makes you sound worse on calls and makes other noise more noticeable.

Soft, dense, irregular things absorb sound. Try moving these into your office:

- **A rug**, ideally a thick one with a pad underneath.
- **Curtains**, the heavier the better. Full-length curtains over a window help with both echo and outside noise.
- **Upholstered furniture**, like an armchair or a padded bench.
- **Full bookshelves.** Books of different sizes break up sound reflections. Put a shelf against a shared wall for a small added benefit.
- **Soft wall hangings**, such as a quilt, tapestry or fabric panel.
- **Throw pillows and blankets** on any sofa or chair.

A quick way to test echo is to clap once in the middle of the room. If you hear a sharp ring or flutter, add more soft surfaces.

### Acoustic panels

If the room still echoes, acoustic foam or fabric-wrapped panels absorb mid and high frequencies well. Place them at the first reflection points: the wall behind your monitor, the wall behind you, and the side walls level with your head. They make a big difference to call quality. They don't block sound coming in from outside, though, so don't expect them to stop a neighbor's music.

## Step 3: Quiet your own equipment

Sometimes the loudest thing in the office is on your desk.

### Keyboard and mouse

- Clicky mechanical switches are loud. Linear or silent-rated switches, or a membrane or scissor keyboard, are much quieter.
- A desk mat under the keyboard and mouse dampens clatter.
- Many mice have quiet-click versions.

### Computer

- Clean dust out of fans and vents; clogged fans spin faster and louder.
- Keep the computer off the floor if it's pulling in dust, and away from walls that reflect fan noise at you.
- Check power settings; some high-performance modes run fans harder than needed.
- A laptop on a stand gets more airflow underneath, which can help fans stay quieter.

### Desk and chair

- Rubber pads under desk feet reduce vibration and hum transmitted to the floor.
- A squeaky chair usually needs its bolts tightened or a little lubricant on the mechanism.
- Standing desk motors make noise while moving. That's normal, but loud grinding may mean it's overloaded.

### Other devices

- Move printers, air purifiers and fans away from your desk, or run them on lower settings during calls.
- Put a soft pad under anything that vibrates on a hard surface.

## Step 4: Mask what you can't block

Masking adds a steady, gentle sound that makes intermittent noises less noticeable. It doesn't lower the noise, but it makes it much less distracting.

- **White, pink or brown noise** from a speaker or app. Brown and pink noise are lower-pitched and many people find them less harsh than white noise.
- **A fan** is a simple free option.
- **Background music or nature sounds**, if you can work with them.

Keep masking noise at a low, comfortable volume. It should blend into the background.

## Step 5: Use a headset for calls and focus

A good [[headsets|headset with active noise cancellation]] is the most reliable way to deal with noise you can't control, like construction, housemates or a barking dog.

When comparing headsets, look for:

- **Active noise cancellation (ANC)** for low, steady noise like traffic and HVAC.
- **A noise-reducing microphone** so others on the call don't hear your background.
- **Comfort for long wear**: light weight, soft ear cushions, adjustable headband.
- **Closed-back design** for isolation. Open-back headphones let sound in and out.
- **Wired or wireless**: wireless is freer; wired avoids charging and pairing issues.

Most video call apps also have built-in noise suppression. Turn it on and set it to its stronger level if your room is noisy.

Take regular breaks from headphones, and keep the volume moderate. If you notice ringing in your ears or trouble hearing, see a doctor or audiologist.

## Step 6: Choose the quietest spot

If you can choose where to work:

- Pick a room away from the street, the kitchen and shared walls.
- Face your desk away from the door so sound reaches you less directly.
- Put bookshelves or closets between you and the noise source.
- A room with a rug and furniture will usually sound better than an empty spare room.

## Common mistakes

- **Buying acoustic foam to block outside noise.** It reduces echo; it doesn't soundproof.
- **Ignoring the door gap.** It's often the biggest leak and the cheapest fix.
- **Relying on headphones for everything.** Wearing them all day is tiring. Fix the room first.
- **Leaving a loud computer dusty.** A quick clean can cut fan noise.

## Quick checklist

- Identify whether noise is coming in or bouncing around.
- Seal the gap under the door and around windows.
- Add a rug, curtains, upholstered furniture and full bookshelves.
- Place acoustic panels at reflection points if echo remains.
- Quiet your keyboard, mouse, computer fans and chair.
- Use gentle masking sound for intermittent noise.
- Use a noise-canceling headset for calls and loud days.

## Bottom line

You don't need to rebuild your walls to get a quieter home office. Seal the gaps, soften the surfaces and quiet the gear on your desk. For whatever noise is left, a steady background sound and a good noise-canceling headset handle the rest.
