There is no single proven number of hours, but a sensible target for most people is to break up sitting with standing stretches of roughly 15 to 30 minutes, several times a day, and to change position before you get uncomfortable. For many desk workers that adds up to somewhere between one and a few hours of standing across a full workday. The key word is *alternating*. Standing all day is not the goal, and it brings its own problems. What helps is moving between positions often.

## Why "stand more" isn't the whole answer

Standing desks became popular as a response to long, uninterrupted sitting. But swapping eight hours of sitting for eight hours of standing just trades one static posture for another. Standing still for long periods puts steady load on your feet, knees, hips, and lower back, and it can leave your legs feeling heavy and tired by the end of the day.

The benefit of a sit-stand desk comes mostly from **variety**: changing position regularly, shifting your weight, and taking small movement breaks. Think of the desk as a tool for switching positions easily, not as a tool for standing.

## A practical starting schedule

If you are new to a standing desk, your legs and feet need time to adjust. Here is a simple progression that many people find manageable:

| Stage | Standing per stretch | How often | Rough daily total |
|---|---|---|---|
| First week | 10 to 15 minutes | 2 to 4 times a day | 30 to 60 minutes |
| Weeks 2 to 3 | 15 to 30 minutes | Once an hour or so | 1 to 2 hours |
| After that | 20 to 45 minutes | Whenever you feel like a change | Whatever stays comfortable |

These are starting points, not rules. Some people settle at a lot less standing, others at more. Let comfort guide you. If your feet ache or your lower back tightens, sit down; that is the signal to switch, not a sign that you need to push through.

### A common rule of thumb

A pattern that many people like is to spend a bit more time sitting than standing, something like 2 parts sitting to 1 part standing, with a short walk or stretch thrown in every half hour or so. For example, in each hour:

- Sit for about 35 to 40 minutes.
- Stand for about 15 to 20 minutes.
- Move for a couple of minutes: refill water, walk to another room, stretch.

This is a practical habit, not a medical prescription. Its real value is that it keeps you from staying in any one position too long.

## How to make the switch a habit

The hardest part of owning a standing desk is remembering to use it. Many desks end up parked at sitting height for months. A few ways to avoid that:

- **Tie standing to specific tasks.** Stand for phone calls, video meetings, reading email, or reviewing documents. Sit for deep, focused typing work.
- **Use a timer.** A simple repeating reminder on your phone or computer works. Some people set it to go off every 30 to 45 minutes.
- **Save your heights.** If your desk has memory presets, store your sitting and standing heights so switching takes one button press. If you use a manual crank, mark the heights on the leg with tape.
- **Start the day standing.** Some people find it easier to begin standing while they check messages and then sit when focused work begins.

## Stand the right way

How you stand matters as much as how long. A poorly set up standing position will tire you out quickly.

### Get the height right

At standing height, the desk surface should sit at about elbow height, with your elbows bent around 90 degrees and your shoulders relaxed. Your wrists should be roughly straight when you type. Your monitor still needs to be at eye level, with the top of the screen at or slightly below your eyes, which may mean raising it when you stand if you use a converter rather than a full desk.

### Keep moving while you stand

Standing like a statue is tiring. Small movements help:

- Shift your weight from foot to foot.
- Rest one foot on a low box, a book, or a footrest, and switch sides now and then.
- Take a step back and forth occasionally.
- Avoid locking your knees.

### Mind the floor and your feet

Hard floors can make standing uncomfortable quickly. Supportive shoes help a lot, and so does standing on something with a little give. A folded yoga mat or a thick rug is a free place to start. If you stand regularly, an [[anti-fatigue-mats|anti-fatigue mat for standing desks]] is one of the most useful accessories, because it encourages small, constant movements in your legs.

Standing barefoot on a hard floor for long periods is usually the least comfortable option.

## Signs you are standing too much

Your body will usually tell you when it is time to sit. Watch for:

- Aching feet, heels, or arches.
- Tired or heavy-feeling legs.
- Lower back tightness that builds over the day.
- Knee discomfort.
- Leaning heavily on the desk with your forearms or locking your hips to one side.

If you notice any of these, sit down, and reduce the length of your next few standing stretches. If you catch yourself leaning on the desk, that is a very common sign that you have been standing long enough.

## Signs you are sitting too much

The opposite matters too. If your desk rarely leaves sitting height, you may notice:

- Stiffness in your hips or lower back when you get up.
- Feeling sluggish in the afternoon.
- Realizing you have not moved in two or three hours.

The fix is not to stand for hours. It is to break up sitting with short standing stretches or quick walks.

## Who should be more careful

Some people should take a gentler approach or talk to a health professional before standing for longer periods:

- People with existing foot, knee, hip, or back conditions.
- People with circulation or vein problems in the legs.
- People who are pregnant.
- Anyone recovering from an injury or surgery.

For these groups, frequent position changes still tend to help, but the right balance of sitting and standing is individual. If you have persistent or severe pain, or numbness or tingling in your legs or feet, get it checked by a doctor or physiotherapist rather than trying to adjust your routine on your own.

## Don't have a standing desk yet?

You can test whether you like standing before spending money:

- Stand at a kitchen counter for calls or reading.
- Place a sturdy box or stack of books on your desk to raise a laptop and separate keyboard to elbow height.
- Take calls on your feet, walking around the room.

If you find yourself wanting to stand regularly, the next step is choosing between a full sit-stand desk and a converter that sits on top of your existing desk. A converter is usually cheaper and quicker to set up, while a full desk gives you a larger surface and smoother transitions. If you decide on a full desk, look at [[standing-desks|electric standing desks with memory presets]], because easy switching is what keeps people actually using them.

## Common mistakes

**Standing for hours on day one.** This is the most frequent reason people give up. Build up over a couple of weeks.

**Keeping the monitor too low.** If your screen does not rise with your desk, you will stare down the whole time you stand.

**Ignoring footwear.** Thin slippers or bare feet on hardwood get uncomfortable fast.

**Treating standing as exercise.** Standing burns a bit more energy than sitting, but it is not a substitute for walking or other activity. Short walks during the day add more movement than standing still does.

**Only switching when you remember.** Without a cue, most people stay seated. Use a timer or tie standing to recurring tasks.

## Bottom line

Aim to change position often rather than to hit a specific number of standing hours. Start with 10 to 15 minute stretches a few times a day, work up to 15 to 30 minutes about once an hour, and let comfort decide the rest. Stand on a cushioned surface in supportive shoes, keep your desk at elbow height and your screen at eye level, and add short walks. The best amount of standing is the amount that keeps you comfortable and moving throughout the day.
