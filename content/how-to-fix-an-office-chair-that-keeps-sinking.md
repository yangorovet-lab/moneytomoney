An office chair that keeps sinking almost always has a worn-out gas cylinder, the pneumatic post that holds the seat up. The seals inside wear out and the cylinder can no longer hold pressure, so the seat slowly drops under your weight. You can't repair the cylinder itself, but you can replace it, which usually takes under an hour with a pipe wrench and a rubber mallet. If you need a quick stopgap, a hose clamp or a length of PVC pipe around the cylinder can hold the seat at a fixed height.

## Why chairs sink

Most office chairs use a gas cylinder (also called a gas lift or pneumatic cylinder). It contains pressurized gas and oil sealed inside a tube. When you pull the height lever, a valve opens and the seat rises or lowers. Release the lever and the valve closes, locking the height.

Over time, the seals inside the cylinder wear. Gas leaks from one chamber to the other, and the cylinder no longer holds its position. The result:

- The seat slowly drops over hours or minutes
- The seat drops as soon as you sit down
- The seat won't rise at all when you pull the lever

Less common causes include a bent or broken height lever, a stuck actuator (the button on top of the cylinder that the lever presses), or damage to the mechanism under the seat.

## First, rule out simple problems

Before replacing anything, check these:

1. **Look at the lever.** Flip the chair over carefully and watch what happens when you pull the lever. It should press down on the button at the top of the cylinder and release when you let go. If it's stuck in the "pressed" position, the chair will sink. Sometimes bending the lever back slightly or clearing debris solves it.
2. **Check that the seat mechanism is attached firmly.** Loose bolts under the seat can feel like sinking or wobbling.
3. **Test without weight.** If the chair rises when you pull the lever with no one on it, then sinks under weight, the cylinder is almost certainly worn.

## Quick temporary fixes

These don't fix the cylinder, but they stop the chair from sinking below a set height. They're useful while you wait for a replacement part or if you only need one height.

### The hose clamp method

1. Raise the seat to your preferred height.
2. Slide the plastic cover (the telescoping skirt) down the cylinder if there is one, or remove it.
3. Wrap a hose clamp around the exposed metal cylinder just below the chair's mechanism or on top of the base.
4. Tighten it firmly with a screwdriver. Add a strip of rubber or duct tape underneath to improve grip.
5. Add a second clamp if one slips.

The seat will now sink only until the clamp stops it.

### The PVC pipe method

1. Measure the exposed cylinder length at your preferred seat height.
2. Cut a piece of PVC pipe to that length with a diameter a little larger than the cylinder.
3. Cut the pipe lengthwise so you can snap it around the cylinder.
4. Fit it over the cylinder, then sit down. The pipe holds the seat up.

Both fixes are free or nearly free and work well, but they lock the chair at one height. If more than one person uses the chair, a proper replacement is better.

## How to replace the gas cylinder

Replacing the cylinder is the real fix. It's a common DIY job, but it takes some force.

### What you need

- A replacement gas cylinder that matches your chair (see below)
- A pipe wrench
- A rubber mallet
- Penetrating lubricant spray
- Safety glasses and gloves
- A tape measure

### Choosing the right replacement

Most office chairs use a standard-sized cylinder with a tapered top that fits into the seat mechanism and a tapered bottom that fits into the base. Measure the old one before ordering:

- **Total length** of the cylinder, from the top to where it enters the base, and the length when fully extended.
- **Stroke length** (how much height adjustment it gives).
- **Diameter** of the cylinder body.

Check whether you need a standard or taller or shorter cylinder based on your seat height needs. Taller people often benefit from a longer cylinder, and shorter people from a shorter one. Also check the weight rating matches the user. If the chair came with a warranty, contact the manufacturer first; cylinders are often covered.

### Step-by-step replacement

**1. Remove the seat from the cylinder.**
Turn the chair upside down on a soft surface. Spray penetrating lubricant where the cylinder enters the seat mechanism. Grip the cylinder with the pipe wrench and twist while pulling, or tap the mechanism with the mallet near the cylinder to loosen it. Some chairs come apart easily; others take patience.

**2. Remove the base from the cylinder.**
With the seat off, turn the base upside down. Spray lubricant where the cylinder meets the base. Tap the bottom tip of the cylinder with the rubber mallet to drive it out of the base. Some cylinders have a retaining clip at the bottom that needs removing first; pry it off with a flathead screwdriver.

**3. Insert the new cylinder into the base.**
Set the base upright on the floor. Slide the new cylinder into the center hole. Leave any protective cap on the top button until the end.

**4. Attach the seat.**
Remove the cap, place the seat mechanism over the top of the cylinder, and press down firmly. Sit on the chair to seat everything; your weight pushes the tapered joints together.

**5. Test it.**
Pull the lever and check the seat rises and lowers smoothly. Sit for a while to confirm it holds height.

### Safety notes

- **Never try to open, cut, or heat a gas cylinder.** It's under pressure.
- **Wear eye protection** when hammering.
- **Dispose of old cylinders properly.** Check local rules; some places treat them as scrap metal, while others ask you to take them to a recycling center.
- **Don't swap cylinders on chairs with unusual designs** unless you're confident about compatibility.

## Should you fix it or replace the chair?

| Situation | Best option |
|---|---|
| Otherwise comfortable, good-quality chair | Replace the cylinder |
| Chair under warranty | Contact the manufacturer |
| Chair used at one height by one person | Hose clamp or PVC pipe fix |
| Cheap chair with worn padding, wobbly base, or broken armrests | Consider a new chair |
| Chair never fit your body well | Use this as a chance to upgrade |

A replacement cylinder is a modest cost compared with a new chair, and it can give a decent chair several more years of use.

If you do decide to replace the chair, look for one with a sturdy base, a cylinder weight rating that suits you, and adjustments that match your body: seat height, seat depth, lumbar support, and armrests. ## Common mistakes

- **Replacing the whole chair** when a cylinder swap would fix it
- **Buying the wrong cylinder length**, which leaves the seat too high or too low
- **Forgetting the retaining clip** at the bottom of the cylinder
- **Hammering without lubricant**, which can damage the base
- **Ignoring the lever**, which is sometimes the real problem

## Sitting height after the fix

Once the chair holds height again, take a minute to set it correctly:

- Feet flat on the floor, thighs roughly parallel to it
- Elbows around 90 degrees with your keyboard at elbow height
- If your feet don't reach the floor at the right height, add a [[footrests|footrest]]

A chair that's been sinking may have pushed you into a slouched or reaching posture for weeks. If you notice back or neck pain that lingers after fixing the height, or any numbness or tingling, check in with a doctor or physiotherapist.

## Quick checklist

- Check the lever and actuator button first
- Temporary fix: hose clamp or split PVC pipe
- Permanent fix: replace the gas cylinder
- Measure the old cylinder and check the weight rating
- Use lubricant, a pipe wrench, and a mallet; wear eye protection
- Reset your seat height properly afterward

## Bottom line

A sinking office chair almost always needs a new gas cylinder. For a quick, free fix, clamp a hose clamp or split PVC pipe around the cylinder to lock the height. For a lasting fix, buy a matching replacement cylinder and swap it with a pipe wrench and a mallet. It's a simple job that can keep a good chair in service for years.
