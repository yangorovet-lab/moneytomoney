Build your work-from-home routine around a few fixed anchors rather than a minute-by-minute schedule: a consistent wake-up and start time, a short ritual that marks the beginning of work, two or three protected focus blocks, regular movement breaks, a real lunch away from the desk, and a shutdown routine that ends the day. Anchors give your day structure without breaking the first time a meeting runs late. Start with two or three, keep them for a couple of weeks, then add more.

## Why routines fall apart at home

In an office, the commute, colleagues and building hours shape the day for you. At home, all of that disappears, and a few things tend to happen:

- The start of the day drifts later, or work starts in bed on your phone.
- Breaks disappear because nothing prompts them, or they stretch because nothing ends them.
- Work leaks into the evening because the laptop is always right there.
- Days blur together.

A routine replaces the structure the office used to provide. It doesn't need to be strict. It needs to be repeatable.

## Step 1: Pick your fixed anchors

Anchors are the handful of things that happen at the same time every day, regardless of what else changes. Choose these first:

1. **Wake-up time.** The same time on workdays, within about half an hour.
2. **Start time.** When you sit down and begin real work.
3. **Lunch.** A fixed window when you step away.
4. **Stop time.** When the workday ends.

Everything else fits around these. If your job has unpredictable meetings, anchors still work; they just give you a default to return to.

## Step 2: Replace the commute with a start ritual

The commute gave your brain time to switch into work mode. Without it, you need a short replacement. It should take 10 to 20 minutes and happen the same way every day.

Ideas that work for many people:

- A short walk around the block before sitting down, a "fake commute".
- Making coffee or tea and drinking it away from the desk.
- Getting fully dressed, including shoes.
- A quick tidy of the desk and opening the same apps in the same order.
- Writing down the one to three things that must get done today.

The specific ritual matters less than doing it consistently. After a while, it becomes the signal that work has started.

## Step 3: Set up a place that means "work"

A routine is much easier when you have a dedicated spot, even a small one. If you work at the kitchen table, set up and pack away the same way each day so the space changes role.

A quick ergonomic check at the start of the day also keeps discomfort from building up:

- Top of the screen at or slightly below eye level, about an arm's length away.
- Elbows at roughly 90 degrees, shoulders relaxed.
- Feet flat on the floor or a footrest.
- Lower back supported, even if it's a rolled towel.

If you use a laptop at a table, raising it on books and using a separate keyboard and mouse is the most useful free-to-cheap change you can make.

## Step 4: Protect focus blocks

Plan your hardest work for the time of day when you think most clearly. For many people that's the morning, but not everyone. Look back over a week and notice when you get the most done.

How to protect focus time:

- **Block it on your calendar** so others can see it.
- **Close email and chat**, or set yourself to do-not-disturb.
- **Pick a length that suits you.** Some people like 25-minute sprints with short breaks; others prefer 60 to 90 minutes of deep work. Experiment.
- **Decide in advance what you'll work on** so you don't spend the block choosing.
- **Batch shallow tasks** like email and admin into their own blocks later in the day.

Two focus blocks a day is a realistic target for most jobs with meetings.

## Step 5: Build in movement

Working from home often means less walking: no commute, no trips to meeting rooms, no walk to lunch. Put movement into the routine on purpose.

- **Stand up and move every 30 to 60 minutes.** Refill your water, stretch, walk to another room.
- **Look away from the screen regularly.** Focusing on something far away gives your eyes a rest.
- **Take some calls standing or walking** if you don't need your screen.
- **Alternate sitting and standing** if your setup allows it. A [[desk-converters|desk converter]] on your existing desk is a simpler way to try standing than replacing the desk.
- **Get outside at least once a day**, ideally in the morning or at lunch.

A timer or a recurring calendar reminder helps until breaks become a habit. If you have ongoing pain, stiffness, numbness or tingling, check it with a doctor or physiotherapist.

## Step 6: Take a real lunch

Eating at your desk while answering email is easy at home, and it wears you down over time. Treat lunch as an anchor:

- Step away from the desk and eat somewhere else.
- Leave your laptop closed.
- If you can, go outside for part of it.

## Step 7: Create a shutdown routine

Ending the day is where home routines break down most often. A shutdown routine closes the loop so you can stop thinking about work.

A simple version:

1. Check email and messages one last time.
2. Write down where you left off and what's first tomorrow.
3. Update your task list.
4. Close all work apps and tabs.
5. Tidy the desk.
6. Do something physical to end the day: shut the laptop and put it away, turn off the desk lamp, change clothes, go for a walk.

The last step matters. A physical action tells your brain the workday is over, the same way leaving an office did.

## A sample routine

This is one example to adapt, not a template to copy. Shift the times to suit your job, time zone and energy.

| Time | Activity |
|---|---|
| 7:00 | Wake up, get ready, breakfast |
| 8:15 | Start ritual: short walk, coffee, write top three tasks |
| 8:30 | Focus block 1 (hardest task) |
| 10:00 | Break: stretch, water, step outside |
| 10:15 | Meetings, email, collaborative work |
| 12:00 | Lunch away from the desk |
| 1:00 | Focus block 2 |
| 2:30 | Break: move, look at something far away |
| 2:45 | Admin, shallow tasks, calls |
| 4:45 | Shutdown routine |
| 5:00 | Work ends |

## Making it stick

- **Start small.** Pick two anchors, like start ritual and shutdown, and get those right before adding more.
- **Track it loosely.** A tick on a calendar is enough to see if you're keeping it.
- **Plan for bad days.** Decide what the minimum version of your routine looks like when things go wrong.
- **Review weekly.** Once a week, notice what worked and adjust one thing.
- **Tell the people you live with** when your focus blocks and stop time are, so they can help protect them.

## Common mistakes

- **Over-scheduling every minute.** One late meeting ruins the plan and you abandon it.
- **Starting work in bed or on your phone.** It blurs the line between rest and work.
- **Skipping breaks because you feel productive.** Energy and focus tend to drop later.
- **Having no end to the day.** Without a shutdown, work fills the evening.
- **Copying someone else's routine exactly.** Use it as a starting point.

## Bottom line

A good work-from-home routine is a handful of anchors that hold the day together: a consistent start, protected focus time, regular movement, a proper lunch and a clear end. Keep it simple, stick with it for a couple of weeks, and adjust as you learn when you work best.
