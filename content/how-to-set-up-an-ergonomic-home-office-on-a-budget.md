You can set up an ergonomic home office on a budget by fixing positions before buying anything: get your screen to eye level, your elbows near 90 degrees, your feet supported and your lower back filled in. Most of that can be done with books, a towel and a box. Then spend money only where a free fix falls short, which for most people means an external keyboard and mouse (if you use a laptop) and, later, a better chair.

## Start with the targets, not the gear

These are well-established starting points, not hard rules. Adjust until you feel relaxed.

| Body part | Target position | Common sign it's wrong |
|---|---|---|
| Eyes and monitor | Top of the screen at or slightly below eye level, about an arm's length away | Chin pokes forward, neck aches by mid-afternoon |
| Shoulders | Relaxed and down, not shrugged | Tension across the top of the shoulders |
| Elbows | Close to your sides, bent roughly 90 degrees or a bit more open | Reaching forward, or wrists bent up |
| Wrists | Straight and neutral, floating while you type | Wrists resting on a hard edge, bent back |
| Lower back | Supported in its natural inward curve | Slumping, sliding forward in the seat |
| Hips and knees | Hips level with or slightly above knees | Thighs sloping up, pressure behind the knees |
| Feet | Flat on the floor or a footrest | Feet dangling or tucked under the chair |

The trick is to set these up in the right order. Chair height comes first because it decides everything else.

## Step 1: Set your chair height to the desk (free)

Sit with your arms relaxed at your sides and bend your elbows. Your forearms should land at or just above the desk surface.

- **If the desk is too high**, raise the chair until your elbows reach desk height. This usually leaves your feet off the floor, which you'll fix in Step 3.
- **If the desk is too low**, lower the chair and keep the keyboard close to your body.
- **If your chair doesn't adjust at all**, a firm cushion or folded blanket on the seat adds height.

Sit all the way back. You should fit roughly two or three fingers between the seat edge and the back of your knees; if the seat is too deep, a cushion behind your back moves you forward.

## Step 2: Add lower back support (free)

Many budget and dining chairs have flat backs that let you slump. Roll up a towel and place it behind the small of your back, just above your belt line. It should fill the gap, not push you forward. A small firm pillow works too. Try a few thicknesses until it feels supportive without forcing an arch.

## Step 3: Support your feet (free)

If raising your chair left your feet dangling, put something solid under them. Good options you probably have:

- A stack of thick books or old binders
- A sturdy shoebox or storage box
- A step stool
- A yoga block

Your feet should sit flat with your thighs roughly parallel to the floor. If the pile of books keeps sliding around, a footrest is a cheap upgrade; [[footrests|browse adjustable footrests]] once you know the height you need.

## Step 4: Raise your screen (free for monitors, cheap for laptops)

This is the fix people most often skip, and it's the one behind a lot of neck and upper back complaints.

**For an external monitor:** stack books, a ream of paper or a sturdy box under it until the top of the screen sits at or just below eye level. Keep the stack stable and wider than the monitor's base.

**For a laptop:** raising the laptop solves the neck problem but creates a new one, because now you're typing with your hands up high. This is why a laptop setup almost always needs a separate keyboard and mouse (Step 5). Books work fine as a temporary stand.

Distance matters too. Sit back in your chair and stretch your arm out. Your fingertips should roughly touch the screen. If text feels small at that distance, increase the zoom or scaling in your operating system rather than leaning in.

If you wear bifocals or progressive lenses, you may need the screen a little lower than usual so you aren't tilting your head back to read through the bottom of your lenses.

## Step 5: Get your hands in the right place

Your keyboard should sit close enough that your elbows stay near your sides. The mouse should sit right next to the keyboard, not off to the far edge of the desk.

A few free adjustments:

- **Fold the keyboard feet down.** Flat or even slightly tilted away from you is usually better for keeping wrists straight than tilted up.
- **Move the mouse closer.** If you reach for it, your shoulder works all day.
- **Don't rest wrists on a hard desk edge while typing.** Let them float, and rest only during pauses.

### Where your first purchase should go

If you work on a laptop, the most useful thing to buy is an external keyboard and mouse. It's what makes raising the laptop screen practical. A basic wired set is inexpensive and does the job. If you're shopping, you can [[external-keyboards|compare external keyboards]] and look for:

- A low-profile or adjustable design that doesn't force your wrists up
- A compact layout (without the number pad) if you want the mouse closer to your body
- Wired for the lowest price, wireless for a tidier desk

If you already have wrist or forearm discomfort, a vertical mouse, which holds the forearm in a handshake position, is worth considering.

## Step 6: Fix your lighting and glare (mostly free)

Poor lighting makes you squint and lean forward, undoing your posture work.

- Put your screen at a right angle to windows, so daylight comes from the side rather than behind the screen or behind you.
- Close blinds partway if you see reflections on the screen.
- Match screen brightness to the room, and add a small lamp if the room is dim.

## Step 7: Build movement into your day (free)

No setup makes it fine to hold one position for hours.

- Stand up and move for a minute or two at least once every half hour to hour.
- Look away from the screen at something far away every so often to relax your eyes.
- Take phone calls standing or walking.

A kitchen counter gives you a free standing option for part of the day. Keep the same targets: screen near eye level, elbows near 90 degrees.

## What to buy, and in what order

Once the free fixes are in place, upgrade based on what still bothers you:

| Priority | Upgrade | Buy it if... |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | External keyboard and mouse | You work on a laptop |
| 2 | Laptop stand or monitor riser | Your book stack is wobbly or in the way |
| 3 | Footrest | Your feet still don't reach the floor |
| 4 | Office chair | It has no height adjustment, or you slump even with a towel roll |
| 5 | Monitor arm | You need more desk depth or finer height control |
| 6 | Standing desk or converter | You want to alternate sitting and standing regularly |

### Buying a chair on a budget

The chair is usually the biggest expense, so get the core features right. In order of importance:

1. **Seat height adjustment** that fits your desk and body
2. **Adjustable or well-shaped lumbar support**
3. **A seat depth that suits your leg length** (adjustable seat depth is a bonus)
4. **Armrests that go low enough** to slide under the desk, or that can be removed
5. **A stable five-star base** with casters suited to your floor

Skip extras like headrests and "gaming" styling, which add cost without helping most desk workers. Used office chairs from businesses that are downsizing can be a good-value option. Check that every adjustment works before you pay.

## Common budget setup mistakes

- **Buying a chair first** before checking whether a cushion and towel would solve the problem.
- **Working on the laptop screen without raising it**, then blaming the chair for neck pain.
- **Raising the laptop without adding a keyboard**, which moves the strain from the neck to the shoulders and wrists.
- **Setting chair height for the feet instead of the desk.** Set it for the desk, then support the feet.
- **Placing the mouse far from the keyboard**, which leaves the arm reaching all day.
- **Expecting perfect posture to fix everything.** Regular movement matters as much as position.

## When to see a professional

This guide is general information, not medical advice. If you have pain that lasts or gets worse, or any numbness, tingling or weakness in your hands, arms or legs, have it checked by a doctor or physiotherapist.

## Quick checklist

- Chair height set so elbows are level with the desk
- Sitting all the way back, lower back supported
- Feet flat on the floor or a solid support
- Top of the screen at or slightly below eye level, about an arm's length away
- Separate keyboard and mouse for laptops, mouse beside the keyboard
- Screen side-on to windows
- Up and moving at least once an hour

## Bottom line

A comfortable home office is mostly about positions, and positions are mostly free. Set your chair to the desk, support your back and feet, get the screen to eye level and keep your hands close. After that, spend money in order of need: an external keyboard and mouse for laptop users, then stands and footrests to replace wobbly stacks, and a good chair when your budget allows.
