The cheapest way to keep a home office warm in winter is to stop heat from escaping first, then warm your body directly, and only then heat the air in the room. In practice that means sealing drafts around the door and window, getting your feet off a cold floor, dressing in layers, and using a small, safety-certified space heater for the hours you actually sit there instead of cranking up the heat for the whole house.

Below is the order that usually gives the most comfort for the least money, plus the safety rules that matter when you add a heater to a room full of cables and paper.

## Why a home office feels colder than the rest of the house

Sitting still for hours changes how temperature feels. When you walk around, your muscles make heat. At a desk, your hands and feet cool down first, even when the thermostat says the house is fine. A few other things make offices especially chilly:

- **Spare rooms are often on the edges of the house.** Corner rooms, converted garages and rooms over garages have more outside walls and less insulation.
- **Doors stay closed.** That's good for focus, but it blocks warm air from the rest of the house.
- **Desks sit under windows.** Glass loses heat quickly, and the cold air sliding down the pane pools right at your feet.
- **Hard floors pull heat from your feet.** Tile, concrete and laminate feel far colder than carpet at the same temperature.

Most people are comfortable at roughly 68 to 72°F (20 to 22°C) for desk work. Your goal is to make the space around your body feel like that range, not necessarily the entire room.

## Step 1: Find and seal drafts (mostly free)

Heat you pay for is wasted if it leaks out. Spend ten minutes finding the leaks.

**How to find drafts:**

1. On a windy day, hold the back of your wet hand near window frames, the bottom of the door, outlets on outside walls and baseboards.
2. Or hold a lit incense stick nearby and watch whether the smoke wavers. Keep it well away from curtains and papers.
3. Check the gap under the door. If you can see daylight or hallway light, air is moving through it.

**Fixes, from cheapest to most involved:**

- **Door gap:** roll a towel or blanket along the bottom of the door, or use a fabric draft stopper.
- **Windows:** close curtains after dark. Thick or thermal-lined curtains that reach the floor help most. Clear shrink-film window insulation kits are inexpensive and nearly invisible once installed.
- **Frames and gaps:** self-adhesive foam weatherstripping around window sashes and the door frame.
- **Outlets on outside walls:** foam outlet gaskets fit behind the cover plate in a few minutes.
- **Floors:** a thick rug, even a secondhand one, makes a large difference over hard floors.

## Step 2: Warm yourself before warming the room

Your body is a far smaller target than the air in a room. Heating it directly is usually quicker and cheaper.

### Dress for sitting still

- **Base layer:** a thin thermal or merino top keeps heat in without bulk.
- **Warm feet:** wool socks and slippers with a solid sole. Cold feet make the whole body feel cold.
- **Hands:** fingerless gloves keep your palms and wrists warm while leaving fingertips free to type.
- **Lap blanket:** a throw over your legs traps a pocket of warm air under the desk.

### Get your feet off the cold floor

Floors are the coldest surface in most rooms, and your feet rest on them for hours. A few options:

- A folded rug or a piece of thick cardboard under your feet works as a free insulating layer.
- A footrest lifts your feet away from the coldest air near the floor and helps if your chair is set high for your desk. If you need one anyway, [[footrests|look at adjustable footrests]] with a solid platform.
- If you stand at your desk part of the day, a cushioned mat puts a layer of foam between your feet and cold tile. [[anti-fatigue-mats|Anti-fatigue mats]] do double duty here.

### Use personal heat sources

- **Heated throws and electric blankets** use far less power than a space heater because they heat you, not the room. Choose one with an automatic shut-off timer.
- **Heated foot warmers or under-desk heating panels** sit in the footwell and warm your legs.
- **A hot water bottle or microwavable heat pack** on your lap costs almost nothing. Check it for wear and never use boiling water.

## Step 3: Add a space heater, safely

If you're still cold, a small electric space heater is often cheaper than heating the whole house to keep one room comfortable. The idea is simple: lower the central thermostat a little and heat only the room you use.

### Which type suits a home office

| Heater type | How it feels | Best for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oil-filled radiator | Slow, gentle, silent | All-day use in a closed room | Takes a while to warm up |
| Ceramic fan heater | Quick burst of warm air | Short sessions, fast warm-up | Fan noise on video calls |
| Infrared/radiant | Warms objects and people in its path | Drafty rooms, sitting in one spot | Only warm where it points |
| Under-desk panel | Low, steady heat at your legs | Small budgets, cold feet | Won't warm the whole room |

For quiet video calls, an oil-filled radiator or radiant panel is usually the better pick. Fan heaters can be heard on some microphones.

### Safety rules that are worth following every time

- **Keep at least three feet (about a meter) of clear space** around the heater. That includes curtains, paper, the trash can and your bag.
- **Plug it directly into a wall outlet.** Most plug-in space heaters draw up to about 1,500 watts, which is too much for many power strips and extension cords.
- **Look for a tip-over switch and overheat protection,** plus a recognized safety certification mark on the label.
- **Put it on a hard, level surface,** not on a rug or under the desk where cables and papers collect.
- **Turn it off when you leave the room** and before bed.
- **Never use unvented fuel-burning heaters indoors.** Propane or kerosene heaters meant for outdoors can produce carbon monoxide. Make sure your home has working smoke and carbon monoxide alarms, and don't seal every gap in a room that has a gas or oil appliance; it needs ventilation.

### Estimating what it costs to run

You don't need a price table to work this out. A heater's power in kilowatts, multiplied by the hours it runs, gives kilowatt-hours (kWh). Multiply that by the rate on your electricity bill.

For example: a heater running at 1.5 kW for 4 hours uses 6 kWh. Most heaters cycle on and off once the room is warm, so real use is often lower, especially with a thermostat setting.

For one person working in one room, that often costs less than heating the whole house.

## Step 4: Keep the air comfortable

Winter air is often dry, which can make your eyes, throat and skin feel irritated, especially while staring at a screen. Many people feel best when indoor humidity sits around 30 to 50 percent. A cheap hygrometer tells you where you are. If it's low:

- A small humidifier helps. Clean it regularly according to the instructions.

Don't go overboard: high humidity on cold windows leads to condensation and mold.

Also, open a window for a few minutes once or twice a day. A short burst of fresh air clears stuffiness without chilling the room much, since walls and furniture hold their heat.

## Step 5: Rearrange the room if you can

Small layout changes can make a big difference:

- **Move the desk away from the window** or at least pull it a foot or two back. You'll still get daylight without sitting in the cold downdraft.
- **Keep the desk off outside walls** if there's an inside wall available.
- **Don't block radiators or heating vents** with the desk, a bookcase or a filing cabinet.

## Keep moving, too

Cold makes muscles tense, and tense shoulders and neck add to the stiffness many people feel at a desk in winter. Get up at least once an hour, walk around, do a few shoulder rolls and squats. It warms you up and gives your eyes a break. If you notice persistent pain, numbness or tingling in your hands or feet, or fingers that turn white or blue in the cold, get it checked by a doctor.

## Quick checklist

- Seal the door gap and windows; close curtains after dark
- Add a rug or insulating layer under your feet
- Wear a thin base layer, wool socks and fingerless gloves
- Use a heated throw or foot warmer before a room heater
- If you add a space heater: three feet of clearance, wall outlet, tip-over switch, off when you leave
- Keep humidity roughly between 30 and 50 percent
- Move the desk away from cold windows and outside walls
- Get up and move once an hour

Start with the free fixes this week. Most home offices go from chilly to comfortable with a draft stopper, a rug and warmer socks, and a small heater covers the rest.
