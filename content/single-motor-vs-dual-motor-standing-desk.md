A single motor standing desk is enough for a light setup: a laptop, one monitor, a lamp and a few accessories on a modest-size top. A dual motor desk is the better buy if you plan to carry two or more monitors, a desktop tower, a heavy or large desktop, or you want smoother, quieter and more even lifting over years of use. The motor count affects capacity, speed, noise and longevity more than it affects wobble, which comes mainly from the frame design.

## How the two designs differ

**Single motor desks** have one motor, usually in one leg or under the center of the frame. A drive shaft (a long rod) runs across the frame to the other leg so both legs rise together.

**Dual motor desks** have a motor in each leg. A control box keeps the two in sync so the top stays level. There's no connecting drive shaft.

That difference drives almost everything else.

## Quick comparison

| | Single motor | Dual motor |
|---|---|---|
| Lifting capacity | Lower | Higher |
| Typical speed | Slower | Faster |
| Noise | Often louder, with a whine from the drive shaft | Often quieter, since each motor does less work |
| Smoothness under load | Can slow or strain near the limit | More consistent |
| Frame sizes | Usually smaller tops | Supports larger and heavier tops |
| Price | Lower | Higher |
| Weight to move | Lighter | Heavier |
| Things that can wear | One motor, drive shaft and gearbox | Two motors, sync electronics |

Exact capacity, speed and noise figures vary by model, so always check the listed specs rather than assuming.

## Lifting capacity: the deciding factor

Most people should decide based on weight.

### Add up your load

Estimate everything that will sit on the desk:

- **The desktop itself.** Solid wood and large tops are much heavier than thin laminate or particle board.
- **Monitors and arms.** Large screens and heavy-duty arms add up quickly.
- **A computer tower** if it sits on the desk or hangs from it.
- **Speakers, lamps, docks, printers, plants and books.**
- **Leaning.** People lean on desks, and some rest forearms on the front edge. That's extra load too.

If you're unsure of weights, check the product pages or manuals for your monitors and tower, and weigh smaller items on a bathroom scale.

### Leave a margin

A motor working near its maximum rating runs louder, slower and hotter, and is likely to wear out sooner. A sensible rule is to keep your total load comfortably below the listed capacity, with plenty of headroom for things you'll add later. If your current load is already approaching the single motor limit, go dual.

## Speed and noise

Dual motor desks usually rise and lower faster, because two motors share the work. That sounds minor, but it matters for habits: if changing height takes a long time or makes an unpleasant whine, you're less likely to do it several times a day.

Noise depends on the motors, gearing and the frame, but single motor designs often have more mechanical noise from the drive shaft. If you share a room or take calls while adjusting the desk, a quieter frame is worth paying for. Look for a listed noise level in decibels and compare like with like.

## Stability and wobble

People often assume dual motor means less wobble. Motors aren't the main factor. Stability comes from:

- **Leg stages.** Three-stage legs (three telescoping sections) extend higher and often feel sturdier at full height than two-stage legs, though good two-stage designs can be very stable.
- **Leg shape and thickness.** Wider, rectangular legs with tight tolerances between the sections wobble less.
- **Crossbars and frame design.** A well-built crossbar or rigid frame reduces side-to-side sway.
- **Feet width.** Wider feet resist tipping and sway.
- **Height.** Every desk wobbles more near its maximum height.
- **Desktop size.** A large top on a small frame magnifies movement.

That said, dual motor frames tend to be built heavier overall, and heavier frames often sway less. Treat it as a correlation, not a guarantee.

## Height range

Check that both the lowest and highest heights suit you. Many people overlook the minimum:

- **Seated:** the surface should sit at or just below your elbow height when seated with shoulders relaxed.
- **Standing:** the surface should sit at or just below your elbow height when standing in the shoes you usually wear.

Shorter people often need a low minimum height, and taller people need a high maximum. Three-stage legs usually give a wider range, and they're more common on dual motor frames, though you'll find exceptions both ways.

## Reliability and repairs

Both designs can last many years. Things to consider:

- **Single motor:** fewer electronics, but the drive shaft and gearbox are extra moving parts that can wear or loosen.
- **Dual motor:** no drive shaft, but two motors and synchronization electronics. If one motor fails, the desk won't move until it's replaced.

In practice, the warranty is the most useful reliability signal. Look for separate warranty lengths on the frame, motors and electronics, and check whether replacement parts can be ordered.

## Features that matter as much as motor count

- **Memory presets** so you can hit your sitting and standing heights with one button.
- **Collision detection** that stops or reverses the desk if it hits something like a chair arm or windowsill.
- **Soft start and stop** for smoother movement with monitors on top.
- **A child lock** if you have kids or pets.
- **Cable management,** since cables need slack to travel with the desk. An under-desk [[cable-trays|cable tray]] keeps cords from snagging.

## Who should choose which

**Single motor is fine if:**
- You use a laptop or one monitor.
- Your desktop is small to medium and not solid hardwood.
- You're on a tight budget and your total load is well below the limit.
- You adjust height once or twice a day.

**Go dual motor if:**
- You have two or more monitors, especially on arms.
- You keep a tower PC, speakers or a printer on the desk.
- You want a large or heavy top.
- You want faster, quieter adjustments.
- You're tall and need a higher maximum height, or you need a wide height range.

## Not sure you'll stand? Try it free

If you're not sure you'll use a standing desk at all, test the habit first. Put your laptop and an external keyboard on a kitchen counter, a high dresser or a sturdy box on your desk for an hour a day for a week or two. If you find yourself standing regularly, then invest. A desk converter that sits on your current desk is another lower-commitment option.

Remember that the goal isn't to stand all day. Alternating between sitting and standing, and moving around, is what helps most. When standing, an [[anti-fatigue-mats|anti-fatigue mat]] can make longer stretches more comfortable on hard floors.

## Common mistakes

- **Buying for today's setup only.** Many people add a second monitor later.
- **Ignoring the desktop weight.** A heavy solid top can take a big share of a single motor's capacity.
- **Assuming dual motor fixes wobble.** Check the frame design and legs.
- **Forgetting cable slack.** Short cables can yank equipment off the desk when it rises.
- **Setting the standing height too high.** Your elbows should still be around 90 degrees with relaxed shoulders.

## Bottom line

- **Light setup, one screen, modest budget:** a single motor desk does the job.
- **Multiple monitors, heavy top or long-term investment:** dual motor is worth the extra cost.
- **Worried about wobble:** focus on the frame, legs and crossbar, not only the motor count.

If you notice leg, back or foot pain when standing, alternate more often and consider supportive shoes or a mat. Persistent pain, numbness or tingling should be checked by a doctor or physiotherapist.
