Most home office glare disappears once you turn your desk so the window is beside you instead of behind or in front of you, tilt the screen slightly, and replace harsh overhead light with softer, indirect light. Those three changes are free or close to it. If glare persists after that, a matte screen protector, better window coverings or a well-designed desk light can handle what's left.

The trick is to diagnose first. Glare always comes from a specific light source hitting your screen or your eyes, and once you know which one, the fix is usually obvious.

## Two kinds of glare

It helps to separate these, because they have different fixes.

**Reflected glare** is light bouncing off the screen into your eyes. You see a bright patch, a window shape, or your own silhouette on the display. Glossy screens show this the most.

**Direct glare** is a bright light in your field of view while you look at the screen, such as a window behind the monitor or an exposed bulb. Nothing is reflecting; your eyes are just trying to handle two very different brightness levels at once. It causes squinting and fatigue even though the screen itself looks clean.

## Find your glare source in two minutes

1. **Turn the monitor off** during the time of day when glare bothers you.
2. **Sit in your normal position** and look at the black screen. It works like a mirror. Whatever bright shapes you see (windows, ceiling lights, a lamp, a white wall) are your reflected glare sources.
3. **Look just over the top of the monitor.** Anything bright there is direct glare.
4. **Repeat at a different time of day** if the problem comes and goes. Morning and late afternoon sun often cause very different issues.

Write down what you find. Then work through the fixes below in order, starting with the free ones.

## Free fixes

### Put windows to the side

The best position for a desk is usually perpendicular to the window, so daylight comes from your left or right.

- **Window behind you:** it reflects directly in the screen. This is the most common cause of glare.
- **Window behind the monitor:** you stare into bright daylight while trying to read a dimmer screen. Direct glare and eye strain follow.
- **Window to the side:** light falls across the room without bouncing straight into your eyes.

If you can only move the desk a little, even rotating it by 30 or 45 degrees can shift a reflection off the screen.

### Tilt and swivel the screen

Reflections follow simple angles. Tipping the top of the screen back a bit, or swiveling it a few degrees, often moves a ceiling light or window out of the reflection zone. Keep the top of the screen at or slightly below eye level and about an arm's length away while you do this, so you don't trade glare for neck strain.

### Turn off or move the overhead light

A single bright ceiling fixture directly above or behind you is a classic glare source. Try working with it off and using lamps instead. If you need it, check whether it has a diffuser cover, or swap the bulb for a lower-output one.

### Adjust screen brightness to the room

A screen much brighter or much dimmer than its surroundings is tiring. In a bright room, raise brightness enough that the screen doesn't look washed out. In a dim room, lower it so it doesn't glow like a light box. A quick test: a blank white document should look like a sheet of paper in the room, not like a lamp.

### Change what's on the walls

A glossy picture frame or white wall behind you can reflect in the screen. Moving a frame or hanging something matte behind your chair helps. This matters most on video calls too, since the same surfaces reflect in your glasses.

### Use dark mode selectively

Dark backgrounds make reflections more visible, because reflections stand out against black. If you see your own face in the screen, light mode may actually be more comfortable during the day.

## Low-cost fixes

### Control the window

- **Sheer curtains or light-filtering shades** soften daylight without darkening the room.
- **Blinds** let you angle slats to bounce light toward the ceiling instead of the screen.
- **Blackout or room-darkening shades** are useful for a west-facing window in the afternoon, but pair them with good indoor lighting so the room isn't dim.
- **Window film** that diffuses light is an option for renters who can't install hardware; look for removable, static-cling types.

### Add soft, indirect lighting

Glare often comes from too much light from one bright point. Several softer sources are easier on your eyes:

- A floor lamp that bounces light off the ceiling.
- A desk lamp placed to the side and shaded so the bulb isn't visible from your seat.
- Neutral to warm color temperatures in the evening, cooler neutral white during the day if you prefer.

A desk lamp should light your desk and paperwork, not the screen. Position it on the side opposite your writing hand and angle the head down. If you're shopping for one, look for adjustable brightness, an asymmetric or well-shielded head, and good color rendering. You can [[desk-lamps|browse adjustable desk lamps]] with those features.

### Try a monitor light bar

A monitor light bar clips onto the top of the screen and throws light down onto the desk at an angle designed to avoid hitting the display. It's a tidy fix if your desk is dim but lamps keep reflecting in the screen. [[monitor-light-bars|Monitor light bars]] work best on flat, fairly thin monitor tops; check compatibility if your screen is curved or has a thick bezel.

## Screen-level fixes

### Matte vs glossy displays

Glossy screens look punchy but act like mirrors. Matte (anti-glare) coatings scatter reflections so they turn into a soft haze instead of a sharp image. If you're replacing a monitor anyway and your room has unavoidable windows, a matte finish is the safer choice.

### Anti-glare screen protectors

A matte film applied over a glossy laptop or monitor screen can reduce reflections considerably. Trade-offs to know:

- Text can look slightly less crisp.
- Colors may look a little softer.
- Bubbles and dust are common if you apply it carelessly; clean the screen thoroughly first.

For color-critical work, fix the room before changing the screen surface.

### Hoods and shades

Some people who work near large windows use a monitor hood, which is a three-sided shade that blocks side light. It's rarely needed for normal office work, but it can help in a sunroom or with a desk you truly can't move.

## Quick diagnosis table

| What you notice | Likely source | First fix to try |
|---|---|---|
| Window shape in the screen | Window behind you | Turn desk so the window is at your side |
| Bright bar or spot at the top of the screen | Ceiling light | Tilt screen back slightly; turn off overhead light |
| Squinting, screen looks dim | Window behind the monitor | Move desk or close sheer curtains |
| Your own face in the screen | Bright room, dark screen content | Raise brightness or switch to light mode |
| Glare only in the afternoon | Low sun | Blinds or light-filtering shades for those hours |
| Hot spot on the desk and screen | Desk lamp aimed too high | Lower and shield the lamp, move it to the side |

## Common mistakes

- **Cranking brightness to beat glare.** This makes the screen harsher without removing the reflection.
- **Working in a dark room with a bright screen.** It's comfortable for a few minutes and tiring after an hour.
- **Placing a lamp behind the monitor.** You're looking straight into it.
- **Buying a screen filter before moving the desk.** Placement solves most glare for free.
- **Forgetting glasses.** Lenses reflect light too. If you wear glasses, an anti-reflective coating is worth asking your eye care provider about.

## When it's more than glare

Tired, dry or burning eyes can come from glare, but also from not blinking enough, an outdated glasses prescription or a screen that's too close. Every 20 minutes or so, look at something across the room for a few seconds. If eye strain, headaches or blurry vision continue after you've improved your lighting, see an optometrist or doctor.

## Quick checklist

- Turn the monitor off and look for reflections to find the source.
- Put windows to the side of your desk.
- Tilt the screen slightly back and adjust the swivel.
- Turn off harsh overhead lights; use softer lamps and indirect light.
- Match screen brightness to the room.
- Add sheers, blinds or light-filtering shades for problem hours.
- Consider a matte screen or anti-glare film only after fixing the room.
