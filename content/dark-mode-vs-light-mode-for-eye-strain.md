Neither dark mode nor light mode is clearly better for eye strain for everyone. What matters more is matching your screen to your surroundings. In a bright room, light mode (dark text on a light background) tends to be easier to read and makes small text sharper for most people. In a dim room or at night, dark mode can feel more comfortable because the screen isn't a glowing rectangle in the dark. If you have astigmatism, you may find light text on a dark background looks fuzzy or haloed. Choose the mode that feels comfortable for your lighting, then fix the bigger causes of strain: brightness, glare, text size, and breaks.

## The case for light mode

Light mode is dark text on a light background, like print on paper.

- **Sharper text for many people.** In a bright room, the bright screen makes your pupils constrict. A smaller pupil generally gives a sharper image with better depth of focus. Many people find reading easier and faster this way.
- **Better for long reading.** Documents, articles, and dense text often feel easier in light mode.
- **Matches a bright room.** A light screen in a well-lit room has similar brightness to its surroundings, so your eyes don't keep adapting.
- **Works better with astigmatism.** Light text on a dark background can appear to bleed or glow for people with astigmatism, making dark mode harder to read.

The downside: at night or in a dark room, a bright white screen can feel glaring and uncomfortable, and bright light late in the evening may make it harder to wind down.

## The case for dark mode

Dark mode is light text on a dark background.

- **Comfort in dim rooms.** Less total light from the screen feels gentler when the room is dark.
- **Less glare at night.** A mostly dark screen doesn't light up a dark bedroom or office.
- **Preferred by some for coding.** Many developers like dark themes for syntax highlighting. Colored text often stands out well on a dark background.
- **Battery savings on OLED screens.** On phones and laptops with OLED panels, dark pixels use less power. This doesn't apply to most LCD monitors.
- **Light sensitivity.** People sensitive to bright light may simply find dark mode more comfortable.

The downside: small, thin text can be harder to read, especially for people with astigmatism, and in a bright room a dark screen reflects more of the room.

## Side-by-side comparison

| Factor | Light mode | Dark mode |
|---|---|---|
| Bright room | Usually better | Reflections more visible |
| Dim room or night | Can feel glaring | Usually more comfortable |
| Long reading | Often easier | Can be harder for small text |
| Astigmatism | Usually clearer | Text may look fuzzy or haloed |
| Coding | Fine | Popular, largely a preference |
| OLED battery | Uses more | Uses less |
| Light sensitivity | Can be uncomfortable | Often more comfortable |

## The real rule: match your lighting

The biggest source of discomfort is a large difference between screen brightness and room brightness. A blazing white screen in a dark room is tiring. So is a dim, dark screen in a sunny room where you strain to read.

**Aim for this:** a white page on screen should look like a sheet of paper in the room. Not glowing, not gray.

Practical steps:

- **Use auto mode.** Most operating systems can switch between light and dark based on time of day. Pair this with automatic brightness if your device has it.
- **Adjust brightness first.** Many people run monitors much brighter than needed. Lowering brightness in a normal room often helps more than switching modes.
- **Add room light at night.** Instead of switching to dark mode in a pitch-black room, add a lamp. A soft light behind or beside the screen reduces contrast. A [[monitor-light-bars|monitor light bar]] lights the desk without shining on the screen.
- **Use night mode in the evening.** A warmer color temperature later in the day is comfortable for many people, whichever mode you use.

## Settings that matter more than the mode

Switching modes is a small adjustment. These make a bigger difference to eye strain:

### Text size and contrast

- **Increase text size** so you don't lean in or squint.
- **Avoid low-contrast themes.** Gray text on gray looks stylish but strains eyes. In dark mode, choose off-white text on a very dark gray background rather than pure white on pure black, which some people find harsh.
- **Use crisp fonts** at your monitor's native resolution.

### Screen position

- **Top of the screen at or slightly below eye level,** about an arm's length away.
- **Tilt it slightly back.**
- **Keep windows to the side,** not behind the screen or behind you.

Free fix for laptops: raise the laptop on books and use an external keyboard and mouse. If you do this daily, a [[laptop-stands|laptop stand]] makes it more stable.

### Breaks and blinking

- **Look far away regularly.** The 20-20-20 guideline (every 20 minutes, look about 20 feet away for 20 seconds) is an easy habit.
- **Blink fully** when you look away. People blink less when concentrating on screens.
- **Get up every hour or two.**

### Your eyes

A small uncorrected prescription or glasses not suited to your screen distance can cause strain no matter which mode you choose. Tell your eye doctor how much screen time you have and how far away your screen sits.

## Which mode should you pick?

- **You work in a bright room, reading lots of text:** light mode, with brightness matched to the room.
- **You work in the evening or a dim room:** dark mode, or light mode with lower brightness and room lighting.
- **You have astigmatism:** try light mode first. If you prefer dark mode, increase font size and weight.
- **You code:** either works. Pick a theme with good contrast and readable colors.
- **You're light sensitive or get headaches from bright screens:** dark mode may be more comfortable. Talk to a doctor if headaches are frequent.
- **You can't decide:** use automatic switching and see what feels better after a week.

## Try this one-week test

1. Use light mode for a few days in your normal setup, with brightness matched to the room.
2. Switch to dark mode for a few days.
3. Note headaches, tired eyes, reading speed, and comfort at the end of the day.
4. Keep what felt better, or use auto switching for both.

## Common mistakes

- **Using dark mode to fix a too-bright screen.** Lower the brightness instead.
- **Working in a dark room** with any screen mode.
- **Choosing low-contrast themes** for looks.
- **Assuming the mode fixes eye strain,** and skipping breaks and positioning.

## When to see an eye doctor

See an eye doctor if you have persistent eye strain, blurry vision, frequent headaches, double vision, or eye pain. These can have causes a screen setting won't fix.

## Bottom line

Dark mode vs light mode is mostly about comfort and lighting, not eye health. Light mode usually reads better in bright rooms; dark mode is often more comfortable in dim ones. Match screen brightness to the room, keep text large and contrast clear, position your screen well, take breaks, and keep your eye prescription current.
