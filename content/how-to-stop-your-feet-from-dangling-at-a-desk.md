Your feet dangle because your chair is set high enough for your arms to reach the desk, but your legs are shorter than that height. The fix is to bring the floor up to your feet: put something stable under them, like a sturdy box, a stack of books or a footrest, so your feet sit flat with your knees at roughly hip height. Don't solve it by lowering the chair, because that pushes your arms and shoulders into a worse position.

## Why dangling feet are worth fixing

It feels like a small thing, but unsupported feet change how you sit for the whole day.

- **Pressure behind the knees.** When your feet can't reach the floor, the front edge of the seat carries the weight of your legs. That presses on the back of your thighs, which many people notice as numbness, heaviness or cold feet after a while.
- **Sliding forward.** To reach the floor, most people scoot to the front of the seat. That takes your back off the backrest and away from any lumbar support.
- **Awkward leg positions.** Crossing your legs, tucking your feet under the chair or hooking them on the chair base are all ways the body tries to find support. They're fine for a minute, not for hours.
- **Restless fidgeting.** Constantly shifting because nothing feels stable is tiring, even if you don't notice it.

If you regularly get numbness, tingling or swelling in your legs or feet, don't just treat it as a furniture problem. Get it checked by a doctor or physiotherapist.

## First, check that the chair height is right

Before fixing your feet, make sure the chair is at the height your upper body needs. Otherwise you'll set up a footrest for the wrong position.

1. Sit back fully in the chair so your lower back touches the backrest.
2. Let your arms hang, then bend your elbows to about 90 degrees.
3. Raise or lower the seat until your forearms are level with the desk surface or just above it, with your shoulders relaxed.
4. Now look at your feet. If they're flat on the floor with your knees roughly level with your hips, you're done. If they hang, or only your toes touch, keep reading.

The gap between the bottom of your heels and the floor is the height you need to fill. Measure it with a tape measure while sitting in your normal working position. Most people find they need somewhere between one and five inches.

## Free fixes you can do today

You probably already own something that works. The requirements are simple: it must be stable, it must not slide, and it should be wide enough for both feet.

### Books or binders

A stack of hardcover books is easy to adjust an inch at a time. Put the biggest ones on the bottom and tape the stack together if it shifts. Two large ring binders placed spine-toward-you give you a gentle slope, which many people find more comfortable than a flat surface.

### A sturdy box

A shipping box packed with paper or old magazines makes a solid platform. Tape it shut so it doesn't crush in the middle. This works well if you need a bigger lift, around four inches or more.

### A yoga block or step

Foam yoga blocks can be placed on their side or flat to give you a few height options. A low step stool also works if the height is right, though many are taller than you need.

### What to avoid

- **Anything that rolls or wobbles**, like a round cushion or a stack of loose magazines on carpet.
- **Your chair's star base.** Resting your feet on the base raises your knees but puts your weight on your toes.
- **A trash can or storage bin** that could tip.

## When a footrest is worth buying

A free fix is fine as a test. Once you know the right height, a proper footrest has a few advantages: a non-slip surface, a tilt that lets your ankles move, and a fixed shape that stays where you put it. If you find yourself adjusting your book pile every morning, it's time to [[footrests|look at adjustable footrests]].

### What to look for

| Feature | Why it matters | Who needs it most |
|---|---|---|
| Adjustable height | Lets you match the exact gap between your feet and the floor | Anyone who shares a desk or changes chairs |
| Adjustable tilt | A slight upward angle is gentler on the ankles | People who sit for long stretches |
| Wide platform | Room to shift your feet and change position | Taller people, people who move a lot |
| Textured or grippy top | Stops socks and shoes from sliding | People on hard floors |
| Rocking option | Encourages small ankle movements through the day | Fidgeters, people with stiff calves |
| Firm base | Stays put on carpet and hard floors | Everyone |

### Hard or soft?

- **Hard platforms** (plastic, wood, metal) are more stable and usually adjustable. They suit people who wear shoes at their desk.
- **Foam or cushioned footrests** feel pleasant in socks and are easy to push aside. They compress over time, so check the height is still right after a few months.

## How to set the footrest height

1. Sit back in your chair at the height you set earlier.
2. Place the footrest where your feet land naturally, with your knees bent roughly 90 degrees or slightly more open.
3. Your thighs should be about parallel with the floor, or your knees slightly lower than your hips.
4. You should be able to slide a couple of fingers under the front of your thigh, just behind the knee. If you can't, the footrest is too low or the seat is too deep.
5. If your knees rise above your hips, the footrest is too high. Lower it or take a book off the stack.

A good sign is that you stop noticing your feet at all.

## Other causes of dangling feet

Sometimes the problem isn't only the desk height.

### The seat is too deep

If your chair's seat is long compared to your thighs, you'll slide forward to bend your knees, and your feet will leave the floor. Check this by sitting all the way back: you want a gap of about two to four fingers between the seat edge and the back of your knees. If there's no gap, try a seat depth adjustment if your chair has one, or add a firm cushion or rolled towel behind your lower back to move you forward.

### The desk is too high for you

Standard desks are around 29 to 30 inches tall, which suits taller people better than shorter ones. If you need a big footrest just to keep your arms level with the desk, the desk may be the real issue. A keyboard tray that sits below the desk surface, or a height-adjustable desk, lets you lower your work surface and your chair together so your feet reach the floor on their own.

### You're sharing a chair

If someone else uses the same setup, a footrest with adjustable height is the easiest way to switch between users without resetting everything.

## Common mistakes

- **Lowering the chair to reach the floor.** Your feet touch down, but now the desk is at chest height and your shoulders shrug to reach the keyboard.
- **Making the footrest too high.** Knees up near your chest put pressure on your hips and lower back.
- **Placing the footrest too far forward.** You end up with straight legs and your weight sliding toward the seat edge.
- **Using the footrest only sometimes.** If it's kicked aside half the day, it's in the wrong spot or at the wrong height.
- **Forgetting to move.** Even perfect support doesn't replace standing up, walking around and stretching every half hour or so.

## Quick checklist

- Chair height set so forearms are level with the desk and shoulders are relaxed.
- Back against the backrest, not perched on the front edge.
- Feet flat on a stable surface, not dangling or tucked back.
- Knees at or slightly below hip height.
- A small gap between the seat edge and the back of the knees.
- Start with books or a box, then buy an adjustable footrest once you know your height.
- Persistent numbness, tingling or swelling gets checked by a professional.

## Bottom line

Dangling feet are almost always a sign that your chair is correctly set for your arms but too high for your legs. Keep the chair where it is and raise the floor. A pile of books or a sturdy box is a perfectly good start. If it helps, a stable, adjustable footrest with a non-slip top is a small upgrade that tidies up the whole setup.
