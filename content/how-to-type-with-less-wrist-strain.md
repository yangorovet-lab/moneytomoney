To type with less wrist strain, keep your wrists roughly straight in every direction, let your hands hover over the keys instead of anchoring the heels of your palms, press keys only as hard as needed, and put the keyboard at or slightly below elbow height so your forearms can stay level. Most wrist strain from typing comes from small bends held for hours, not from any single dramatic mistake. Fix the angles first, then the habits, and only then think about new equipment.

## What actually strains your wrists

Your wrist is happiest in a neutral position: the back of your hand in line with your forearm, not bent up, down, or sideways. Typing tends to push it out of that position in four ways:

- **Extension (bending up).** Happens when the keyboard is too high, when the rear feet are flipped out, or when you rest your palms on the desk and lift your fingers to reach the keys.
- **Ulnar deviation (bending toward the pinky).** A regular keyboard is narrower than your shoulders, so your hands angle outward to meet it.
- **Contact pressure.** Resting the underside of the wrist on a hard desk edge presses on the area where tendons and the median nerve pass through.
- **Static load and force.** Holding the same position for a long time, and hammering keys all the way down, keeps forearm muscles working without a break.

You don't need to eliminate all four perfectly. Reducing each one a little adds up.

## Step 1: Get the height right

Height sets the angle of everything else, so start here.

1. **Sit with your shoulders relaxed and upper arms hanging close to your sides.**
2. **Bend your elbows to about 90 degrees**, or slightly more open (90 to 110 degrees is comfortable for most people).
3. **The home row of the keyboard should sit at or just below elbow height.** If you have to lift your forearms or shrug to reach it, it's too high.

On a standard desk, many people find the keyboard is too high because the desk was made for writing, not typing. Your options, cheapest first:

- **Raise your chair** until your elbows are level with the keys, then support your feet with a box, a stack of books, or a sturdy step if they no longer rest flat.
- **Use a keyboard tray** that mounts under the desk and sits lower than the desktop.
- **Lower the desk** if it's adjustable.

If raising your chair leaves your feet dangling, a [[footrests|simple adjustable footrest]] is a cheap way to keep the higher chair position comfortable.

## Step 2: Flatten the keyboard

Most keyboards come with little feet at the back that tilt the keys toward you. It looks natural, but it usually bends the wrists upward.

- **Fold the rear feet in.** A flat keyboard is better than a tilted one for most people.
- **Consider a slight negative tilt** (front edge higher than the back) if your keyboard sits at lap height or below elbow level. This lets your wrists stay straight as your hands drop toward the keys.
- **Watch thick keyboards.** A tall keyboard forces your wrists up even when it's flat. A palm rest of the same height can help here, used correctly (see below).

## Step 3: Float, don't anchor

This is the habit that makes the biggest difference for many typists.

When you anchor the heels of your hands on the desk or a wrist rest and type by lifting your fingers, your wrists bend up and stay bent. Instead:

- **Let your hands hover** while you're actively typing, moving from the elbow and shoulder to reach keys instead of stretching fingers from a fixed point.
- **Use a palm rest only during pauses.** If you have one, it's for resting between bursts, and it should touch the fleshy base of your palm, not the underside of your wrist.
- **Reach for far keys by moving your hand**, not by twisting the wrist sideways or stretching a pinky to its limit.

Floating feels tiring for the first few days if you're used to anchoring. Keep sessions short while you adjust.

## Step 4: Straighten sideways angles

Ulnar deviation is easy to miss because it's a side-to-side bend. Look down at your hands on the home row. If your wrists bend outward so your fingers point toward the center, try these:

- **Bring your elbows slightly out from your body** so your forearms angle in toward the keyboard. Your wrists can then stay straight.
- **Center the keyboard's typing area on your body**, not the whole keyboard. On a full-size board, the "B" key should be roughly in front of your belly button. The number pad pushes the letter keys to the left.
- **Try a compact or split layout** if you still bend. A tenkeyless keyboard removes the number pad, which also lets your mouse sit closer. A split keyboard lets each half sit in line with each forearm.

## Step 5: Type lighter

Many people press keys much harder than needed and "bottom out" on every stroke. That force travels back through the fingers into the forearm.

- **Listen to yourself type.** Loud clacking often means you're striking hard.
- **Aim to press just past the point where the key registers.** On most keyboards that's well before the key hits the bottom.
- **Relax your thumbs and pinkies.** Tension there tends to spread into the whole hand.
- **Use keyboard shortcuts** to cut repetitive mouse clicks, and text expansion for phrases you type often.

## Step 6: Keep the mouse close

Wrist strain from typing often gets blamed on the keyboard when the mouse is doing half the damage. If you reach far to the right past a number pad, your arm and wrist sit at an awkward angle all day.

- Keep the mouse at the same height as the keyboard and as close to it as possible.
- Move the mouse with your forearm, not just by flicking the wrist.
- Switch hands occasionally if you can manage it.

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | What it does | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Rear keyboard feet flipped out | Bends wrists upward | Fold them in |
| Palms planted while typing | Holds wrists in extension | Hover while typing, rest during pauses |
| Wrists on the desk edge | Presses on the inside of the wrist | Pull the keyboard back from the edge or use a soft palm rest |
| Keyboard too high | Lifts forearms and bends wrists | Raise the chair or use a keyboard tray |
| Laptop keyboard all day | Hands close together, screen too low | Add an external keyboard |
| No breaks | Muscles never get to relax | Short pauses every 20 to 30 minutes |

## Laptop users: a special case

A laptop forces a tradeoff: if the screen is at a good height, the keyboard is too high, and if the keyboard is at a good height, the screen is too low. The laptop keyboard is also small, which squeezes your hands together.

The standard fix is to raise the laptop on books or a stand so the screen is near eye level, then use a separate keyboard and mouse at elbow height. An [[external-keyboards|external keyboard]] is one of the most useful upgrades for anyone typing on a laptop for hours a day, and it doesn't have to be fancy. A flat, full-width board already beats the laptop's built-in one for wrist angles.

## Breaks and simple stretches

Even perfect posture becomes a problem if you hold it for three hours straight. Build small breaks in:

- **Micro-pauses:** drop your hands into your lap for a few seconds whenever you stop to think.
- **Short breaks:** every 20 to 30 minutes, stand up, shake out your hands, and let your arms hang.
- **Gentle stretches:** extend one arm with the palm up and gently pull the fingers back with the other hand, then repeat palm down. Hold lightly for 15 to 20 seconds. Stretching should feel mild, never painful.

Timer apps or a simple kitchen timer can remind you until breaks become habit.

## When to see a professional

This article is general information, not medical advice. If you have pain that lasts more than a couple of weeks, pain that wakes you at night, numbness or tingling in your fingers, weakness in your grip, or you drop things more than usual, see a doctor or physiotherapist. Those signs can point to problems that need proper assessment, and early treatment usually works better than waiting.

## Quick checklist

- Elbows about 90 to 110 degrees, shoulders relaxed
- Home row at or slightly below elbow height
- Keyboard flat, rear feet folded in
- Hands hovering while typing, resting only during pauses
- Wrists straight side to side; letter keys centered on your body
- Light keystrokes, no bottoming out
- Mouse right next to the keyboard, at the same height
- A short break every 20 to 30 minutes

## Bottom line

Straight wrists, the right height, a floating typing style, and a lighter touch fix most typing-related wrist strain without spending anything. If you work from a laptop or your hands still angle outward after adjusting, an external or compact keyboard is the first purchase worth making. Anything beyond that, like split or tented keyboards, is worth considering only after the basics are in place.
