A practical rhythm for desk work is three layers of breaks: a few seconds of eye rest every 20 minutes or so, a minute or two of standing and moving every 30 to 60 minutes, and a proper 10 to 15 minute break away from the screen every couple of hours, plus a real lunch. No single schedule is proven best for everyone. What matters most is that you never stay locked in one position, staring at one distance, for hours at a stretch. Short, frequent breaks tend to be easier to keep up than long, rare ones.

## The three kinds of breaks you need

Different parts of your body need different kinds of rest, and they don't all run on the same clock.

| Break type | How often | How long | What it's for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Eye break | About every 20 minutes | 20 seconds or more | Relaxing the focusing muscles in your eyes |
| Movement micro-break | Every 30 to 60 minutes | 1 to 3 minutes | Changing posture, getting blood moving |
| Real break | Every 90 to 120 minutes | 10 to 15 minutes | Mental reset and a longer stretch of movement |
| Meal break | Once or twice a day | 30 minutes or more, if you can | Food, daylight, a full step away from work |

You don't have to follow this rigidly. Treat it as a default to adjust.

## Eye breaks: the 20-20-20 habit

When you look at a screen, your eyes stay focused at a fixed, fairly close distance. Many people also blink less while concentrating on a screen, which can leave eyes feeling dry or tired.

A widely used habit is the **20-20-20 rule**: every 20 minutes, look at something about 20 feet away for about 20 seconds. Out a window is ideal. Across the room works if that's all you have.

While you're at it:

- Blink fully a few times.
- Check that your screen isn't much brighter than the room around it.
- Notice if you've been leaning toward the screen, which can be a sign the text is too small.

An eye break doesn't require leaving your chair, which makes it the easiest one to keep up.

## Movement micro-breaks

Sitting or standing still for long periods tends to leave people stiff. Your posture also tends to slide over time: shoulders creep forward, your head drifts toward the screen, your lower back loses its support.

Every 30 to 60 minutes, stand up and move for a minute or two. Ideas that fit into a workday:

- Refill your water glass or make a drink.
- Walk to another room and back.
- Stand up and roll your shoulders, then gently turn your head side to side.
- Reach both arms overhead, then let them hang and shake out your hands.
- If you have a sit-stand desk, change position.
- Take the next phone call standing or walking.

These breaks are short enough that they don't really interrupt your concentration. Many people find they come back with a clearer head.

## Longer breaks

Focus also fades. After a long stretch of concentrated work, most people notice they are rereading the same sentence or making small mistakes. A longer break every hour and a half to two hours helps.

Make it a real break:

- Step away from your desk and screen. Scrolling on your phone doesn't rest your eyes or your posture.
- Go outside if you can, even briefly. Daylight and a change of scene help many people reset.
- Do something physical: a short walk, some stairs, a few stretches, a household task.
- Have a snack or drink.

And take a proper lunch. Eating at your desk while answering email is an easy habit to fall into when you work from home, and it removes the one natural long break in the day.

## Popular break schedules

If you want a structured system, here are a few common approaches. None is proven superior; pick what fits your work.

### Work intervals of about 25 minutes

Work for 25 minutes, take a 5-minute break, and after four rounds take a longer break of 15 to 30 minutes. This interval approach is often called the Pomodoro technique. It works well for tasks you tend to put off and for people who like clear structure. It can feel too choppy for deep, creative work.

### Hour-based blocks

Work for about 50 minutes, then take 10. This fits neatly with meetings that start on the hour, and it's easy to remember.

### Longer focus blocks

Work for 75 to 90 minutes, then take 15 to 20. Good for deep work like writing, coding, or analysis. Pair it with eye breaks and a quick stand-up every half hour so you're not static for the entire block.

### Task-based breaks

Take a break whenever you finish a task or reach a natural stopping point. Flexible and natural, but easy to forget if a task drags on. A backstop timer helps.

## How to actually remember to take breaks

Knowing you should take breaks and actually taking them are different things, especially at home where no one around you gets up for coffee.

- **Use a timer or break reminder app.** A repeating reminder is the simplest tool that works.
- **Drink water.** A glass of water at your desk creates natural breaks, both for refills and trips to the bathroom.
- **Put your phone or printer across the room.** Small inconveniences add movement.
- **Stand for calls.** Link a recurring event with a posture change.
- **Schedule meetings to end early.** Ending 25 or 50 minute meetings instead of 30 or 60 leaves time to stand up between calls.
- **Block lunch in your calendar.** If it's on the calendar, it's less likely to be booked over.

## Signs you need more frequent breaks

Your body usually tells you before you notice the clock:

- Eyes feel dry, tired, or blurry by midafternoon.
- Neck or shoulders feel tight at the end of the day.
- Lower back stiffness when you stand up.
- Restless legs or the urge to fidget.
- Rereading the same line or losing your train of thought.
- Headaches toward the end of the workday.

If you notice these regularly, shorten the gap between breaks and check your setup too. A screen that is too low, a chair that doesn't support your back, or a keyboard that's too high can make you feel worse faster, and breaks alone won't fully fix them.

## Breaks don't replace a good setup

Breaks and ergonomics work together. A few setup basics to check:

- Top of the monitor at or slightly below eye level, about an arm's length away.
- Elbows at about 90 degrees with the keyboard at elbow height.
- Feet flat on the floor or a footrest.
- Lower back supported. A rolled towel works if your chair lacks lumbar support.

If you want an easy way to make position changes part of your routine, a sit-stand desk or [[desk-converters|a desk converter that sits on your existing desk]] lets you switch between sitting and standing in seconds. It isn't necessary, though. Standing up and walking around the room does most of the same job for free.

## Common mistakes

- **Taking breaks on your phone.** Your eyes stay close-focused and your neck stays bent.
- **Skipping breaks when busy.** Busy days are when you most need them.
- **Only taking one long break.** A single lunch break doesn't offset four hours of stillness on either side.
- **Working through lunch at the desk.**
- **Waiting for discomfort.** By the time you notice stiffness, you've usually been still too long.

## When to get help

If you have eye strain, headaches, or neck, shoulder, back, or wrist pain that persists despite regular breaks and a good setup, or any numbness or tingling, see a doctor, optometrist, or physiotherapist. Frequent breaks are a good habit, not a treatment.

## Quick checklist

1. Every 20 minutes: look at something far away for 20 seconds.
2. Every 30 to 60 minutes: stand up and move for 1 to 3 minutes.
3. Every 90 to 120 minutes: a 10 to 15 minute break away from screens.
4. A real lunch away from your desk.
5. Use timers, water, and standing calls to make breaks automatic.
