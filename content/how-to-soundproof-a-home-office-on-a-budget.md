Real soundproofing, the kind that stops a neighbor's TV completely, means adding heavy mass and decoupled walls, and that's expensive construction work. On a budget, you can still make a home office noticeably quieter by doing three things in order: seal the air gaps (especially around the door), add soft, heavy materials to the room, and handle the rest with a good headset. That combination fixes most complaints about echo on calls and a big share of household noise, for far less than renovation.

## First, know which problem you have

"Soundproofing" gets used for two different problems, and the fixes are different.

| Problem | What it sounds like | What helps |
|---|---|---|
| **Noise coming in** (or going out) | Kids, TV, traffic, neighbors, voices in the hall | Sealing gaps, mass, heavy door fixes |
| **Echo inside the room** | Your voice sounds hollow on calls, room feels "boomy" | Soft furnishings, rugs, curtains, panels |

Acoustic foam panels are the classic budget buy, but they mainly reduce echo. They do very little to stop noise coming through a wall. If your issue is the TV next door, foam on your walls won't solve it. If your issue is colleagues saying you sound like you're in a bathroom, foam or soft furnishings will.

Many home offices have both problems. Start with whichever bothers you more.

## Step 1: Seal the gaps (biggest impact per dollar)

Sound travels through air. Even a small gap lets through a surprising amount of noise. The door is almost always the weakest point.

### The door

- **Door bottom gap.** Most interior doors have a gap at the bottom. A door sweep or a draft stopper blocks it. Free version: roll up a thick towel or blanket and press it against the bottom of the door while you work.
- **Door edges.** Self-adhesive weatherstripping along the door frame seals the sides and top. It's cheap and easy to install. Close the door on a sheet of paper; if it slides out easily, there's a gap.
- **Hollow-core doors.** Many interior doors are hollow and let a lot of sound through. Hanging a heavy blanket or a thick curtain over the door adds mass. Replacing it with a solid-core door is the bigger upgrade if you own the home.

### Windows

- Weatherstrip or caulk gaps around the frame.
- Close windows during calls. Obvious, but open windows let in more noise than almost anything else.
- Heavy, lined curtains that cover the whole window and extend past the edges help a little with outside noise and a lot with echo.

### Outlets and other holes

Gaps around outlets, pipes, and vents on shared walls can leak sound. Foam gaskets behind outlet covers are inexpensive. Don't block ventilation that's needed for heating, cooling, or air quality.

## Step 2: Add soft, heavy stuff to the room

Hard surfaces reflect sound. Empty rooms with bare floors and walls echo. You can fix much of this with things you already own.

- **Rug on the floor.** A thick rug with a pad underneath reduces echo and footstep noise. Bigger is better.
- **Bookshelf on the shared wall.** A full bookcase with books of different depths adds mass and breaks up reflections.
- **Curtains.** Hang them over windows, and even on a bare wall if the echo is bad.
- **Upholstered furniture.** A fabric armchair or small couch absorbs sound.
- **Blankets and quilts.** Hanging a thick quilt on the wall behind or beside you is a cheap echo fix for calls.
- **Closet trick.** Recording or taking calls facing an open closet full of clothes can sound surprisingly good.

### Where echo matters most for calls

Your microphone picks up reflections from the surfaces nearest you. Focus soft materials:

1. Behind your monitor, on the wall you face
2. On the wall behind you
3. On the side walls near your desk
4. On the floor under and around your desk

## Step 3: Consider acoustic panels (for echo only)

If you've added soft furnishings and still hear echo, acoustic panels are the next step. Tips for budget buyers:

- **Thicker panels absorb a wider range of sound.** Very thin foam tiles mostly affect high frequencies.
- **You don't need to cover every wall.** A few panels at the reflection points listed above often do most of the work.
- **Removable mounting** (command strips or hanging hooks) is kinder to walls if you rent.
- **Check fire-safety information** for any foam product you put on walls.

Remember: panels treat echo, not noise from outside the room.

## Step 4: Handle what's left with a headset

After sealing and softening, some noise will remain. A good headset handles it from both directions:

- **Noise-canceling headphones** reduce steady background noise like fans, traffic, and appliances for you.
- **A directional or boom microphone** picks up your voice and rejects more of the room, so others don't hear the dog or the dishwasher.

For many people working from home, this is the single most effective purchase for calls, and it works in any room. If you're shopping, [[headsets|compare headsets with noise-canceling mics]] and look for a boom mic if calls are a big part of your day.

Most video call apps also have noise suppression settings. Turn them on and try different levels.

## Cheap fixes ranked by impact

| Fix | Cost | Helps with |
|---|---|---|
| Close door and windows, towel at door bottom | Free | Incoming noise |
| Turn on call-app noise suppression | Free | Others hearing your background |
| Move desk away from the shared wall or noisy window | Free | Incoming noise |
| Rug, curtains, blankets, bookshelf | Free to low | Echo, some incoming noise |
| Weatherstripping and door sweep | Low | Incoming noise |
| Headset with a good mic | Low to moderate | Calls in both directions |
| Heavy curtain over the door | Low to moderate | Incoming noise |
| Acoustic panels | Moderate | Echo only |
| Solid-core door | Higher | Incoming noise |

## Move the desk, not just the noise

Where your desk sits matters.

- Avoid the wall you share with a neighbor, the kitchen, or a TV room.
- Keep away from windows facing the street if traffic is the problem.
- Face your mic toward a soft surface, not a bare wall.
- If you can choose the room, pick the one farthest from household activity and with the fewest shared walls.

## Common budget mistakes

- **Buying thin foam to block a neighbor's noise.** It treats echo, not transmission.
- **Ignoring the door.** A great wall treatment is undone by a gap under the door.
- **Covering walls with egg cartons.** They do very little and can be a fire risk.
- **Blocking vents.** Sealing air gaps is good; sealing required ventilation is not.
- **Skipping the headset.** It's often the cheapest way to fix call quality.

## Renting? What's safe

Most budget steps are renter-friendly: rugs, curtains on tension rods, blankets, bookshelves, door draft stoppers, removable weatherstripping, and panels on removable strips. Check your lease before drilling, and keep packaging for anything you might remove when you move out.

## Bottom line

On a budget, you can't make a room truly soundproof, but you can make it quiet enough to work and take calls. Seal the door first, soften the room with rugs, curtains and bookshelves, move your desk away from noisy walls, and use a headset with a good mic to handle the rest. Save acoustic panels for echo you can't fix with furnishings.
