Setting up a home office in a bedroom comes down to three things: choosing a spot where the desk gets decent light and doesn't face the bed, fitting furniture that suits the room instead of shrinking your ergonomics, and creating a clear boundary so the room still feels like a place to sleep. You can do most of it with what you already own. The plan below walks through each step, from measuring to packing work away at night.

## Step 1: Measure before you move anything

Bedrooms have less spare floor space than you think once doors, closets, and drawers need room to open. Before choosing a desk, measure:

- **Wall length** available for the desk, avoiding radiators, vents, and outlets you need to reach.
- **Clearance behind the chair.** You need roughly 3 feet (about 90 cm) between the desk edge and the nearest obstacle to pull a chair out and sit comfortably. Less than that and you'll bump the bed or a dresser every time you stand up.
- **Door and closet swing.** Make sure your chair won't block a door when you're seated.
- **Outlet locations.** Placing the desk near an outlet avoids extension cords across the floor.

Sketch the room on paper with these numbers. It takes ten minutes and saves you from buying a desk that doesn't fit.

## Step 2: Choose the right spot

Where the desk goes affects your light, your focus, and your sleep. Common options, from usually best to usually worst:

| Spot | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| Beside a window (window to your side) | Good daylight, no screen glare | May need blinds for afternoon sun |
| Against a wall away from the bed | Clear separation, easy cable routing | Can feel boxed in; needs a good lamp |
| In a closet or alcove | Hides work completely at night | Tight fit, poor airflow, dim |
| Facing a window | Nice view | Bright light behind the screen strains eyes |
| At the foot of the bed, facing it | Uses dead space | Bed stays in view; hard to switch off |

A few rules of thumb:

- **Don't face the bed.** Seeing your bed all day makes it harder to focus, and seeing your desk from bed makes it harder to switch off. If possible, put the desk where your back is to the bed, or out of the line of sight from your pillow.
- **Keep windows to the side.** A window directly behind your monitor causes glare in your eyes; a window behind you reflects on the screen.
- **Think about video calls.** Check what the camera will see behind you. A plain wall or a bookshelf is better than an unmade bed.

## Step 3: Pick a desk that fits the room, not a tiny one that fits nothing

The temptation in a bedroom is to buy the smallest desk possible. That often backfires, because a desk that's too shallow puts the monitor too close and leaves no room for a keyboard and mouse.

Minimum sizes worth aiming for:

- **Depth:** at least 24 inches (about 60 cm) if you use an external monitor. A laptop-only setup can get by with 20 inches, but deeper is more comfortable.
- **Width:** at least 40 inches (about 100 cm) for a monitor, keyboard, mouse, and a bit of writing space.
- **Height:** the standard is around 28 to 30 inches (71 to 76 cm). What matters is that your elbows sit at roughly 90 degrees with your forearms level when typing.

Space-saving options:

- **Wall-mounted or fold-down desks** free up the floor completely but are usually shallow and fixed in height.
- **Corner desks** use awkward space well.
- **A dresser with a clear top** can double as a standing workstation if its height happens to match your elbow height when standing.
- **Height-adjustable desks** are worth considering if the bedroom is your only workspace and you'll be there all day. Changing position helps when you have fewer excuses to leave the room. ## Step 4: Get the ergonomics right

A bedroom office is still an office. The same posture basics apply:

- **Chair:** feet flat on the floor, thighs roughly parallel to the floor, lower back supported. If your chair lacks lumbar support, a rolled towel or small cushion at the small of your back is a good free fix.
- **Screen:** top of the screen at or slightly below eye level, about an arm's length away.
- **Keyboard and mouse:** close to the front edge of the desk, at a height where your shoulders stay relaxed.
- **Laptop users:** raise the laptop on books or a box so the screen is at eye height, and use a separate keyboard and mouse. Working from the bed with a laptop on your lap is the fastest way to a sore neck.

Avoid using the bed as your chair. It seems convenient in a small room, but there's no back support, the height is wrong, and it blurs the line between rest and work even more.

If your chair is too low for the desk, raise the chair and use a box or stack of books as a footrest rather than buying a new desk.

## Step 5: Light it properly

Bedroom lighting is usually designed for relaxing: warm, dim, and often a single ceiling fixture. That's not enough for eight hours of screen work.

- Add a **desk lamp** placed to the side of your work area, angled at the desk rather than your eyes.
- Use **neutral white light** (around 4000K) during the day and switch to warmer light in the evening.
- Keep some **ambient light** on so your screen isn't the brightest thing in the room.

A desk lamp with a warm setting can also serve as a bedside-style light in the evening, so one purchase does two jobs.

## Step 6: Control cables and clutter

Clutter in a bedroom follows you to bed. Keep the work zone tidy:

- Run cables along the back edge of the desk and bundle them with velcro ties or twist ties.
- Use a single power strip mounted under or behind the desk.
- Give work papers and gear one home: a drawer, a box, or a shelf. If it fits in a box, it can leave the desk at night.
- Mount the monitor on an arm if desk depth is tight; it frees the surface underneath.

## Step 7: Build a boundary between work and sleep

This is what makes a bedroom office livable long term. Some options:

- **Visual screening.** A folding room divider, a curtain on a ceiling track, or a tall bookshelf placed perpendicular to the wall can hide the desk from the bed.
- **Shutdown routine.** At the end of the day, close the laptop, turn off the monitor, and put work items away. Some people drape a cloth over the monitor or close a cabinet door.
- **Different lighting.** Use cool, bright task light while working and switch to warm, dim light once work is done.
- **No work in bed.** Keep email and laptops off the bed so it stays associated with sleep.
- **Keep devices out of reach.** If you tend to check messages at night, charge your work phone at the desk rather than the nightstand.

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing a desk that's too shallow**, which forces the screen too close and cramps your arms.
- **Placing the desk facing the bed**, which makes it harder to focus and harder to relax.
- **Relying on the ceiling light** alone and ending up with eye strain.
- **Using a dining chair or the bed** instead of a supportive chair.
- **Leaving work visible at night**, which keeps your brain in work mode.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Measured wall space, chair clearance, and outlets
- [ ] Desk beside a window, not facing the bed
- [ ] Desk at least 24 inches deep for a monitor
- [ ] Screen at eye level, about an arm's length away
- [ ] Supportive chair with feet flat (footrest if needed)
- [ ] Desk lamp with neutral daytime light
- [ ] Cables bundled, one home for work items
- [ ] Divider, curtain, or shutdown routine to separate work from sleep

A bedroom office works well when it's planned rather than squeezed in. Measure first, protect your posture, and give yourself a clear way to "leave work" at the end of the day, even if you only walk three steps to the other side of the room.
