Negative tilt means the front edge of the keyboard, nearest to you, is higher than the back edge, so the keys slope away from you. It's the opposite of what most keyboards do when you flip out their rear feet. The point is to keep your wrists straight rather than bent upward when your hands are at or below elbow height. It tends to help people who type with the keyboard low, such as on a keyboard tray or at a standing desk, and people whose wrists bend up while typing. It helps less, or not at all, if your keyboard is already too high.

## Three kinds of keyboard tilt

Keyboard tilt describes the front-to-back angle of the keys.

- **Positive tilt:** the back edge is raised, so the keys slope toward you. This is what happens when you extend the little feet under most keyboards.
- **Zero tilt (flat):** the keyboard lies flat on the desk.
- **Negative tilt:** the front edge is raised, or the keyboard sits on a surface angled away from you, so the keys slope away.

Positive tilt became the default largely out of habit, possibly from typewriters, where keys were stepped in rows. For many typists, though, it pushes the wrists into extension (bending the hand up toward the back of the forearm).

## Why negative tilt can help

Think about the path from your elbow to your fingertips. If your elbows are at about 90 degrees and your keyboard sits slightly below elbow height, your forearms slope gently downward toward the keys. With a positive-tilt keyboard, the keys slope up toward you, so your hands have to bend back to reach them. A negative-tilt keyboard slopes the same direction as your forearms, which lets your wrists stay in a straighter, more neutral line.

That's the basic idea:

- **Keeping wrists straight** rather than bent up may reduce strain on the tendons that run through the wrist.
- **Less extension** can mean less pressure in the carpal tunnel area, the passage on the palm side of the wrist.
- **Hands can hover naturally** over the keys, with fingers curling down rather than reaching up.

Negative tilt isn't a cure for wrist problems, and it doesn't suit everyone. It's one tool for getting wrists into a neutral position.

## Who it helps most

Negative tilt tends to make sense for:

- **People using a keyboard tray** below desk height, where forearms slope down to the keys.
- **Standing desk users** who set the keyboard slightly below elbow height.
- **Anyone who notices their wrists bent back** while typing, especially if they anchor their palms and lift their fingers.
- **People who type with the keyboard in their lap** or on a low surface while reclining in a chair.
- **Typists who want to try hovering** rather than resting their palms.

## Who may not benefit

Negative tilt isn't a good match for:

- **Setups where the keyboard is already above elbow height.** If the desk is too high, raising the front of the keyboard makes things worse by lifting your hands further. Fix the height first.
- **Very thick keyboards without a palm rest.** Raising the front of a tall keyboard can put the keys even higher relative to your hands.
- **People who prefer resting palms on the desk** while typing. With negative tilt, the front edge rises, and you'll likely need a palm rest at the right height.
- **Some laptop users.** Tilting a laptop negatively makes the screen tip away, which isn't practical. Use an external keyboard instead.

## How much tilt is typical?

There's no single perfect angle. Keyboard trays with negative tilt adjustment often allow somewhere around 0 to 15 degrees. Many people find a small amount, just a few degrees to around 10, feels most natural. Start with a slight angle and increase only if your wrists still bend up.

## How to check if you need it

Try this quick test:

1. **Sit (or stand) at your normal typing position** with shoulders relaxed and elbows at about 90 to 110 degrees.
2. **Put your hands on the home row** as you normally type.
3. **Look at your wrists from the side,** or have someone take a photo.
4. **If the back of your hand angles up from your forearm,** your wrists are in extension. Negative tilt, a lower keyboard, or a flatter keyboard could help.
5. **If your wrist is already straight,** you probably don't need negative tilt.

## How to get negative tilt

### Free options first

- **Fold in the rear keyboard feet.** This alone takes you from positive to flat, which often solves most of the problem.
- **Wedge the front edge.** Put a thin, firm object under the front of the keyboard, like a folded towel, a thin book, or a strip of cardboard, to raise it a bit. Make sure it's stable.
- **Lower the keyboard.** A lower keyboard surface naturally changes the angle of your forearms, and flat keys may then feel right.

### Keyboard trays

An under-desk keyboard tray with adjustable tilt is the most common way to get true negative tilt. Look for:

- Height adjustment, so you can put the tray at or slightly below elbow level
- Tilt adjustment that includes negative angles
- A tray wide enough for the keyboard and mouse at the same height
- Solid, wobble-free mounting

### Keyboards with front feet or built-in tilt

Some ergonomic keyboards include feet or risers at the front edge, or a built-in palm rest that sets a slight negative slope. When browsing [[external-keyboards|ergonomic keyboards]], look for descriptions mentioning negative tilt, front risers, or adjustable tenting and tilt.

### Palm rests

With negative tilt, a palm rest at the front edge helps during pauses. It should support the fleshy base of your palm, not the underside of your wrist, and match the height of the front of the keyboard.

## Negative tilt vs. tenting and split keyboards

These terms often show up together, but they fix different problems.

| Adjustment | What it does | Main problem it addresses |
|---|---|---|
| Negative tilt | Front edge higher than the back | Wrists bending upward (extension) |
| Tenting | Raises the middle so each half slopes sideways | Forearms twisting palms-down (pronation) |
| Split layout | Separates the halves | Wrists bending outward toward the pinky (ulnar deviation) |

A keyboard can combine all three. If you're addressing one issue, start with the simplest adjustment and add others only if needed.

## Getting used to it

A new angle can feel strange for a few days. To ease in:

- **Start with a small angle** and increase gradually.
- **Practice hovering.** Negative tilt works best when you let your hands float while typing and rest only during pauses.
- **Keep the mouse at the same height** as the keyboard. A tray with room for the mouse helps.
- **Pay attention to your shoulders.** They should stay relaxed, not lifted.

## When to see a professional

This is general information, not medical advice. If you have wrist pain that doesn't improve after adjusting your setup, or numbness, tingling, or weakness in your hands, see a doctor or physiotherapist. Those symptoms deserve a proper assessment.

## Quick checklist

- Check your wrist angle from the side while typing
- Fold in the rear keyboard feet first
- Keyboard at or slightly below elbow height
- Try a slight negative tilt (a few degrees) if wrists still bend up
- Use a palm rest only during pauses
- Keep the mouse at the same height as the keyboard

## Bottom line

A negative-tilt keyboard slopes away from you so your wrists can stay straight when your hands are at or below elbow height. It helps most with keyboard trays, standing desks, and anyone whose wrists bend upward while typing. Start by folding in the keyboard's rear feet and lowering it if needed; add true negative tilt with a wedge, an adjustable tray, or an ergonomic keyboard if your wrists still bend back.
