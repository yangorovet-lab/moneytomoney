You need a wrist rest only in specific situations: when your keyboard is tall enough that your wrists bend upward to reach it, or when you tend to rest your wrists on a hard desk edge between bursts of typing. The important catch is that a wrist rest is for resting during pauses, not for planting your wrists on while you type or mouse. Used that way, it can reduce pressure and awkward bending. Used as an anchor while typing, it can make things worse.

## What a wrist rest is supposed to do

A wrist rest is a padded strip placed in front of a keyboard, or a small cushion in front of or built into a mouse pad. Its jobs are to:

- **Level the height** between the desk and the top of a thick keyboard, so your hands don't have to climb up to the keys.
- **Soften contact** when you pause, so you're not resting the underside of your wrists on a hard desk edge.
- **Remind you to keep wrists straight**, rather than bending them up or down.

The word "rest" is literal. During active typing, your hands are meant to float slightly above it, moving from the shoulder and elbow rather than pivoting at the wrist.

## Why typing on a wrist rest can backfire

When you anchor your wrists and type, two things tend to happen:

1. **Your wrists bend.** To reach keys far from the resting point, you flex your wrists upward or sideways instead of moving your whole hand.
2. **Pressure builds on the underside of the wrist.** The carpal tunnel runs through that area. Pressing it into a surface for long periods is generally best avoided.

The same applies to a mouse. Planting your wrist and moving the mouse only by flicking your wrist side to side keeps the joint bent and working hard. Moving from the elbow, with the forearm doing more of the work, is usually easier on the wrist.

## Who benefits from a wrist rest

A wrist rest is likely useful if:

- **Your keyboard is tall.** Many mechanical keyboards and some full-size keyboards sit high at the front. Without a rest, your wrists bend upward to reach the keys.
- **You rest your wrists on the desk edge** between typing bursts, especially if the edge is hard or sharp.
- **You use a keyboard tray** where your wrists would otherwise hang or rest on a hard lip.
- **Your mouse is tall or thick** and your wrist dips down to the desk while using it.
- **You pause often**, such as reading, thinking, or on calls, with hands at the keyboard.

## Who probably doesn't need one

You can likely skip it if:

- **Your keyboard is low-profile**, like most laptop-style or slim keyboards.
- **You type with hands floating** and don't rest your wrists anyway.
- **Your forearms are supported** by armrests or the desk surface, and your wrists stay straight.
- **You use a vertical or ergonomic mouse** that already keeps your hand in a neutral position.

## Keyboard vs mouse wrist rests

| | Keyboard wrist rest | Mouse wrist rest |
|---|---|---|
| Main purpose | Match keyboard height, cushion pauses | Keep wrist from dipping or bending |
| Most useful with | Tall or mechanical keyboards | Tall mice, long mouse sessions |
| Common shape | Long strip, same width as the keyboard | Small pad or built into a mouse pad |
| Main risk | Anchoring wrists while typing | Pivoting at the wrist instead of the arm |
| Free alternative | Rolled towel, folded cloth | Folded cloth, small book as a spacer |

## Try free fixes first

Before buying a wrist rest, check your overall setup, because many wrist problems come from height and position rather than a missing pad.

- **Lower the keyboard feet.** Many keyboards have flip-out legs at the back. Tilting the keyboard back often causes wrists to bend upward. Keeping it flat, or even slightly tilted away from you, keeps wrists straighter.
- **Adjust chair height.** If your elbows are below the desk, your wrists bend up to reach the keys. Raise the chair until your forearms are roughly level, and use a footrest if your feet no longer reach the floor.
- **Move the keyboard closer.** Reaching forward encourages you to plant your wrists.
- **Keep the mouse next to the keyboard**, not off to one side.
- **Try a rolled towel or folded cloth** in front of the keyboard for a few days. If it helps, a proper wrist rest will likely help too.

## What to look for in a wrist rest

If you decide to buy one:

- **Height that matches your keyboard.** The top of the rest should be level with or slightly below the front edge of the keyboard. A rest that's higher than the keys pushes your wrists into a downward bend.
- **Firm, supportive padding.** Very soft gel or foam lets your wrists sink and can create more pressure in one spot. Medium-firm is usually more comfortable over time.
- **Length that matches the keyboard.** A rest that's much shorter leaves one hand unsupported.
- **A wide enough surface** to support the heel of your palm rather than the narrow underside of the wrist.
- **Non-slip base** so it doesn't slide around.
- **Easy-to-clean surface.** Fabric covers can get grimy; a wipeable surface stays fresh.

## How to use a wrist rest correctly

1. **Rest the heel of your palm**, not the underside of your wrist, on the pad. The heel of the hand is the fleshy area just above the wrist crease.
2. **Lift your hands slightly while typing.** Let your hands hover and move from the elbow and shoulder to reach keys.
3. **Rest only during pauses.** When you stop to read or think, let your palms settle on the rest.
4. **Keep wrists straight**, not bent up, down, or to the side.
5. **For the mouse, move from the elbow.** Rest the heel of your hand only when you're not actively moving the mouse, or keep contact light.

It takes a few days to break the habit of anchoring. A useful check is to watch your wrists while typing: if they stay stuck to the pad while your fingers stretch to reach distant keys, you're anchoring.

## When the real issue is elsewhere

If your wrists hurt even with a wrist rest, other factors may be involved:

- **Desk too high**, forcing wrists to bend.
- **Keyboard too wide**, pushing the mouse far out to the side. A compact keyboard without a number pad brings the mouse closer.
- **Mouse shape** that twists your forearm palm-down. Some people find [[vertical-mice|a vertical mouse]] more comfortable because it keeps the forearm in a handshake position.
- **Keyboard design** that forces your wrists outward. A split or tented design can help some people, and it's worth reading up on [[external-keyboards|ergonomic keyboards]] if a standard layout feels cramped.
- **Long stretches without breaks.** Short pauses to stretch your hands and change position help more than any accessory.

## When to see a doctor

Wrist rests are a comfort tool, not a treatment. See a doctor or physiotherapist if you have:

- Numbness or tingling in your fingers, especially at night.
- Weakness in your grip or dropping things.
- Pain that lasts more than a couple of weeks or keeps getting worse.
- Swelling, or pain that spreads up the arm.

## Bottom line

- **Tall keyboard or hard desk edge:** a wrist rest is worth it.
- **Low-profile keyboard, floating hands:** you can probably skip it.
- **If you use one:** rest the heel of your palm on it during pauses, keep wrists straight, and float your hands while typing.
- **Fix the basics first:** chair and desk height, keyboard tilt, and mouse position often matter more than any pad.
