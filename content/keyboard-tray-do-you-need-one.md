You need a keyboard tray if your desk is too high for you to type with relaxed shoulders and your elbows at roughly 90 degrees, and you can't fix that by raising your chair. That's common for shorter people at standard 29 to 30 inch desks. If your desk is already at elbow height, or you have a height-adjustable desk, a tray usually adds little and can even get in the way. The quickest way to decide is a two-minute measurement.

## The two-minute test

1. Sit in your chair with your feet flat on the floor and your back against the backrest.
2. Let your arms hang by your sides, then bend your elbows to about 90 degrees with forearms roughly level.
3. Measure from the floor to the underside of your forearm, just below the elbow. That's your ideal keyboard height (the home row of the keys should be at about this height).
4. Measure from the floor to the top of your desk.

**Compare the two numbers:**

| Desk vs elbow height | What it means | Best fix |
|---|---|---|
| Desk within about an inch of elbow height | Good as is | No tray needed |
| Desk 1 to 3 inches too high, feet still reach floor when chair is raised | Chair can compensate | Raise the chair; add a footrest if needed |
| Desk more than 2 to 3 inches too high, raising chair lifts feet off the floor or hits the desk | Classic tray case | Keyboard tray, or a lower or adjustable desk |
| Desk too low | Tray won't help | Raise the desk with risers, or use a different desk |

Remember that a keyboard itself adds height. A thick keyboard on top of a desk that's already at elbow height puts the keys above your elbows.

## Why desk height matters for typing

When the keyboard is higher than your elbows, you compensate in one of a few ways:

- **Shrugging your shoulders** to lift your arms, which builds tension in the neck and upper back over a day.
- **Bending your wrists upward**, which puts the wrists in extension for hours.
- **Reaching forward** and leaning away from the chair's backrest.

A keyboard tray drops the keyboard and mouse below the desk surface, letting your arms hang naturally while you type.

## Who benefits most from a keyboard tray

- **Shorter people at fixed-height desks.** Standard desk heights suit people of roughly average height or taller. Shorter people often need the keyboard a few inches lower.
- **People using a desk that doubles as a dining table or a thick-topped desk.**
- **Anyone whose chair can't go high enough** without their thighs hitting the desk or their feet dangling.
- **People who want negative tilt.** Some trays let you angle the keyboard slightly away from you, which can keep wrists straighter than a keyboard flat or tilted up on its feet.
- **People with limited desk depth.** A tray can pull the keyboard closer, freeing the desk for a monitor farther back.

## Who probably doesn't need one

- **People with a height-adjustable standing desk.** Set the desk to elbow height and you're done.
- **Taller people.** Standard desks are often close to right or even too low.
- **People whose chairs rise enough** while their feet stay flat or on a footrest.
- **People who type on a laptop keyboard all day.** You'd be better off with an external keyboard and a raised laptop first.

## Try the cheaper alternatives first

Before drilling into your desk, see if these solve it:

### Raise your chair and support your feet

This is the most common fix. Raise the seat until your elbows are level with the desk surface, then put something under your feet so they rest flat. A box, a stack of books or a sturdy step works. A [[footrests|height-adjustable footrest]] is a neater, longer-term option.

Watch for one thing: your thighs need clearance under the desk. If raising the chair jams your legs against the drawer or apron, a tray (or a different desk) is the better answer.

### Use a thinner keyboard

A low-profile keyboard can lower your hands by half an inch or more compared with a tall one. Fold in the keyboard's rear feet too; they tilt the keys upward, which bends your wrists back. A slim, compact [[external-keyboards|low-profile external keyboard]] also lets you keep the mouse closer.

### Sit closer and use armrests correctly

Pull your chair in so your elbows sit close to your sides, and lower the armrests so they don't push your shoulders up.

## What to look for in a keyboard tray

If a tray is the right answer, the details matter.

- **Height adjustment:** you should be able to set it a few inches below the desk surface. Some also go slightly above.
- **Tilt adjustment:** a mechanism that allows flat or slightly negative tilt is useful.
- **Room for the mouse:** a tray that fits only the keyboard forces the mouse onto the desk, higher and farther away. Look for a platform wide enough for both, or a mouse platform that attaches at the same height.
- **Stability:** a bouncy tray is irritating to type on. Sturdy rails and a solid mechanism help.
- **Mounting fits your desk:** check the depth and underside of your desk. Rail-mounted trays need enough flat space under the desk; clamp-on trays need a suitable edge. Glass desks and desks with drawers can be a problem.
- **Knee and thigh clearance:** the tray and its mechanism sit under the desk. Make sure your legs still fit when it's lowered.
- **Stowing:** many trays slide under the desk when not in use, freeing up space.

## Common mistakes with keyboard trays

- **Setting the tray too far below the desk.** You want elbow height, not lap height. Too low and your wrists bend down or you hunch forward.
- **Leaving the mouse on the desk.** Mousing a few inches higher than typing, off to the side, causes shoulder strain.
- **Too little depth.** If your wrists hang off the front edge of a shallow tray, you'll end up resting them on a hard lip.
- **Ignoring the monitor.** With the keyboard lower and pulled closer, recheck that the top of your screen is still at or slightly below eye level and about an arm's length away.
- **Installing a tray under a desk that's also too low.** A tray can only lower things.

## A tray vs an adjustable desk

If your desk is too high and you're thinking of replacing it anyway, a height-adjustable desk solves the problem more completely. It lets you set exact typing height, change it for different tasks, and alternate between sitting and standing. A tray is the better choice when you're keeping your current desk, need a smaller budget fix, or rent and can't replace furniture.

## If you have pain already

Shoulder, neck, or wrist discomfort from typing often improves once keyboard height is right. If your pain persists or you notice numbness, tingling or weakness in your hands or arms, see a doctor or physiotherapist rather than relying on equipment changes alone.

## Bottom line

- Measure elbow height and desk height before buying anything.
- If the gap is small, raise your chair and add a footrest.
- If the desk is more than a couple of inches too high and your legs or feet can't handle a higher chair, a keyboard tray is a good fix.
- Choose a tray with height and tilt adjustment and space for your mouse.
- If you have a height-adjustable desk, you almost certainly don't need a tray.
