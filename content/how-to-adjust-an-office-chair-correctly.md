Adjust your office chair in this order: seat height first (feet flat, knees roughly level with or slightly below your hips), then seat depth (two to four fingers of space behind your knees), then lumbar support (in the small of your back), then recline and tension, and finally armrests (level with the desk, shoulders relaxed). Doing it in that sequence matters, because each setting changes how the next one feels. The whole process takes about ten minutes, and you only need to redo it if someone else uses the chair or you change desks.

## Before you start

Wear the shoes you usually wear at your desk, because a thick sole can change the right seat height by an inch or more. Then find the controls. If you are not sure what a lever does, pull it while sitting and see what moves.

Typical controls you might find:

| Control | Where it usually is | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Height lever | Right side, under the seat | Raises or lowers the seat |
| Seat slider | Under the front of the seat or right side | Moves the seat forward or back |
| Tilt tension knob | Center, under the seat | Makes reclining harder or easier |
| Tilt lock | Left side or combined with height lever | Locks the backrest angle |
| Lumbar adjustment | Back of the backrest or its sides | Moves the lumbar pad up, down, or deeper |
| Armrest buttons | On or under each armrest | Changes armrest height, width, or angle |

Your chair may have only some of these. The steps below include workarounds.

## Step 1: Set the seat height

Stand in front of the chair and raise or lower the seat until the highest point of the cushion is just below your kneecap. That is a good starting point. Now sit down and check:

- Both feet rest flat on the floor without pressure under the front of your thighs.
- Your knees are at about the same height as your hips or a little lower.
- Your thighs are roughly parallel to the floor, or angled slightly downward.

If you feel the edge of the seat pressing into the underside of your thighs, the seat is too high. If your knees point up above your hips, it is too low.

### When the chair and desk disagree

Here is the common problem: the right seat height for your legs leaves your elbows below the desk surface. If your desk has a fixed height, raise the chair until your elbows sit at about desk level, and then bring the floor up to meet your feet. A sturdy box, a stack of books, or a small step stool works. If you want something designed for it, [[footrests|look at adjustable footrests]] with a tilting platform.

If you have a height-adjustable desk, do the opposite: set the chair for your legs, then move the desk to match your elbows.

## Step 2: Set the seat depth

Sit all the way back so your lower back touches the backrest. Now slide two to four fingers between the front edge of the seat and the back of your knee.

- If you cannot fit two fingers, the seat is too deep. It will press behind your knees and nudge you to slouch forward to get comfortable.
- If you can fit a whole fist or more, the seat is too shallow for you. Your thighs are not supported enough.

Use the seat slider to fix it. No slider and a seat that is too deep? A firm cushion behind your back moves you forward.

## Step 3: Position the lumbar support

Your lower spine has a gentle inward curve just above your belt line. The lumbar support should fill that curve, not sit in the middle of your back or push against your tailbone.

To find the right spot:

1. Sit back fully in the chair.
2. Move the lumbar pad (or the whole backrest, on some chairs) up or down until the firmest part meets the curve of your lower back, usually around belt height or a bit above.
3. If depth is adjustable, add just enough that you feel supported without being pushed forward.

It should feel like light, even contact, not a bump shoving you forward.

**No adjustable lumbar?** Roll up a small hand towel to about the thickness of your wrist and place it horizontally at the curve of your lower back. This free fix works surprisingly well, and you can change its thickness until it feels right.

## Step 4: Set the recline and tilt tension

Sitting perfectly upright at 90 degrees is not the goal. A slight recline, somewhere around 100 to 110 degrees between your torso and thighs, lets the backrest carry some of your upper body weight.

1. Unlock the tilt.
2. Lean back. If the chair drops back too easily, tighten the tension knob. If you have to push hard to recline at all, loosen it.
3. Aim for a setting where you can lean back with gentle pressure and the chair brings you forward again when you sit up.

Locking the backrest at a slight recline or leaving it free are both reasonable. Shifting positions over the day is generally better than holding one fixed posture.

If your chair has a seat tilt that angles the seat forward, a small forward tilt can help if you tend to perch on the front edge. Leave it flat if you are not sure.

## Step 5: Adjust the armrests

Armrests set too high hike your shoulders toward your ears. Too low and you lean to one side to reach them.

With your shoulders relaxed and your elbows bent to about 90 degrees:

- Raise or lower the armrests until they just touch the underside of your forearms.
- Match them to the height of your desk surface or keyboard tray, so your forearms can move from armrest to desk without a step.
- Bring them in closer if they are wide enough that your elbows flare out.

If the armrests stop you from getting close to the desk, lower or remove them. Being close to the keyboard matters more than arm support.

## Step 6: Check the whole setup

Now sit and work for a few minutes. Look for these signs:

- Your feet are flat on the floor or a footrest.
- There is a small gap behind your knees.
- Your lower back is in contact with the backrest.
- Your shoulders are relaxed, not raised.
- Your elbows are close to your sides, bent at around 90 degrees.
- Your wrists are roughly straight while you type.
- Your eyes land near the top of the screen without tilting your head.

If the last point is off, that is a monitor issue. Fix the chair first, then move the screen to match. A stack of books is a perfectly good monitor riser.

## Common mistakes

**Setting the chair to the desk first.** People often raise the chair until their arms fit the desk and then ignore their dangling feet. Always check your feet after changing the height.

**Perching on the front edge.** If you find yourself sitting forward, the seat may be too deep, the backrest too far back, or the screen too far away. Fix the cause instead of fighting the habit.

**Cranking the lumbar to maximum.** More is not better. Too much lumbar support can be as uncomfortable as none.

**Never touching the tension knob.** Factory tension suits an average body weight. If you are lighter or heavier, adjust it.

**Sharing a chair without resetting it.** A small piece of tape on the gas cylinder marks your seat height for a quick reset.

## When your chair can't be adjusted enough

Basic task chairs and dining chairs may have only a height lever or nothing at all. You can still get close:

- **Height:** Use a firm cushion to raise the seat, and a footrest or box to support your feet.
- **Lumbar:** Use a rolled towel or small pillow.
- **Depth:** Put a cushion behind your back to shorten a deep seat.
- **Armrests:** Skip them. Keep your elbows close to your body and rest your forearms on the desk edge if it is not sharp.

If you have made these fixes and you are still uncomfortable after several weeks, it may be time to upgrade. When comparing [[office-chairs|office chairs with adjustable lumbar and seat depth]], prioritize seat depth adjustment, adjustable lumbar height, a height range that fits your legs, and armrests that go low enough to clear your desk.

## A note on pain

Adjusting your chair can make long work days more comfortable, but it is not a treatment. If you have persistent or severe back, neck, or hip pain, or any numbness or tingling in your legs or arms, get it checked by a doctor or physiotherapist.

## Quick checklist

1. Seat height: feet flat, knees level with or slightly below hips.
2. Seat depth: two to four fingers behind the knees.
3. Lumbar: firmest point at the curve of your lower back.
4. Recline: slight lean back, tension set so the chair supports you.
5. Armrests: just touching your forearms, level with the desk, shoulders relaxed.
6. Then adjust the desk, footrest, or monitor to fit the chair, not the other way around.
